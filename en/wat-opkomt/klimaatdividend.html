The climate dividend

How excess mortality in the 75+ age group generates just enough to address the climate problem structurally

Jacobus van Merksteijn

  • Author — Jacobus van Merksteijn
  • Date — 23 July 2026 · Palma, Mallorca
  • Section — What is emerging · Climate-demographic analysis
  • Basis — EuroMOMO excess mortality 2022–2026 · Dutch government Wlz report · Nature Medicine heat mortality · EU Ageing Report 2024
  • Sources — Santé publique France, EuroMOMO, Nature Medicine, Imperial College Grantham, Eurostat SILC, OECD Health at a Glance

The numbers add up. The heatwaves from 2022 to 2026 killed approximately 263,000 people in Europe, 190,000 of them in the 75+ age group. What governments and the surviving working population therefore no longer have to spend on pensions, long-term care and pharmaceuticals for this group amounts to €60–80 billion per year in the EU. Precisely enough to address structurally the climate problem that caused the excess mortality. A cycle that finances itself.

What the heatwaves cost in human lives

Between 2022 and 2026, climate-related heatwaves caused approximately 263,000 excess deaths cumulatively in the EU-27. Two-thirds of them — 190,000 — belonged to the 75+ age group. Southern Europe bears a disproportionate share: Italy 68,400 deaths, Spain 47,200, France 38,600, Greece 18,300, Portugal 12,800.

What the deceased represented in collective costs

The public cost per person aged 75 and over per year ranges from €21,180 in Greece to €66,786 in the Netherlands. Differences arise from pension levels, long-term-care systems and curative healthcare costs. For the EU as a whole: an average of €32,380 per person aged 75 and over per year.

Applied to the 190,000 deceased people aged 75 and over, adjusted for harvesting (40% would have died within six months anyway) and with a remaining life expectancy of ten years on average, the net deferred public expenditure over the five-year period 2022–2026 amounts to €33 billion. On an annual basis, structurally from 2030: €12–14 billion in avoided collective expenditure per year.

“Every deceased person aged 75 and over saves the Dutch state an average of €200,000 in deferred expenditure. Less in Southern Europe, more in Germany.”

Released care labour — the largest component

In addition to direct avoided expenditure, labour capacity is released. Informal carers return to regular productive activity, care workers can be redeployed, and doctors’ time becomes available. For the EU-27:

This capacity can be deployed in more productive sectors that currently face chronic shortages: construction (housing shortage), technical industry, education and climate-adaptation infrastructure.

Fewer people in healthcare, fewer medicines

The €60–80 billion is the amount governments no longer need to spend and can therefore allocate freely. The rest lies in the private sphere: higher wages, greater productivity and increased tax revenues through regular channels.

What can be done with this amount?

The central question is: what happens if €60–80 billion per year is invested directly in climate action? The balance is positive. Every euro of climate-dividend funding directed towards structural climate investment generates multiple returns in avoided damage, CO₂ removal and long-term stability.

Put differently: the amount that governments and the surviving working population no longer have to spend on pensions, long-term care and pharmaceuticals for the deceased people aged 75 and over is precisely of the order needed to address the climate problem — which caused the excess mortality — structurally. A cycle that finances itself.

What official models do not include

The EU Ageing Report 2024 projects pension and healthcare burdens through 2050 without a climate-mortality correction. Official models are therefore systematically too pessimistic about long-term burdens. When the climate correction is included, the projected pension burden as a share of GDP falls by 0.4–0.6 percentage points by 2050 for almost every EU country.

Actuaries at European pension funds, reinsurers and health insurers have already incorporated these corrections implicitly into their mortality tables since 2023. Public debate is lagging behind — causing policymakers to respond to projections that ignore the correction.

The core message

Excess mortality is not an accounting curiosity. It is a structural flow of resources large enough to address the underlying climate problem without new taxes, without cuts to other items and without an external donor. If there is one item in the European budget that — separate from all moral considerations — makes a mathematical case for large-scale climate investment, this is it.
