Gevolgenkaart · Singapore · juni 2026

De Singaporese Gevolgenkaart

Vijftien scenario's, tien Singaporese federale mechanismen. Wat uw aandeel in Singapore in 2030 oplevert onder cumulatief MOF/MAS/MOM-beleid — wanneer CPF-hervorming, GST 9%, Pijler Twee, BTO+Prime, werkvergunning-quota's, ABSD, COE, koolstofbelasting en de MAS S$NEER-band op derde orde door elkaar werken.

Door Jacobus van Merksteijn · Malta · juni 2026

If the Brussels Consequences Map brought the EU's hidden accumulation into view, and the Swiss version did the same for Bern, then the Singaporean Consequences Map asks how a small, highly developed city-state stacks up its own choices. Singapore's policy stack is exceptionally legible — every mechanism is documented in MOF budget statements, CPF announcements, MAS quarterly reports and HDB framework documents. Yet third-order accumulation is rarely shown within a single framework.

This map plots fifteen Singaporean scenarios against ten national mechanisms in operation in 2026. Each cell is the % effect on income (citizens) or turnover (businesses) by 2030 under cumulative policy. Read column by column to see what each lever does. Read row by row to see what one person or business absorbs.

What the map shows

Each row is a Singaporean scenario — from Mr Tan, the 67-year-old retiree in a four-room HDB flat in Bedok, via Daniel the software engineer in Bishan, to a semiconductor multinational in Tuas. Each column is one national mechanism, from CPF Life reform via the GST 9% increase, Pillar Two for multinationals, the BTO + Prime/Plus flat regime, work-permit quotas, ABSD, COE, the carbon-tax progression and the MAS S$NEER band. The far-right column is an alternative MOF reference — what the same scenarios would produce under a more SME-friendly, more affordability-anchored course.

The ten mechanisms

Social + fiscal

  • CPF Life reform — the 2024-2026 adjustments to the Basic Retirement Sum, Full Retirement Sum and the payment profile of the CPF Life Escalating Plan.
  • GST 9% (2024+) — the increase in GST from 7% to 8% in 2023 and to 9% in 2024, with compensation through the Assurance Package.
  • Pillar Two DTT/IIR — Singapore's Domestic Top-up Tax and Income Inclusion Rule for large multinationals, introduced from 2025.

Housing + labour

  • BTO + Prime flat policy — the 2024 BTO classification framework with Prime, Plus and Standard categories, MOP rules and subsidy clawbacks.
  • Work-permit quotas — MOM's tightening of S-Pass, Work Permit and EP quotas across sectors.
  • ABSD stamp duty — Additional Buyer's Stamp Duty for second/third homes and foreign buyers.
  • COE car quotas — the Certificate of Entitlement quota system for vehicle ownership.

Climate + monetary

  • Carbon tax S$25→S$80 — the progression of the carbon tax from S$25/tonne (2024) to S$45 (2026-27) and S$50-80 (2030).
  • MAS S$NEER band — the Monetary Authority of Singapore's nominal effective exchange-rate policy, currently on a modest appreciation path.

BiCRS/ethanol route

  • BiCRS/ethanol route — what the same scenarios would produce under the replacement of the carbon tax and LNG import subsidies with BiCRS (biomass injection in the equatorial belt, directly bordering Singapore) plus ethanol as a motor fuel. Singapore is dependent on energy imports; BiCRS adoption decompresses import-cost and carbon-tax pressure drastically. Heavy industry, semiconductor MNCs and logistics hubs benefit disproportionately; households benefit through lower energy and transport costs.

How to read the figures

These are third-order modelled effects — direct costs, behavioural adjustment, secondary equilibrium — under cumulative policy. They are not forecasts. They are compiled from MOF budget statements 2024-2026, CPF annual reports, MAS quarterly reports, HDB BTO Prime/Plus framework documents (2024), the MOM Work Permit framework (2025), and the revisions to the carbon-tax legislation (2025).

What the map does show is which scenarios are net winners and which are net losers under cumulative Singaporean national policy as it stands in mid-2026 — and what escape route the BiCRS/ethanol route offers: a consistently positive effect on all fifteen scenarios, from +7% for the fresh graduate to +57% for the Logistics hub in Changi.

Singapore's policy stack is the most legible in this series. Yet the accumulation is the same as everywhere else: a citizen or business considers each policy on its own and finds it manageable. Read together — as the map does — the picture changes. And the BiCRS/ethanol route, pursued from the equatorial belt that literally borders Singapore, shows that there is an escape route that works for everyone.

What follows

The Singaporean Consequences Map is being published in parallel with the Swiss Consequences Map. Both are first iterations of a methodology that travels well: take a documented policy stack, plot fifteen to twenty representative scenarios against ten to twelve mechanisms, model third-order accumulation, and compare it with an alternative course.

The aim is not to predict anyone's 2030 outcome. The aim is to place cumulative policy within a single readable framework, ahead of the budgets and elections that decide them.
