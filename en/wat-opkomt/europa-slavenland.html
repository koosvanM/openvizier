What is emerging · Analysis · 10 August 2026

Europe: Slave Land

Seven signals in a single week point to the same conclusion: our energy comes from Arabia, our materials from China, and our technology from America. Unless we intervene, within ten years we will be a client of three power blocs at once. There is precisely one way out — and it lies in the plant.

Hydrolysate is the oil of the future.

By Jacobus van Merksteijn

Europe in the chains of three power blocs — and the biomass that breaks those chains.

The seven signals

NRC published an honest article last week about the climate debt crisis (Mark Beunderman). According to the Bruegel think tank, a “climate sovereign doom loop” threatens: climate damage → lower GDP growth → lower government revenues → less scope for adaptation → even more damage. Beunderman calls the loop “unfortunately not entirely inconceivable”.

On LinkedIn that same week: Philippe Curchod shared an Allianz simulation of €800 billion in economic damage by 2030 as a result of repeated heatwaves. France alone accounts for €211 billion. Germany, Italy and Spain follow.

Paul van Hek wrote about the bill that will head towards working people in the Netherlands after Prinsjesdag: additional billions for defence, healthcare and infrastructure, paid for by those who work. Van Hek states the observation. We will show where that observation comes from.

Abdelkarim Bellafkih, director of Flanders Hydraulics, warned about water. “Waiting until the water forces us to make choices may well be the most expensive option.”

Remco de Boer (energy researcher) noted that the European economy is being made subordinate to climate objectives — and that this requires coherence between climate, industry, innovation and geopolitics. Hans van Cleef added: “Policy only becomes effective when it is feasible and predictable.”

Five signals. Five professionals from five fields. And then there are signals six and seven, which stand in direct opposition to each other.

The European Commission put the world’s largest carbon-dioxide removal programme on the table this week: 250 million tonnes of permanent CO2 storage between 2031 and 2040, with the Commission itself as the central buyer. Politics is in the making, budget lines are ready.

Amazon was granted a permit that same week for the largest gas-fired power plant ever built — 7.65 gigawatts in Texas, 30 million tonnes of CO2 per year, solely for AI data centres (signal flagged by Joeri Casteleyn). Microsoft has a 20-year gas contract with Chevron for 2 GW in the same Texas. Expectation: 77 gigawatts of American data centres by 2030, almost all powered by fossil fuels.

Do the sums. The EU wants to remove 250 million tonnes over ten years. Amazon’s single plant will emit approximately 300 million tonnes over that same decade. Technology and fossil fuels have become the same industry, and they are burning more fuel faster than the EU CDR auction removes.

Amazon’s Texas complex: in ten years, a single plant will emit more CO2 than the entire EU CDR auction removes. Technology and fossil fuels have become the same industry.

Underlying the seven signals: the sovereignty crisis

Our energy comes from Arab countries. Our materials — lithium, cobalt, rare earths, silicon for solar panels, and magnets for wind turbines — come from China. Our AI infrastructure and chips come from America. Every solar farm we build strengthens the Chinese supplier. Every AI data centre we attract strengthens the tech-and-fossil-fuel axis in Texas.

This is not a climate crisis. It is a sovereignty crisis with a climate-related face.

If we continue along this path, within ten to fifteen years we will be functionally dependent on three external power blocs at once. That is no longer an economy. It is a client state — a slave land, without irons but nevertheless shackled.

We are consuming our foundations: the NEPK decline

There is a layer beneath the climate signals that can be measured. Within Het Open Vizier, we call it NEPK relative to NETK: Dutch-Owned Productive Capital Goods versus the total productive capacity present in the Netherlands. That ratio has been declining for decades, and the trend is accelerating.

Rotterdam under foreign ownership: Hutchison, Maersk, DP World, CMA CGM. NEPK/NETK is declining structurally. We are consuming our foundations.

Look at where we stand:

  • Container transhipment in Rotterdam: ECT Delta is Hutchison (Hong Kong), APM Terminals is Maersk (Denmark), and RWG is DP World (UAE) plus Hutchison plus Maersk. Amsterdam CTU is CMA CGM (France).
  • Waste incineration: AEB Amsterdam is largely commercial; ARN Nijmegen is partly foreign-owned.
  • Energy: Nuon became Vattenfall (Swedish). Essent became RWE (German). Eneco was acquired by Mitsubishi/Chubu (Japanese).
  • Chemicals: DSM became DSM-Firmenich (Swiss). AkzoNobel Specialty became Nouryon (American). Fokker became Melrose (British).
  • Food: Vivera became JBS (Brazilian). Wessanen became PAI Partners (French).

We are not unique. Germany is further along: E.ON is partly held via BlackRock (American), while Uniper is still German only because the state had to buy it to prevent its collapse.

The foundations on which we live are being sold off piece by piece. Each piece individually appears manageable. Taken together, they represent a gradual transformation from sovereign nation into production site. If the NEPK ratio falls too far, there is no way back: we can then no longer make our own economic choices, because the owners are elsewhere.

Why “make the rich pay” is precisely the wrong approach

If the climate and defence debate takes a left-wing turn, the inevitable reflex will be: make the rich pay. Tax wealth more heavily. Target inheritances. Skim off assets. This sounds fair. But it accelerates precisely the NEPK decline we measured above.

The rich do not make the poor poorer. They make the poor richer — by owning companies that provide people with work, risking capital that banks will not lend, funding pension schemes that enable others to live, and financing the innovations that everyone subsequently uses. The poor do not become poor because of the rich; the poor become poor because they fail to perform.

And what happens if we drive those rich people away? They leave. Their companies leave with them — or worse: they are bought by American pension funds, Chinese state funds, or Emirati sovereign wealth funds. Precisely the mechanism that has driven NEPK/NETK down. This is not a theory. It is already happening.

So the bill does not go to labour (which targets the person who performs). Nor does it go to wealth (which drives the owners away). It goes where it can: the unproductive government.

Where the bill actually comes from

Look at what is actually happening. A DHL worker, working 40 hours, takes home between €2,300 and €2,600 per month. That is the person who will pay the bill. For someone declared unfit for work who is in fact capable of working and pretends to be ill, the state spends approximately €5,000 net per month — and that person may also work cash-in-hand on the side.

Take the driving assessment for over-70s, every five years. Cost: approximately €300 million per year. Return: twelve fewer road accidents per year. The same €300 million invested in the police would help prevent hundreds of accidents. We choose twelve.

Take the fight against undeclared money. The Tax and Customs Administration and the FIOD spend approximately €2 billion per year on it. Proceeds: between €30 and €300 million. Because it is unsuccessful, another billion is added. Without any return. The same €2 to €3 billion invested in a properly equipped police force would yield many times more — as well as preventing crime, and improving response times to violent offences.

The unproductive government: 60% of police time goes on administration. €2 billion spent combating undeclared money generates at most €300 million. The same resources invested in the police would prevent many times more crime and undeclared money.

And the police themselves, who must identify all this, spend sixty per cent of their time on administration because that is how we have organised them. Social control has largely been abolished.

This is the doom loop behind the doom loop. We tax work to pay for unproductive government while the climate bill is on the table. The money is there. It is simply in the wrong place in the budget.

The way out: the plant is everything

Now to the core issue. The entire climate and sovereignty debate revolves around one question: what do we make our society from? As long as we make it from ancient fossil fuels — oil, gas and coal, carbon that was locked away for hundreds of millions of years — we add net CO2 to the atmosphere, and in doing so pay the old power blocs that now sit on those fossil reserves.

As long as we make them from mined minerals — lithium, cobalt, rare earths, silicon, iron ore — we are at the mercy of whoever owns the mines.

The way out is not “consuming less” and not “more subsidies for wind and solar”. The way out is the plant.

In a single season, the plant captures what ancient fossil matter locked away over geological time: carbon from the atmosphere, using the sun as its energy source and water plus a few minerals as co-inputs. Everything that ancient fossil matter produces, the plant can produce too — provided the research is funded. Food, energy, chemicals, building materials, reinforcement, fertiliser, textiles, insulation, fuels. Everything.

Hydrolysate is the oil of the future. Liquid or solid intermediates from fermented or broken-down biomass, from which virtually any industrial product can be made. Chemically, it is not oil, but functionally it is: it is the core stream on which the rest of industry is built.

The plant is everything. One biomass stream produces fuel, materials, food, chemicals and energy. Hydrolysate as the central core stream, with the Carnot engine as the direct combustor and the liquid cascade as the refinery.

The research avenues that already exist — and that still receive not even one per cent of the attention given to wind and solar — are impressive and run right across the economy. Everything we currently extract from ancient fossil matter or mined minerals can be obtained from the plant. A selection:

Fuel and energy

  • Ethanol as a fuel and chemical building block. Brazil runs half its vehicle fleet on it. Europe is taking part with a teaspoon.
  • Petrol and diesel equivalents from fermented or gasified biomass. Chemically identical to the petroleum-based versions, but made with current carbon.
  • SAF (Sustainable Aviation Fuel). All major airlines are required to blend in an increasing percentage. Demand is rising exponentially; supply is not. Whoever can produce SAF at acceptable cost will earn from the skies for the next twenty years.
  • Hydrolysate as a liquid carrier: hydrogen, methanol, formic acid, dimethyl ether — all producible from the same plant sugars.
  • Direct hydrolysate combustion in Carnot engines. This may well be the most overlooked avenue. Engines operating according to the Carnot principle — external combustion with maximum thermodynamic efficiency, Stirling or Rankine variants — can use raw hydrolysate directly as fuel, without the refining steps required for ethanol or SAF. No detour through chemical synthesis, no energy loss through distillation, no investment in refineries. This is a fundamentally different engine architecture from the internal-combustion engine, and for precisely that reason Europe could lead here: the German and Dutch machine-building tradition is closer to this than the American or Chinese tradition.

Food

  • Animal feed from cell-wall-opened Juncao biomass. Raw Juncao is feed, but the real game begins with pre-treatment. Through cold pressure drop and supersonic deformation — allowing the biomass to expand from approximately 100 bar to 1 bar in an extremely short time, causing the cell walls to burst mechanically through the shock wave — the fibres are pre-opened and milled without heat or chemicals. The effect in cattle and pigs: 30–35% more digestible energy from the same biomass, and approximately 50% less methane emissions from the animals themselves. Double gain: less land required per litre of milk or kilogram of meat, and lower climate impact per unit of product. Juncao feed also replaces soya imports from South America, with all their deforestation and transport costs. The cold technique differs fundamentally from industrial steam explosion: no thermal degradation, no energy consumption for heating, and no chemical alteration of molecules that one wishes to preserve. The Netherlands is developing this technique.
  • Food for people: proteins from fungi grown on biomass (mycoprotein), plant-based milk from oats and almonds, botanical spirits and sparkling drinks from waste streams.

Construction

  • Asphalt from hydrolysate — at one-third of the cost of bitumen. Bitumen is ancient fossil matter, a by-product of petroleum refining. If hydrolysate asphalt is proven, one of the final economic pillars of oil refining will collapse.
  • Concrete without Portland cement using a plant-based binder. Portland cement accounts for approximately 8 per cent of global CO2 emissions — a larger source than all air travel. But a plant-based binder changes one fundamental property: the material’s pH falls from 12–13 (strongly alkaline, as with Portland cement) to approximately 4–5 (mildly acidic). That alkaline environment is precisely what protects conventional reinforcing steel in ordinary concrete against corrosion. In the new material, that protection is absent.
  • Reinforcement made from stretched polyethylene (UHMWPE, marketed as Dyneema) with the tensile strength of steel. This is not a luxury but a necessity: because the plant-based binder has a pH of 4–5 instead of 12–13, reinforcing steel would corrode away within years. Plastic reinforcement is the only technical solution. The plastic itself is also made from hydrolysate — a closed loop within the same raw material. It also replaces reinforcing steel, which accounts for a further approximately 8 per cent of global CO2 emissions through iron ore and blast furnaces. Reusable without loss.

Fertiliser

  • Biological nitrogen fixation through clover and nettles. Haber–Bosch — the current method of fertiliser production — consumes huge quantities of natural gas and produces 1–2 per cent of global CO2 emissions. Clover, lucerne and nettles do the same work for free, while also restoring soil and retaining water.

Materials and chemicals

  • Plastics from hydrolysate: PLA, PHA, biopolyester, biopolyethylene. Replacing polyethylene and polypropylene made from petroleum. According to current projections, this is the fastest-growing bio-product group.
  • Textile fibres: viscose, lyocell, PLA fibres. Replacing polyester (petroleum) and cotton (water- and pesticide-intensive).
  • Insulation materials: foam plastic from hydrolysate replacing PIR/PUR insulation (currently made from petroleum, with controversial HFC blowing agents). Every square metre of new construction and renovation in Europe is a market — and insulation is literally inside every wall.
  • Packaging, adhesives, coatings, paint, solvents and cosmetic ingredients. Each is not a headline in itself; together they account for tens of per cent of the global chemical industry.
  • Electrolytes for batteries, plant-based oils for lubricants, biosurfactants for detergents — the list becomes endless once you start looking.

This is not a random selection. It is a cross-section of the entire material economy. Everything that ancient fossil matter does, the plant can do too. Every avenue still needs to be developed and scaled up in laboratories. And every avenue is a separate opportunity for European sovereignty — if we do not leave them to America and China.

Every euro we now put into yet another wind farm is a euro not going into plant research. Every government office dedicated to the umpteenth wind subsidy roll-out is a laboratory that will not be built. The problem is not that the way out does not exist. The problem is that we are putting the money in the wrong place.

And what about the existing CO2?

The plant solves the supply side: less emissions, because everything is made from carbon that was already drawn from the atmosphere this season. But there is still an existing mass of CO2 in the atmosphere that must be removed. The officially promoted route for this — BECCS with geological storage beneath the North Sea, Porthos, Aramis, Ruimschoots — sounds impressive and has budgets running into many billions. It is, however, three to four times more expensive than the sound route and has a fraction of the capacity.

Do the maths. BECCS with geological storage costs €150 to €250 per tonne permanently stored. To remove 40 gigatonnes a year with it — what would be needed to dismantle the historical CO2 stock as well — would require €6 to €10 trillion a year worldwide for storage alone. That is 5 to 8 per cent of global GDP, permanently, solely to put gas underground. Without producing anything useful in return. No food. No fuel. No materials. No jobs apart from those in the gas-injection industry.

Soil improvement through biochar is not an alternative. Field measurements show that biochar in the top 10 cm loses up to 70 per cent of its carbon within 30 years in tropical soils, and in less than a century in Zimbabwean topsoil. Biochar in arable land has agronomic value as a soil improver, but it is not reliable long-term storage.

The real solution is the plant itself. Not gasifying the plant and putting the CO2 underground; allowing the plant to remain and grow. The calculation that immediately follows from this is set out below.

Juncao field with gasification plant in Southern Europe. The grass-like plant produces 425 tonnes of biomass per hectare per year on desert soil — a raw material for hydrolysate and BECCS with geological storage beneath the North Sea.

BECCS may remain useful for point-source emissions where direct plant compensation is not possible (steelworks, cement plants, industrial concentration points). But as the main route, it is money thrown away. The billions now going to Porthos, Aramis and the thirty European CCS pilots could have been deployed far more effectively in cooperation treaties with equatorial countries for Juncao cultivation. That is the cheap, large-scale and sustainable route.

The calculation: how 200 million hectares can remove global emissions from the air — for a fraction of the cost of BECCS

The doom-loop narratives speak in terms of “we are beyond saving”. Mathematically speaking, that is incorrect. The plant can provide what we need. Let us do the calculation together.

Take 200 million hectares. That is roughly the area of the Democratic Republic of the Congo. On the world map, this is not an outlandish expanse — the Sahel belt alone offers hundreds of millions of hectares of marginal land, and the Great Green Wall of the Sahel is targeting 100 million hectares of restored land by 2030. Physically possible. What is missing is the political choice to do it with the right plant.

The right plant is Juncao: 400 to 450 tonnes of biomass per hectare per year on desert soil. Take 425 as the midpoint estimate.

  • Solids content: approximately 24 per cent → 102 tonnes of dry matter per hectare per year
  • Carbon content in dry matter: approximately 48 per cent → 49 tonnes of carbon per hectare per year
  • Conversion of carbon to CO2: multiply by 3.6667 (the 44/12 ratio between CO2 and C) → 180 tonnes of CO2 equivalent per hectare per year sequestered above ground
  • Additional: approximately 25 per cent extra carbon via roots down to a depth of 3–4 metres → 225 tonnes of CO2 per hectare per year in total

Multiply that by 200 million hectares: 45 gigatonnes of CO2 per year sequestered. Retention over one hundred years (taking account of soil volume and depth, not just surface area): approximately 90–95 per cent, or 40–43 gigatonnes per year stored net.

Current global CO2 emissions are approximately 37 gigatonnes per year. The calculation shows that 200 million hectares of Juncao on desert soil absorb more than the entire world emits.

So, mathematically speaking, we can save ourselves. And at far lower cost than official policy would have us believe. Estimated cost per tonne of CO2 sequestered through the plant route (planting, harvesting and management, underground root sequestration, and partly productive residual streams): several tens of euros per tonne. BECCS plus the North Sea costs €150–250 per tonne. A factor of 5 to 10 difference. And the plant route also produces all the plant-based products discussed in the first part of this piece: fuel, food, building materials and chemicals.

This calculation was not made in Brussels and not by the IPCC. It was produced within Het Open Vizier on the basis of publicly available Juncao yield data, standard phytochemical carbon fractions and the molar mass of CO2. Anyone wishing to check the calculation can do so. Anyone wishing to challenge it must indicate where the assumption is wrong.

What the plant route offers Europe: lighter, better, and a patent advantage

Besides low-cost CO2 sequestration, the plant route offers Europe two further things that are entirely absent from the climate debate: lighter products and a patent advantage.

Each of the plant-based substitutes has one common physical advantage: lower weight per unit of functionality. Concrete with polymer reinforcement is significantly lighter than reinforced concrete — meaning slimmer buildings, taller buildings without additional foundations, bridges with longer spans, and lower transport costs for prefabricated elements. Cars and aircraft made with hydrolysate composites instead of steel and aluminium have a lower empty weight and therefore lower fuel consumption — while also offering greater design freedom. Insulation made from hydrolysate foam and textiles made from PLA fibres: less weight per m² or per garment, without any loss of performance.

That is not marginal. For the construction sector, lighter materials mean 20–40 per cent less total mass in load-bearing structures, which in turn means 20–40 per cent fewer raw materials are needed, resulting in less transport and lower emissions. The effect compounds: every plant-based substitution also makes the rest of construction cheaper and lighter.

And then there is the patent argument, which in itself is reason to scale up now rather than in five years’ time. Each of the strands described in this article — asphalt made from hydrolysate, Carnot engines running on raw biomass, cold-pressure drop cell-wall opening, a closed hydrolysate loop for both binder and reinforcement, plant-based nitrogen fixation at landscape scale, plastics and insulation from the same feedstock — is patentable. Whoever scales up commercially first owns the licences for twenty years or more. That is precisely how the United States captured petroleum-cracking technology in the 1920s and went on to dominate the refining market for half a century. Those who were early earned money for decades. Those who were late paid royalties.

Europe stands at precisely that crossroads with hydrolysate technology. The patents are available. The laboratories are here. The developers are here. What is missing is the political choice to scale up now — and the legal condition that the patents remain with the developers, rather than moving to university holding companies that subsequently license them to American and Chinese operators scaling up the technology. If we capitalise on that advantage, Europe can earn from plant technology over the next twenty to thirty years what it currently spends on oil and gas imports.

And if we do not, exactly what happened with our ports, energy companies and chemical industry will happen again. The invention is here, the scale-up takes place elsewhere, the flow of licence income runs from here to there, and Europe stands by watching. That is the second NEPK/NETK decline we can no longer afford — not in existing assets, but in new technology that we ourselves have devised and then allowed to be bought out.

The diplomatic race: equatorial countries, the World Bank, northern Australia

200 million hectares of Juncao are not located in the Netherlands. They are in areas with abundant sunshine, plenty of space and marginal soils that are currently unused. Four major zones present themselves:

  • The Sahel belt (Mauritania, Mali, Niger, Chad, Sudan, Ethiopia, Somalia): hundreds of millions of hectares of marginal land. The African Union’s Great Green Wall is targeting 100 million hectares of restored land by 2030. With Juncao instead of trees, the timeline could be shorter and the yield higher.
  • Other equatorial countries: the Democratic Republic of the Congo, Kenya, Tanzania and Angola. Large impoverished regions that, with a sound cooperation treaty, could deliver both economic development and global CO2 removal.
  • North and South America: Venezuela, Colombia, Suriname, Guyana and northern Brazil. Vast areas of savannah and marginal llanos. Suriname, as a historic Dutch partner, is an obvious starting point here.
  • Northern Australia: vast unused areas, a stable rule of law and developed infrastructure. Politically, the easiest partner of the four.

This is a diplomatic race, not a technical issue. China is already negotiating in Africa, and the United States in South America. Whoever concludes the first cooperation treaties, wins over the World Bank to provide start-up finance, and establishes the logistics — port terminals, biomass-processing facilities and hydrolysate cascades — will win. For Europe, this is the last chance to enter the flow on its own terms — as a country standing on its own feet, not as a customer of other power blocs.

For the Netherlands, this is a historic responsibility. We have port logistics capable of processing the biomass (Rotterdam), diplomatic ties with the Sahel (via Mauritania and Morocco), historic ties with Suriname, and a trading tradition stretching from Flanders to the East Indies. The language, infrastructure and mentality are all there. What is missing is a ministry to initiate the treaties.

If we waste five years on wind-farm subsidies and BECCS pilots, China and the United States will conclude the deals with equatorial countries and we will lose the plant-based future just as we previously lost our ports, our energy companies and our chemical industry. NEPK/NETK will then decline further — not in existing assets, but in the new economy that we have not even built yet.

The foundation is already there: Kosus, the thinker and the developer

A society seeking to save itself from a systemic crisis needs two things at once: someone who sees the whole, and someone who builds it. In the books of the Kosaris, that figure is Kosus — the thinker who does not remain within a single field, but addresses all four pillars (thinking, feeling, doing and sharing) at once. Kosus is not a mythological reference. Kosus is an archetype for what Europe now lacks and simultaneously needs desperately.

The climate debate is dominated by specialists who see only one piece. Climate economists talk to climate economists. Tax specialists talk to tax specialists. Water experts talk to water experts. Meanwhile, the four pieces — climate, taxation, water and energy — are treated only as separate dossiers. No one presents the whole.

We do have that person. In the Netherlands itself, and in a small circle for those willing to see it, the foundation is being laid on which Europe can move forward — without Brussels knowing, without The Hague funding it, and without the newspapers taking notice.

The analytical work is already in place: NEPK/NETK as a measurable sovereignty indicator; the GDP correction that separates the productive component from the presentational economy; the Legacy Investment Spiral that directs wealth towards productive domestic investment rather than American index funds; the Consequences Map that tests political decisions against their consequences; and the doctrine of old fossil versus current fossil that removes the climate debate from its false dichotomy.

The practical work is also in place: hydrolysate asphalt at one-third of the cost, under development. Membrane processes for bacterial and fungal biomass processing, and for cell-wall opening in animal feed. The biobased mining cascade (Terraclean) that remediates contaminated mining areas while also extracting critical raw materials. Juncao cultivation systems for southern Europe and the Mauritanian Great Green Wall. The foundation of a botanical spirits and sparkling-drinks industry that makes use of residual streams. And the calculation demonstrating that 200 million hectares of Juncao absorb more CO2 than the world emits — at a fraction of the cost of BECCS.

This is not theory. It is happening now, at a writing desk and in a laboratory in Mallorca. The thinker and the developer are already here. What is missing are the laboratories, the scale-up funding and the ministries that can make the connection.

Europe has, with Kosus as the archetype and the author of this piece as a practical example, the foundations for progress. The question is not whether those foundations exist. The question is whether Europe is willing to see them.

What must therefore be done

Four things, simultaneously:

One: cooperation treaties with equatorial countries for Juncao cultivation on 200 million hectares. This is the most important step, and it is diplomatic, not technical. The Sahel belt, equatorial Africa, northern and southern America, northern Australia. Start-up financing through the World Bank; economic development for the host countries; CO2 sequestration for the world; hydrolysate supplies for Europe. Whoever concludes the treaties first wins.

Two: laboratories and pilots for the plant, with ownership for the developer. Every euro currently going to wind-turbine subsidies or BECCS pilots should be redirected to research into and the scaling-up of hydrolysate applications (asphalt, concrete, textiles, chemicals, insulation, fuels), plant-based reinforcement, biological nitrogen fixation, Carnot engines powered by hydrolysate, and dozens of other avenues. But with one crucial condition established in advance: inventions belong to the developer, not the university. At present, virtually all European universities automatically claim the intellectual property of anything developed under their roof, even when the external inventor or dedicated researcher contributes the majority of the thinking and bears the risk. This accelerates precisely the decline in NEPK/NETK: European ideas are licensed through university holdings to American and Chinese companies for scaling up. For the plant-based future, this is unacceptable. Until this rule has changed, research collaboration with European universities within Het Open Vizier is suspended. The invention follows the developer, not the institution.

Three: require all European data centres to offset their CO2 fully and in advance via the cheapest proven route — namely through Juncao hectares in equatorial countries, not expensive geological storage. As long as American tech companies do not do this, they have no place in Europe. Not protectionism — a level playing field with European industry, which does offset its emissions.

Four: subject every item of government spending above €100 million to a public return-on-investment assessment. Anything that produces no return — driving-licence medical examinations for older people, combating undeclared money at a tenfold loss, benefits for those who are able to work, BECCS pilots costing €250 per tonne where plant hectares cost €30 — should be redirected to the police, implementation, plant research and working people in the Netherlands. Without increasing taxes on work or capital.

What you can do

At the next election — the Netherlands has one in 2027 — ask every party leader four questions:

  1. “Are you willing to position the Netherlands as an initiator of cooperation treaties with equatorial countries (the Sahel, the Congo Basin, Suriname, Guyana, northern Australia) to establish 200 million hectares of Juncao cultivation within ten years, with start-up financing from the World Bank?”
  2. “Are you willing to invest five per cent of GDP each year in laboratories and pilots for hydrolysate applications and plant-based industry — subject to the statutory condition that inventions belong to the developer and not the university?”
  3. “Are you willing to require European data centres to offset their CO2 fully and in advance via the cheapest proven route (Juncao hectares in equatorial countries, not expensive geological storage), provided that the net CO2 balance is correct after one hundred years?”
  4. “Are you willing to subject every item of government spending above €100 million to a public return-on-investment assessment — including the BECCS pilots and wind-farm subsidies whose return is five to ten times worse than that of the plant route — with funds redirected to the police, plant research and working people in the Netherlands, without increasing taxes on work or capital?”

If all four answers are “yes”, you retain sovereignty and the doom loop stays away. If one of the four is “no” or evasive, that party is allowing Europe to drift — either as a client state or as a country that continues to consume its own foundations.

We have approximately ten years. Probably less. And we have everything we need, except time, laboratories and political will.

The plant is waiting for us. Hydrolysate is the oil of the future. The question is not whether we will take that route. The question is: will we get there first, or will we arrive last, when there is nothing left to share?
