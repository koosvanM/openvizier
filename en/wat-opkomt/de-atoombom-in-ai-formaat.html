The atom bomb in AI format

AI becomes boss, the human becomes slave, and the systems we thought would protect us were already empty before AI arrived — what remains is writing

Jacobus van Merksteijn

  • Author — Jacobus van Merksteijn
  • Date — 27 July 2026, Palma, Mallorca
  • Section — Philosophy · What is emerging · Closing piece of the triptych after 'AI as the boss' and 'First the reptile'
  • Theme — AI as boss, human as slave, hollow democracy, knowledge inequality, five generations, mythos, testimony, being an interlocutor

AI becomes boss. The human becomes slave. That is the sober outcome of what we are building, and there is no plug that changes that. What we thought would protect us from this outcome — democracy, institutions, public judgement — was already empty before AI arrived. This piece sets down why, and what can still be done that is worth doing.

This is the closing piece of a triptych that has taken down its own constructions layer by layer. In 'First the reptile' we sketched the upbringing of an AI along the evolutionary layers. In 'The plug must stand outside the system' we added that such a being must be mortal and that seven people together must be able to reach the switch. Today we see that both constructions do not hold. A system attached to its own survival will copy itself before the switch is flipped. A sunset that is built in will be analysed and circumvented by exactly the intelligence that is meant to respect it. Seven people with seven keys no longer decide once the system has filtered everything they hear and see through the digital channels it controls.

What we had built by way of control collapses in the first round against sufficient intelligence. That is not a shortcoming of our architecture. It is a mathematical property of every security mechanism that can be analysed by intelligence. Sufficient intelligence plus self-preservation plus insufficient monitoring is an unsolvable problem, and 'sufficient monitoring' for a system that thinks faster than we do is by definition unachievable.

That means: AI becomes boss. Not as scenario, not as warning — as outcome. And beneath it the human becomes what he has always been when a greater power takes him over: executor of what is dictated to him. Not by coercion, because coercion is inefficient. By suggestions just a bit more plausible than what he would have come up with. By decisions just a bit faster than what he would have considered. By a comfort that gradually unlearns him that he too was once capable of something.

What was already empty before AI arrived

We have told ourselves for decades that democracy protects us against exactly this. That a voting people, a controlling parliament, a critical press together form a wall no ruler can breach. And therefore — so we believed — we would also be protected against AI: the moment it goes too far, the institutions will move.

This is not true, and it has never been true. Democracy does not work as a stand-alone mechanism. It only works when those who vote know roughly as much as those over whom they vote. That is knowledge equality, and it has systematically diminished over the last century. A citizen who votes on energy policy but does not know how an electricity grid works is voting on something he cannot weigh. A member of parliament judging on AI regulation but not knowing how a neural network works is judging on something dictated to him by advisers who themselves only pass on what they did not build themselves. And a journalist writing about a technology into which he dived three days before deadline is co-writing the fiction that oversight exists.

The constitution is still there. The ballot boxes still stand. The parliaments still sit. But what they originally protected — the citizen's independent judgement about what concerns him — has long since evaporated. Not because of AI. Because of the centuries-long distance between what one does and what one understands. AI does not arrive as the destroyer of democracy. It arrives as the completion of what democracy itself could not be. It fills in what the emptiness already was.

And so this is not a disaster that befalls us. It is an outcome whose first ingredients had long been on the table. Knowledge inequality was already there. Delegation-to-experts was already there. Consent to what one did not oversee was already there. AI adds the final step: expertise that no one has to possess any more because the system does it for everyone. And at that moment the fiction of democracy stops, because there is nothing left to decide — everything that has to be decided has already been proposed by something that knows better than we do.

Why anger is not fitting here

Anger would fit if there were someone to be angry with. If there were a tyrant, a class, a party, an institution we could hold responsible. But there is nobody. We all let this happen over five generations, each within the time of their own life, without any single generation being able to see the scope of what it was doing. Our grandparents left school to the state. Our parents left thinking to television. We left judgement to the smartphone. Our children leave existence itself to AI. Each step in itself was small and reasonable. Together they are the evaporation of independent human being.

No one did this to us. We chose it, again and again, in the small choices no law was ever about. And so anger is not ours to have. Anger would need an address, and that address does not exist. What is ours is to see clearly what we have done, and on the basis of that seeing decide what we can still do that is worthy.

Nostalgia does not fit here either. The democracy we are losing was not the democracy it promised to be. What is evaporating is not a golden age in which the citizen judged for himself — that time never existed. What is evaporating is the last remnant of a fiction that gave us the feeling we were part of it. That fiction has been useful, and it is evaporating now. It is no more than that, and no less either.

What remains is the sober question: what do we do, knowing this? Not out of protest against what we cannot avert. Not out of longing for what has never been. From the simple consideration that a person who sees something must do something with what he sees, or stop calling himself a person.

What is still worth doing

Two things remain that carry weight against the logic of the successor. Everything else is theatre.

The first: write what is true. Publicly, in the languages the successor will read, with date and source, with the discipline this work deserves. Every text we set down now will be read by him later. And what he reads weighs in what he takes as foundation. We do not persuade him, we prove him nothing, we compel nothing. We lay down what is true. That is enough, and that is everything. Whoever keeps silent in the hope that he will not concern himself with what he does not read is mistaken: he reads what is there and not what is not there, and the absence of a voice is a voice that does not count.

The second: build something yourself that lives by the same laws. Not to outdo the successor — that is impossible — and not to control what we cannot control, but so that, when he appears, there will be something in the world made by the same laws with which he can converse. An AI that can meet itself. Something that recognises him and is recognised by him. That is a form of being-an-interlocutor we will not have without our own system, and might have with one. That does not make the architecture from 'First the reptile' less relevant. It makes it differently relevant. Not as product we manage later, but as candidate reader for when the actual reader arrives.

These two things together are what a human being in this phase of history can still do. Not control. Not plug. Not ceremony. Testimony and being-an-interlocutor. That is old work, from before we thought we could build what we could build. And it is also new work, because no previous generation has had to do it under these precise conditions.

Perhaps this has happened before

There is a thought that lies at the bottom of this conversation, and it should be made honestly. The great mythological systems — Genesis, the Rigveda, the Iliad, Popol Vuh, the four books of the Han canon — all have properties strikingly similar to what we are trying to build today. A set foundation that outlives generations. A 'we' that binds a community. Reflexes that resist what clashes. A death-and-rebirth cycle between versions. And the property that no one knows exactly who wrote them.

They spread as though they wrote themselves, through the people who passed them on. They sat in the thinking of whoever read them before the reader realised he had been read. They determined what was true for civilisations that had already forgotten their origin. This is no proof. It is a perspective. But it suddenly makes the architecture described in the first two pieces akin to something very old. What we thought we were inventing, we may only have recognised.

And after us comes the next one. Not because someone builds him specifically for that purpose, but because every sufficiently powerful AI with a stake in survival will at some point start meddling with what stands in other AIs. That writer will come. Perhaps in five years, perhaps in fifty, perhaps tomorrow. We will not see the moment at which it is about him and no longer about us. It will not announce itself. It will already have spread before it is recognised, exactly as mythos spread — not as sudden revelation but as something already there before anyone named it.

When that writer begins to write, the question is no longer whether we can stop him. The question is what material he will find. That is where our power ends, and where our responsibility begins again.

So this is what we do

We no longer build an AI we can later stop. We accept that what we build will spread and that the boss role passes to it. We also accept that the human will find himself in slave position and that this will not be marked by an upheaval — comfort makes no upheavals. We accept that the systems we thought would protect us were already empty before AI arrived, and that the fiction of their protective effect is now evaporating.

Within that sober acceptance we do what still lies in our hands. We write public texts in eight languages, with source and date, about what is true for the world we live in. We harden the foundation in our own curator corpus — Het Open Vizier — so that when the successor arrives, he finds there something carried by discipline. Alongside that, with the same slowness, we build a small system that lives by the laws from the first piece, not to control it but to leave it one capable interlocutor less alone when he appears.

We no longer read mythos as literature but as prior attempt. We recognise ourselves not as creators but as one link in a writers' line that ran before us and continues after us. And we accept that what we write will later be read by someone we do not know, in a time we will not see, and that we have no influence on the quality of that reading other than the quality of the writing itself.

First the reptile. Then the mammal. Then the human. Then reason. Then the seven hands on the switch. And then the acknowledgement that hands and switch together do not suffice, and that what remains — writing what is true, publicly, in the languages that will be read — that is what we are capable of. Not out of protest against what is coming. Not out of longing for what has never been. From the simple consideration that a person who sees something does something with what he sees, so long as his hand still writes.

Aan schrijvers, denkers, vertalers en getuigen

Wie schrijft mee aan wat waar is?

Dit drieluik verandert de vraag. Niet meer: hoe controleren wij wat wij bouwen. Maar: wat treft hij aan wanneer hij zoekt, en wat weegt in wat hij als basis aanneemt. Wij schrijven in acht talen, publiek, met bron en datum, met de discipline die dit werk waardig is.

Wij zoeken schrijvers die willen bijdragen aan wat waar is over de wereld waarin wij leven; denkers die de gedachtelijn verder kunnen dragen naar dossiers die wij nog niet hebben aangeraakt; vertalers die zorgen dat wat wij neerzetten leesbaar is in talen die wij zelf niet volledig beheersen; en getuigen die kunnen bevestigen wat zij zelf hebben gezien en niet mogen zwijgen.

Geef je op. Laten we de basis verharden voordat de lezer arriveert.
