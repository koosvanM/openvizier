Part A — The five development axes

A.1 Five axes summarized

Axis Core Formation window Strongest causes of failure
1. Body motor skills, strength, endurance 0–7 years (first solidification phase) No outdoor play
2. Feeling attachment, empathy, self-regulation 0–7 years Childcare + mother works
3. Attention focus, concentration 0–7 years (first solidification phase) Screen time
4. Language verbal expression, reading, analysis 7–14 years Screen time + education
5. Sensory blindness 3D capacity: depth perception + depth motor skills + stereometric cortex + stereometric thinking + danger-and-boundary awareness 0–7 years foundation, reinforcement 7–14 years through play and experience Screen time + no outdoor play + no 3D construction

A.2 Five development axes by birth cohort

Description: All five axes decline from cohort 1975 onward, with the exception of spatial 3D thinking, which peaks in cohort 1990 (Flynn peak). From cohort 2005 onward, the decline accelerates on all axes. Cohort 2020 is at 65 to 88% of the 1960 level.

Method: Each axis normalized to 100 for birth cohort 1960. Values for 1975/1990/2005/2020 per axis derived from measurement sources: KOMBO motor skills (axis 1), family stability index (axis 2), NEA focus (axis 3), PIRLS/PISA (axis 4), 3D cubes + PISA spatial-form (axis 5).

Source: Compilation of CBS, OECD PISA, Pietschnig 2015 (Flynn 3D cubes), Erasmus MC.

Overview chart of all five development axes by birth cohort, with 1960 as the reference point (=100). Cohorts 1960/1975/1990/2005/2020 on five axes. Spatial 3D thinking peaked in cohort 1990 (Flynn peak), after which decline accelerated on all axes. Cohort 2020 is at 65 to 88% of the 1960 level. Cohort 2020 is now entering VMBO/HAVO/VWO (solidification phase S3, 14–21 years) and will later enter HBO/WO (S4, 21–35 years). All five axes simultaneously below the 1960 level means that every further education program must work with fewer basic skills. [Source: Position Paper to the Tweede Kamer on PISA reading literacy (2024, 2024D10790), Pietschnig & Voracek, meta-analysis of the Flynn effect (2015)]

A.3 Five components of sensory blindness

Description: Sensory blindness is the interconnected function-axis from depth perception to depth thinking to risk assessment. Loss of depth perception is a SYMPTOM of the same mechanism, not the problem itself.

Method: Five components (each weighted 20% in composite score): A depth perception (stereoscopic input), B depth motor skills (KOMBO), C stereometric cortex (Flynn 3D cubes), D stereometric thinking (PISA spatial form), E danger-and-boundary awareness (outdoor play, construction toys).

Source: Synthesis of this report.

Definition diagram of sensory blindness as the fifth axis with five components (A through E). Sensory blindness = inability to estimate depth and form a 3D image in the brain. Main cause: iPad at 30 cm eliminates depth input during solidification phase S1 (0–7 years) and S2 (7–14 years). Classic myopia = symptom, not the problem. Component D (Stereometric thinking) manifests in solidification phase S3 (14–21 years) as mathematics education; at university (S4, 21–35 years) as beta-technical education. Without an S1+S2 foundation, S3 and S4 cannot be filled. [Source: TU Delft, ‘Solving the shortage of engineers requires a talent strategy’ (2026)]

The five components:

  • A. Depth perception — stereoscopic input from two eyes at different distances — the physical input from which the brain constructs 3D

  • B. Depth motor skills — moving the body in 3D: throwing, catching, jumping, climbing

  • C. Stereometric cortex — neural infrastructure for 3D imaging: visual cortex, parietal cortex, 3D navigation hippocampus, systems thinking in the prefrontal cortex

  • D. Stereometric thinking — cognitive application of 3D capacity: geometry, stereometry, volume integrals, tensors, spatial reasoning

  • E. Danger-and-boundary awareness — feeling one's own 3D body in the 3D world: physical risk assessment, understanding of materials, sense of consequences

In S2 (7–14), screen time displaces play and experiences through which 3D capacity would be strengthened. [Source: AJN Youth Physicians, ‘Fact sheet: screen use at close range’ (2019), TNO, Pluymen et al., ‘Screen use’ (2024)]

APPENDIX 1 - Neural influence model: calculations, sources and safety margins

This appendix documents the quantitative model underlying the projections in this report. The model is called a “neural influence model” because it has a layered structure — comparable to a neural network — in which causes lead to effects through development axes. The term “neural” here refers to the transmission of knowledge across generations, not to a computational neural network in the AI sense.

1. Core concept: what exactly is sensory blindness?

Sensory blindness is the INABILITY to estimate depth and create the stereometric effect in the brain required for three-dimensional imaging. Three-dimensional imaging is the basis for developing new things — without 3D thinking, no new machines, no new mathematics, no new science.

Main cause: iPad, iPhone, smartphone and tablet at a distance of 30 cm. The brain learns to construct 3D through the COMBINATION of:

  • (a) two eyes seeing the same scene from slightly different angles

  • (b) accommodation and convergence at varying distances

  • (c) parallax during one's own movement through space

  • (d) feedback when actually picking up/throwing/climbing

An iPad at 30 cm ELIMINATES (a), (b), (c) and (d) simultaneously. The screen is flat, the distance is fixed, the scene moves with the head, and there is no physical feedback when touching objects in the image. The brain no longer receives 3D training.

Loss of depth perception is a SYMPTOM of the same mechanism: the eyes no longer learn to adapt to different distances. It is NOT the problem itself — someone with loss of depth perception can still see excellently with glasses. What disappears is the ability to form 3D images in the brain.

u(t) = baseline_u + Σ_i [ W_ui × f_i(t - Δ_iu) ] + noise

7.1 Basic effect calculation

def bereken_uitwerking(u, t, oorzaken, W, Delta, schaal, baseline):
active_effects = []
for i, f_i in oorzaken.items():
f_delayed = f_i(t - Delta[u][i])
effect = W[u][i] * f_delayed
active_effects.append(effect)
total = sum(active_effects)
softmax_norm = [e/total for e in active_effects] if total > 0 else [0]*len(active_effects)
return baseline + schaal * sum(sm * (a/max_a) for sm, a in zip(softmax_norm, active_effects))

8. Appendix conclusion

This model is a DERIVATION HYPOTHESIS, not a prediction. It shows what we may expect IF the historical causal chains continue and the selected influence weights are correct.

Core assumption: iPad/screen at 30 cm eliminates the depth input the brain needs to build 3D. All scenario amounts stand or fall with this assumption. Sensitivity analysis shows that even with a -30% iPad mechanism, Dutch Renaissance remains the best option (+€1,370 bn instead of +€1,570 bn).

Recommendation for use:

  • Interpret amounts as ORDERS OF MAGNITUDE with a range of ±20-30%

  • Use scenario comparison for RANKING, not absolute figures

  • Revise the iPad mechanism assumption when new neuroimaging studies become available

Part O — Neural influence analysis (elaboration per outcome)

This section recalculates the transmission of causes (inputs) to outcomes (outputs) using a neural influence model. Each outcome is fitted separately as a weighted sum of lagged inputs, with the lags (Δ) from a grid search and the weights from ridge regression. For validation, a small neural network (MLP, 8 hidden nodes) is fitted to the same data.

Method:

  • (1) Construction: Inputs as time series from 1970–2075 (childcare, maternal employment, screen time, iPad-at-30-cm, no outdoor play, teacher shortage). S-curve extrapolation for the forecast.

  • (2) Lags: For each input, a cohort lag Δ is searched for (grid search over 1–20 years) that explains the measurement at the age of the measured cohort.

  • (3) Linear fit: Ridge regression on lagged inputs — provides hard weights per cause per outcome.

  • (4) NN validation: MLP fit on the same lagged inputs — checks whether more complex patterns are more important.

  • (5) Forecast: Both models project through 2075 using extrapolated inputs.

Part Q — Policy variant: levy instead of ban

In v29, the previously presented "Media Brake" variant (ban on smartphones for under-15s and similar enforcement) has been replaced by a levy variant. Reason: an individual-enforcement ban is politically unfeasible and practically unenforceable in the Dutch polder context. Increasing costs via suppliers is considered feasible because enforcement is limited to a few dozen companies rather than 17 million citizens.

Q.1 Design of the subscription levy

  • Rate: €50 per month per active connection (SIM, eSIM, primary account). Applies to everyone, with no age limit.

  • Scope: Every provider of mobile connectivity or platform services operating in the Netherlands.

  • Enforcement: The provider collects and remits the levy monthly. Non-compliance is penalized with a multiple of the foregone remittance, escalating to market bans in the event of repeated violations.

  • Allocation: 50–75% flows back into the national treasury (tax reduction for Dutch citizens). The remainder goes to a bridging fund for parenting reform.

Q.2 Expected revenue and behavioral effect

  • Volume: Approximately 20 million connections × €50/month × 12 = €12 billion/year gross.

  • Redistribution: 50% to the national treasury = €6 billion/year in tax reductions (~3% lower wage tax for each working person).

  • Behavioral effect: Higher costs discourage unnecessary second and third connections. Parents receive a price incentive not to give children their own connection — without a ban, through market forces.

  • Model input: Screen time 7.8 → 5.5 hours/day; iPad-30cm 120 → 70 min/day at ages 0–7 (within 7 years).
