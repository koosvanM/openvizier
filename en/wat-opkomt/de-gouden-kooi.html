Een krant over denken zonder oogkleppen

What Emerges · Analysis · 12 August 2026

The Golden Cage

Dutch citizens locked in a cage of 74 to 90 percent tax burden, while foreign owners walk through untaxed

By Jacobus van Merksteijn

All figures are from Statistics Netherlands national accounts 2024 and OECD Revenue Statistics.

1. The cage and those who pass through it

For its own working citizens, the Netherlands is a golden cage. Anyone who lives, works and earns here pays between 74 and 90 percent of their actual income in taxes and compulsory contributions. Those who merely own assets here — the foreign shareholder who controls 25.6 percent of Dutch business — pass through the bars untaxed. Dividends flow through tax treaties to countries with low or zero withholding tax. Profits are statistically recorded as “Dutch national income”, but the money leaves the country.

For every euro of profit that an American or British shareholder takes from a Dutch subsidiary, they pay the Netherlands less tax than a Dutch nurse pays on one euro of wages.

2. Why the statistics conceal this: GDP versus GNI

Statistics Netherlands, the Ministry of Finance and the media consistently present the tax burden, government size and public debt as a percentage of GDP. According to Statistics Netherlands, GDP in 2024 was €1,122 billion. That denominator is misleadingly large. The income actually paid to workers (GNI under the Swiss method: gross wages plus the employer share of social contributions plus self-employed income) is €585 billion — 52.2% of GDP.

Two core observations. First, Statistics Netherlands records corporate profits (€425bn gross, much of which goes to foreign shareholders) in full as “Dutch national income”, even when the money leaves the country. Second, the statistics include €241bn of notional and accounting entries (depreciation, imputed rent and FISIM) in GDP, although nobody receives those items as income.

The officially presented tax burden of 38.5% of GDP equals 73.8% of the income actually paid to workers. Government expenditure of 44.4% of GDP is in fact 85.2% of GNI. For every euro Dutch workers earn, the government spends 85 cents — a substantial part financed by those same workers, while foreign owners take their share untaxed.

3.1 The congruence of taxation and expenditure

It is striking that a tax burden of 73.8% and government expenditure of 85.2%, both measured against GNI, lie very close to each other. That is not coincidence. It is systemic.

The gap between the two percentages:

  • Taxation as a share of GNI: 73.8%
  • Government expenditure as a share of GNI: 85.2%
  • Difference: 11.4 percentage points — the annual financing deficit measured against what workers actually produce.

Officially presented as “a 0.9% of GDP deficit — disciplined”, this becomes a 11.4% gap against GNI that must be financed by additional borrowing every year. For every euro the Dutch worker earns, the government spends 12 cents more than it levies from that worker. That difference is passed on, through public debt, to future Dutch workers.

The congruence shown by these two percentages is diagnostic. The tax burden relative to GNI (74%) and government size relative to GNI (85%) both fall in the high-70 to mid-80 percent range. This means that the government structurally claims almost all genuinely earned income of the working population — partly through direct taxation (74%) and partly through debt finance (11%). Together: 85%.

Put differently: the Dutch worker pays 74% of their wage to government, and the remaining 11% that government spends in addition will come from future levies on their children. The government de facto consumes 85% of the real wage bill.

That is exactly why the two percentages are so close: government expenditure determines — through borrowing capacity plus taxation — how much must be taken from GNI. Taxation is pushed as close to that expenditure as is politically feasible, with the gap covered by debt. The “low public debt of 43.8% of GDP” is in reality 84% of GNI and grows by 11.4 percentage points of GNI every year — unless taxation rises further or spending falls.

4. Pointing to others

The real asymmetry is compounded by an asymmetry of framing. Dutch politicians and publications consistently present:

  • “Tax burden: 38.5% of GDP” — which sounds moderately high by European standards.
  • “Government expenditure: 44% of GDP” — which sounds normal for the West.
  • “Public debt: 43.8% of GDP” — which sounds disciplined.

Countries such as the United States, Switzerland, Singapore and Ireland are then portrayed as countries that “raise too little tax” or as “tax havens”. Yet:

  • The real tax burden on wages paid out in the Netherlands is 74% (on a GNI basis).
  • The Netherlands itself, through the participation exemption, mailbox structures and hundreds of tax treaties, enables foreign shareholders to take profits out of the Netherlands virtually untaxed.
  • Dutch workers therefore de facto pay for the government that spares the foreign owners of their productive capacity.

This is not a neutral statistical fact. It is a misleading presentation of real relationships, with concrete political consequences: redistribution debates begin from false premises; tax reforms are judged against a GDP denominator that masks the real burden; and international criticism of the Netherlands as a conduit country is obscured by its self-presentation as having a “normal tax burden”.

5. A call for reframing

The figures in this document are not controversial. They come, one by one, from Statistics Netherlands, OECD Revenue Statistics and the Financial Annual Report of the State. What is controversial is the choice to present them always against GDP and never against what workers actually earn.

More press releases in this direction are needed. Not as a party-political attack, but as a correction to a statistical framing that renders the real inequality between resident workers and foreign owners invisible. Without this correction, political debate will continue to revolve around which group of domestic workers “must contribute still more”, while the largest untaxed flow of money — profit accruing to foreign owners — remains out of sight.

The Netherlands does not have a “low tax burden”. The Netherlands has one of the highest tax burdens in the world — for residents who work. For foreign owners it has one of the lowest. These two truths united in one country are what we call the golden cage.
