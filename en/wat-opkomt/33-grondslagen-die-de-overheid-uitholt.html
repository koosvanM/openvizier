The eleven holes in Dutch tax collection

Eleven policy areas, together 2.5% of GDP and 2.5% of GNP in annual net losses

Jacobus van Merksteijn

  • Author — Jacobus van Merksteijn
  • Date — 14 August 2026 · Calvià, Mallorca
  • Section — What is emerging · Taxation · The Netherlands
  • Core point — Enforcement costs more than there is left to collect
  • Theme — Policy areas with a negative net return and the system that keeps them in place

Eleven policy areas in which the Netherlands structurally loses more money than it brings in: around €27.8 billion a year in total, or 2.45% of gross domestic product (GDP) and 2.49% of gross national product (GNP). The anti-money-laundering approach alone accounts for eight billion in losses. And the same euro spent elsewhere produces five to ten times as much.

Note on the figures: all percentages below are given in parallel as proportions of gross domestic product (GDP, €1.134 bn in 2024) and of the adjusted domestic productive core (DPC, approximately €585 bn). GDP includes imputed rent, FISIM and profits flowing to foreign owners; DPC corrects for these and represents the actual domestic productive base against which each loss should be measured.

The eleven domains that destroy money

What follows is an inventory of eleven policy areas in which taxation is no longer a source of revenue diminished by leaks, but an expenditure that no longer recoups itself. For each domain, four sentences set out what it costs, what it yields, what the loss is, and which consequential losses arise that only become apparent over time.

The ranking below is based on three items per domain: revenue for the Treasury, the direct cost of enforcement, and the consequential damage caused by the policy itself (illegal substitution, de-risking, and crime generated by the policy). Amounts are in billions of euros per year, based on 2024 figures and, where possible, public sources (Court of Audit, Financial Annual Report of the State, CBS, WODC, Enexis, FIOD). Where no public figure is available, an estimate is given; these are conservative and separately marked in the table at the end.

1. Anti-money-laundering approach (Wwft) — net −€8.1 billion

Costs: in 2024, banks incurred €1.6 billion in direct Wwft compliance costs, involving 13,000 FTEs, plus approximately €3.4 billion in other gatekeeper costs among notaries, accountants, trust offices and estate agents, together amounting to around €5 billion in direct sector costs per year. Returns: in its March 2026 report Consequences substantial, returns unknown, the Court of Audit concluded that the return on this effort cannot be determined and that WODC rates its effectiveness as “very limited”; the return for the Treasury and for actual money-laundering prevention is therefore zero. Losses: the money-laundering flows themselves, estimated at €13 to €16 billion a year in the Netherlands, do not disappear but move to hawala, crypto mixers and underground banking. Consequential losses: banks exclude legitimate customers (charities, small businesses and customers with a migrant background) because the compliance cost per customer is up to €4,000 a year higher than the margin, SME financing deteriorates, and the underground economy is actively fuelled — this de-risking damage is conservatively estimated at €6.5 billion per year.

2. Undeclared work / forgone payroll tax — net −€4.1 billion

Costs: direct enforcement by SZW inspections, the Tax and Customs Administration and the Labour Inspectorate costs an estimated €0.1 billion per year in investigations, inspections and administrative processing. Returns: enforcement against undeclared work structurally generates virtually no collected payroll tax or contributions — most cases end in settlements or lapse for lack of evidence. Losses: according to CBS, around 400,000 people work undeclared each year in a circuit with turnover of approximately €4 billion, meaning that payroll tax and national-insurance contributions on this gross sum are entirely evaded. Consequential losses: the black market depresses formal wages in construction, hospitality, agriculture and horticulture, and parcel delivery, leaving the labour tax wedge politically untouchable; the pension gap among these 400,000 people will eventually become a collective call on social assistance.

3. VAT on illegal drugs — net −€2.0 billion

Costs: enforcement is carried out through the police, the prosecution service and Customs and is not budgeted separately; the share explicitly allocated to VAT collection is zero, since the law does not recognise a taxable event. Returns: the Court of Justice has ruled that the illegal import of narcotic drugs does not create a VAT liability, meaning that the return is €0 by definition, regardless of turnover. Losses: Dutch drug sales are estimated at €5 to €10 billion a year, which at a rate of 21% amounts to at least €1 to €2 billion in structurally forgone VAT. Consequential losses: the criminal money injected into the property market, hospitality and car trade by the absence of taxation pushes up prices for legitimate market participants and creates a parallel money-laundering entry channel that feeds directly back into the Wwft item.

4. VAT carousel fraud — net −€1.7 billion

Costs: FIOD and the Tax and Customs Administration together spend approximately €0.2 billion per year investigating and prosecuting intra-Community shell companies in electronics, cars and emissions allowances. Returns: recovery of unpaid VAT is fractional because the shell companies have already been stripped of assets when discovered and the perpetrators are often outside Dutch jurisdiction, making the direct return from enforcement virtually zero. Losses: the Tax and Customs Administration estimates the structural damage at €1 to €2 billion per year; individual cases can involve damage of €9 to €10 billion per investigation. Consequential losses: with every new legislative amendment, the fraud shifts to the next product category (currently emissions allowances and green certificates), while the reputational damage to the sectors concerned makes legitimate business in the same niche more difficult.

5. Corporate tax through underground banking — net −€1.05 billion

Costs: investigations are intertwined with Wwft efforts and have therefore largely already been counted under point 1; specific corporate-tax enforcement through hawala and crypto-mixer investigations costs approximately €0.05 billion a year in targeted FIOD capacity. Returns: recovered corporation tax from this structure is negligible because the construction is designed precisely to remain outside every tax return. Losses: the National Risk Assessment on Money Laundering 2023 identifies hawala and crypto mixers as structural channels; the forgone corporation tax is conservatively estimated at €1 billion per year. Consequential losses: this flow fuels criminal infiltration of SME takeovers (cash pushes legitimate bidders out of the market) and systematically erodes equal market conditions in hairdressing, hospitality and the motor trade.

6. E-commerce VAT under-declaration — net −€0.55 billion

Costs: Customs spends approximately €0.05 billion per year checking the €150 threshold for parcels from countries outside the EU. Returns: since 1 July 2026, Customs has levied €3 in import duty per e-commerce shipment, generating several hundred million gross, but this rule does not address VAT under-declaration itself. Losses: parcels from China are systematically declared at an artificially low customs value, depriving the state of 21% import VAT on at least €2.5 billion in actual import value — around €0.5 billion structurally forgone each year. Consequential losses: Dutch online retailers must charge the full rate of VAT and consequently lose market share, which in time further erodes regular VAT collection and employment in retail.

7. Undeclared crypto in Box 3 — net −€0.53 billion

Costs: the Tax and Customs Administration is investing in DAC8 and CARF cross-checks against crypto exchanges, at an estimated cost of €0.03 billion per year in data work and legal capacity. Returns: revenue from additional assessments and fines is only beginning to emerge and is currently still fractional — the first substantial payments will only become visible from 2026 onwards. Losses: the Tax and Customs Administration can impose fines of up to 300%, implying that undeclared crypto assets are considered substantial; forgone annual Box 3 tax is conservatively estimated at €0.5 billion. Consequential losses: the historic backlog of undeclared assets since 2017 continues to accumulate, while the shift towards crypto mixers as a concealment layer feeds back into the Wwft and corporation-tax items.

8. Illegal cigarette factories on Dutch soil — net −€0.33 billion

Costs: Combiteam Smoke (a joint Customs and FIOD operation) spends approximately €0.03 billion per year investigating domestic illegal cigarette production. Returns: tobacco duty actually recovered after a raid is negligible because the finished products have already been removed, and the factories are generally found abandoned by the time they are discovered. Losses: in 2024 and 2025, six factories were dismantled in Bunnik, Bladel, Stroe, Schaijk, Heerhugowaard and Herkenbosch, with seizures of up to 11 million cigarettes per site; forgone tobacco duty from domestic production is conservatively estimated at €0.3 billion per year. Consequential losses: regular tobacco-duty revenue came in €0.5 billion below forecast in 2024, partly because this domestic production, together with port imports and cross-border trade, is hollowing out the legal channel.

9. Payroll tax on platform work (bogus self-employment) — net −€0.32 billion

Costs: since the intensification of enforcement on 1 January 2025, the Tax and Customs Administration has spent approximately €0.02 billion per year on bogus-self-employment inspections at platforms and among clients. Returns: the first additional assessments are under way, but direct revenue was still nil in 2024 because enforcement only began in 2025. Losses: historically, Deliveroo, Uber and comparable platforms systematically avoided payroll tax and national-insurance contributions through self-employed arrangements that the Supreme Court ruled bogus in 2023; the loss is conservatively estimated at €0.3 billion per year. Consequential losses: within this arrangement, the platforms built up a competitive advantage over employers who did pay payroll tax, putting competing sectors under pressure; the pension provision of hundreds of thousands of couriers and drivers also shows a gap that will later have to be covered collectively.

10. Red-diesel fraud — net −€0.17 billion

Costs: FIOD and Customs together spend approximately €0.02 billion per year investigating the illegal use of red diesel in agriculture, shipping and transport. Returns: additional assessments following detection generate a few million euros — negligible compared with the scale of the problem. Losses: red diesel was abolished for most uses in 2013, but the €0.50-per-litre advantage remains attractive to farmers and transport operators near the border; structurally forgone duty is estimated at €0.15 billion per year. Consequential losses: legitimate fuel traders at the Belgian border lose turnover, contributing to the broader 6.5% decline in fuel sales in the Netherlands through August 2025.

11. Other minor items combined — net approximately −€0.5 billion

Costs: for the six other areas combined (Box 3 rental income, illegal medicines, waste exports, private-jet air tax, port imports of tobacco, and import duties on counterfeit goods), the joint direct enforcement effort is estimated at approximately €0.10 billion per year. Returns: together, these items generate approximately €0.10 billion per year through seizures, fines and additional assessments, meaning that enforcement just covers itself. Losses: the combined forgone tax (rent, VAT, excise duty and import duties) is conservatively estimated at €0.5 billion per year. Consequential losses: each item is too small to tackle politically on its own, but together they constitute a structural erosion of small tax bases that undermines tax morale more broadly.

What the bill shows in total

Taken together, these eleven domains cost between €20.8 and €24.8 billion per year in enforcement and forgone tax combined: 1.84–2.19% of 2024 gross domestic product (€1.134 billion) and 3.56–4.25% of the adjusted domestic productive core (DPC, ~€585 billion). On top of that comes approximately €14.95 billion in consequential damage to the wider economy — a total of €35.8–€39.8 billion per year, or 3.16–3.51% of GDP and 6.12–6.80% of DPC. This consequential damage is shown separately in a second chart because it is not a cost borne directly by the government, but a cost the policy induces in the wider economy.

Across these eleven domains, every euro of tax collected generates €2 to €8 in direct enforcement costs and indirect consequential damage. This is not a tax base with a leak, but an expenditure that no longer recoups itself.

What the second chart shows: consequential damage arises in each of the eleven domains. The Wwft item is the largest at €6.5 billion (de-risking of legitimate customers, exclusion of charities, small businesses and migrants by banks). VAT collection lost to illegal substitute markets and undeclared work adds €3.0 and €2.0 billion respectively. The estimates are drawn from WODC and AMLC estimates and allocated proportionally among the eleven domains.

The pattern is the same across all eleven: enforcement is politically untouchable (because it sends a signal), consequential damage is technically immeasurable (because there is no counter-figure), and the return is by definition zero (because the levy never directly reaches the illegal side). Citizens pay the cost, criminals win the arbitrage, and Parliament records it as decisive action.

And the press?

The press treats every new law as a news event without weighing the net outcome. A law that officially tackles the problem is reported as progress, even when it displaces or magnifies the problem. That is the error keeping the eleven domains above alive: not one of the eleven appears on the political agenda as a loss-making item, even though together they cost 1.8% of GDP each year.

The €20 billion per year that these eleven domains cost is almost twice the annual defence budget that the Cabinet is painstakingly assembling in 2026. It exceeds total central-government spending on primary education. It is available afresh every year, as soon as politicians dare to measure what each law actually delivers instead of what it promises.

Conclusion

Eleven policy areas, together 1.8% of GDP, evaporating every year in a system incapable of correcting itself because correction would threaten its own raison d’être. That is the bill: eleven concrete holes, each with a name, each with a minister responsible, each with a demonstrably negative balance. And each enforced by the same electoral-signal logic: announcing “stricter oversight” always wins more votes than announcing “we are stopping a system that does not work”.

The question is no longer whether this pattern really exists. The question is which of these eleven will be the first to be tackled by a political class that wants to win elections by naming the problem rather than guarding it.

It is conceivable that deploying these resources at another institution, such as the police, would produce a better return. This editorial makes no statement on that matter; it describes.
