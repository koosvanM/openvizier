The thought in the vault

European innovation cannot but withdraw — in defence, in order to survive

By Jacobus van Merksteijn

  • Author — Jacobus van Merksteijn
  • Date — 11 August 2026 · Calvià, Mallorca
  • Section — What is emerging · Philosophy
  • Core — Whoever builds outside Europe is not building an escape route but a vault
  • Theme — Safe-keeping of innovation capacity for those who will later ask for it

What a thought needs in order to live

An innovative thought is not a text and not a patent. It is a living constellation of four things: a carrier who understands it, an environment that allows it, means with which to test it, and time in which to let it ripen. Miss any one of the four and it evaporates within a generation. Miss two and it disappears within years.

Europe historically delivered all four. Universities kept carriers alive, guilds and workshops delivered the environment, prosperous cities and later the state delivered the means, and the unhurried tempo of European culture delivered the time. Out of that combination came the scientific revolution, the industrial revolution, and most of the basic innovations on which the modern world runs.

Each of those four has been dismantled step by step over the last half-century. Not at a stroke, not out of ill will, but through a slow shift in what the system steers by. The question is no longer whether the thought can still live here. The question is where it can live, and how it can get there without being stolen or punished.

The immune system of the established order

Every established structure develops an immune system against whatever endangers it. In a healthy civilisation that immune system is selective: it fends off real threats and lets useful change through. In an exhausted civilisation the immune system loses the distinction and starts attacking all change — precisely including the change that would save the civilisation itself.

Europe stands in that second stage. Anyone who attempts something new that touches the existing balance meets not a single opponent but a network of reactions that starts up on its own: bureaucratic requirements that must first be met before the innovation is allowed to begin, fiscal treatment that assumes the innovator is already making profit before the first revenue is in, media frames that immediately place success in a suspect context, political proposals that seek to tax the innovator in advance for consequences that still have to appear.

This is not a conspiracy. It is a system that has come to consider its own reproduction more important than its own purpose. The immune system no longer defends the civilisation, but its own existence. And it is precisely in that mode that it is no longer capable of distinguishing what is harmful from what is necessary.

Symptom management as a form of policy

The deeper cause of this immune reaction is that the European government no longer has instruments with which to address causes. Every apparatus once set up for deep intervention — industrial policy, education system, spatial planning, fiscal design freedom — has over recent decades been cut into powers distributed among Brussels, The Hague, provinces, municipalities, and dozens of independent regulators. What remains is symptom management: whenever something goes wrong somewhere, a rule is added, a subsidy set up, a committee installed.

Symptom management has one property that both explains and paralyses it: it demands no understanding of the whole. Every symptom can be tackled by the people who work on that symptom, without anyone ever asking where the symptom comes from. Whoever does ask that question meets the same immune reaction as the innovator: it is treated as an attack on the existing division of powers.

The result is that the system can only still react to things that fit inside its own language. A bio-based replacement for a petrochemical chain is not such a thing. A different model of governance is not such a thing. An education system that puts ripening before reproduction is not such a thing. These are changes at the level of causes, and the system is structurally unequipped to deal with them. It pushes them away — not out of ill will, but because they are not readable to it.

Why the government is not going to help

This is the point that is often made personal, but that is not personal. Whoever tries to set up in the Netherlands an innovation that touches the existing balances meets no opponent, but an absence. There is no one who wishes ill. There is also no one who is authorised to say yes. Every counter refers to the next, every policy paper asks for coordination with another policy paper, and by the time the counter is reached that could genuinely decide, the innovation is already past the point where the decision would still have mattered in substance.

The government experiences the innovator who does not wait for that as a fugitive. From the counter’s perspective this is correct: the question was not completed, the form not filled in, the committee not awaited. From the innovation’s perspective it is not correct, because the innovation cannot wait. It has its own tempo, which does not coincide with that of the bureaucracy.

Whoever has been through this knows that the government is genuinely unable to adjust itself to it. That is not a reproach. It is an observation that emerges from dozens of failed attempts at the same innovation dialogue. The government is not going to help, because helping requires precisely the authority no one still holds. And because the system has long since learned to eliminate every carrier of such an authority.

Where the intuition now comes from

Meanwhile, elsewhere, the thought is being carried. China and India at this moment deliver the people who used to arise in Europe: individuals who worked their way up under pressure, who kept the mammalian foundation intact in the first years of their lives, who know what a mistake costs and what responsibility means. America keeps itself standing precisely by attracting those people and giving them room, supplemented by a residual hardness of its own that is kept in place by its labour market.

Europe hardly still produces such people itself, and no longer attracts them either. Whoever has forced his way up elsewhere rarely chooses a continent where success is suspect and protection expensive. Whoever was already born here and has retained the intuition discovers at some point that he is part of a dying species and that the institutional environment no longer recognises its own kind.

There do not need to be many. Innovation has never been a matter of numbers, always of extremes. A handful of carriers is enough to move a technology, a sector, a civilisation a generation forward. Europe now lacks them in most strategic domains. The few who remain can choose: dissolve into the systematic dismantling, or arrange their work in such a way that it survives.

What a vault is

The metaphor of the vault is precise. A vault is not a space of flight. It is a place where something valuable is kept because the environment to which it belongs is temporarily unsafe for it. What lies in the vault does not belong to the vault-holder. It belongs to whoever asks for it later. The vault-holder is the keeper, not the owner.

When a European thinker today builds structures outside Europe — a production site in Mauritania, a remediation enterprise on Malta, a governance pilot on Sark, a cultivation system in a non-European jurisdiction — this is not a shifting of wealth and not tax optimisation. It is a vault. What lies inside is not money, but a thought, together with the infrastructure needed to keep that thought alive across several decades.

The vault has four properties that distinguish it from flight. It stands in a jurisdiction where the thought can develop without being immediately absorbed by immune reactions. It is connected to a productive activity that delivers real value to the environment where it stands. It is transparent about what lies inside and why, without the carrier having to defend it against every passer-by. And it is retrievable: what lies inside can later, when the time is ripe, be deployed, shared, or introduced into an environment that then asks for it.

What lies in the vault

Concretely it concerns things that stay alive only if they are kept in operation. Knowledge of large-scale biomass processing evaporates within ten years if no one does it any more. A governance model that works at the scale of a community can only be preserved by running it in a real community. An economic measurement methodology such as a corrected GDP or an index for domestic productive ownership becomes reliable only when it is applied to real figures and tested against them. Paper alone is not preservation.

This means that the vault is not an archive but a working installation. A cultivation that keeps growing. A remediation project that keeps running. A pilot community that keeps making its own choices. A publishing house that keeps publishing. Each of these keeps alive a specific piece of knowledge that would be lost the moment the activity stopped.

The choice of jurisdictions outside the European Union — or, in the case of Sark, outside the EU but within the British association — is functional. It allows the activity to actually work as intended, without every single operation first having to pass through a controlling system that does not understand the operation.

Why this is not flight

The difference between flight and safe-keeping lies in what happens to the yield. Whoever flees takes with him what he can and leaves behind what he cannot take. Whoever keeps takes with him what can survive elsewhere, with the task of keeping it available for the place he came from.

That difference is not visible from the outside. To the counter at which an innovator has to report, it looks the same: activity that no longer takes place in the Netherlands, capital no longer taxed here, initiative no longer deployed here. The counter cannot see the difference. Only time can see it.

The question, therefore, is not whether the counter understands it. The counter is not going to understand it, and that is not a problem. The question is whether the thought survives, and whether it is available when someone in two, three or five decades asks: what should have been done, and how was that actually built. At that moment there lies in the vault an enacted answer, not a book. That is the difference.

What the carrier does, and what he no longer tries

The carrier of a thought in this phase does three things at once. He builds and maintains the vault. He documents what lies inside, in a form that will remain intelligible after he himself is no longer available. And he keeps a connection with the place from which the thought originally came — not in order to be accepted there again, but in order to remain findable for the few who recognise the pattern.

What he no longer does is try to change the established structures. Not because he has given up on them, but because he has learned that change from within does not work with structures whose immune system has set itself precisely against change. The energy that went there now goes into the vault and into the documentation. That is not cynical. It is an economic choice of time and attention.

What he also does not do is expect his position to be acknowledged during his lifetime. Recognition comes later or does not come, and that makes no difference to the work. The work is the safe-keeping, not the reputation.

The opening in time

Civilisations that have died of their own immune system pass through a phase of dismantling, followed by a phase of renewed demand. Rome disappeared and was partly reconstructed centuries later by people who did not come from Rome. China went through several cycles of collapse and rebuilding, and each time reached back to what had been kept in safe-keeping elsewhere or reinvented there. What seemed lost turned out later to be available, because someone had stored it outside the collapsing structure.

Europe is probably entering a comparable phase. Not concluded in thirty years, perhaps longer, with intermediate periods in which it looks as if things are improving. But the straight line points one way, and the coming decades will show whether anyone has kept in time what later has to come back. Whoever then has a working installation instead of an archive, whoever then has a community that has lived a governance model instead of describing it, whoever then has a production chain that functions instead of being conceived — that person has something to contribute to the reopening.

Whether the keeper himself sees this reopening is uncertain. The likely outcome is that the vault stands longer than the keeper. That is not tragic, that is the nature of this kind of work. Those who built cathedrals in the twelfth century knew that the roof would be placed by their grandson. Whoever now builds a vault for a thought must reckon with the fact that its user is not yet born.

What government, media and public could understand from this

From the government nothing is asked. It is not going to help, and that is fine. What would help is that the reflex of framing the keeper as a fugitive does not automatically kick in the moment someone sets up structures outside Europe. Every fugitive-frame destroys a vault. Every time someone who is building for the long term is publicly cast as an opportunist, the chance of preservation grows smaller.

From the media it is asked that they distinguish between shifting assets to become richer oneself, and shifting work in order to let it survive. That distinction is not always easy to see, but there are factual indicators: what kind of activity is undertaken in the foreign jurisdiction, what real production or service comes out of it, what community benefits from it, and what is returned to the original environment in the form of knowledge, publications, or retrievable structures.

From the public nothing is asked, except perhaps this one thing: remember that the people you are now favouring by crowning them as perpetrators are the same kind of people as those who favoured your ancestors two generations ago by building things that later became common property. There is a pattern, and it repeats. Whoever can now see through it need not do so loudly. It is enough that, when the time comes and the vault opens, one is able to recognise what then comes out of it.

Closing — the thought in the vault

“The thought in the vault does not belong to the vault-holder. It has been placed in safe-keeping with the one who can carry it at this moment, with the task of passing it on to the one who will be able to use it later. Whoever builds a vault does so not because he does not trust Europe, but because he does not trust the times. The vault stands open to whoever asks for it later. Until then it stays closed, and the keeper keeps working.”
