The incentive we switch off for one group

Why companies leave, migrants arrive, and our own underclass remains in place.

Jacobus van Merksteijn

The figures for the second quarter of 2026 show an economy in which the private productive core is shrinking and government consumption is carrying growth. The previous article analysed this composition. The question that follows is not economic but anthropological: why do some groups adapt under pressure, while others do not?

The answer is simpler than the national debate suggests. The incentive to move towards where labour or capital can earn something is universal. It works for factories, it works for migrants, and it works for the self-employed. It works wherever we do not explicitly switch it off.

The same driving force, three movements

On a global scale, that incentive produces three movements that are often discussed separately but have the same engine.

The first movement is companies leaving the country. Shell moved its headquarters to London in 2022, Unilever had done so earlier, and DSM-Firmenich chose Switzerland. ASML openly threatened to expand elsewhere because of the tax and migration climate. At Chemelot, OCI and Nobian scaled back or relocated parts of their production to the United States and the Middle East, because energy prices and nitrogen capacity allow production there when they do not here. These are not ideological choices. They are cash-flow choices. Where the calculation falls below zero, money moves to where it rises above zero again.

The second movement is Africans migrating to Europe. Exactly the same mechanism, in the opposite direction. Anyone in Mali, Senegal or Nigeria who cannot earn a decent living from their work moves to where they can. The journey through the Sahel and across the Mediterranean is life-threatening. The fact that people nevertheless make that journey demonstrates how powerful the incentive is. It is the same movement as a factory relocating to Texas, only with far higher transaction costs and much less legal protection.

The third movement is that of Dutch benefit recipients. And here we see the opposite: no movement. Not because there is no better place to go, but because the difference between moving and not moving has been smoothed away. Those who do nothing lose nothing. Those who do move gain only marginally. The group that could still make the move does not make it.

What the comparison demonstrates

Taken together, the three groups show that the incentive is neutral. It works in Africa, it works for multinationals, and it works for the self-employed and migrants who do take action. It is not a moral judgement about laziness or diligence. It is a mechanical phenomenon: where loss bites, movement follows.

The asymmetry does not lie in the people. It lies in policy. For companies, we allow the loss to be felt in full — so they relocate or innovate. For African migrants, we allow the loss to be felt in full — so they come, with all the problems this creates for countries of origin and destination. For our own residents at the bottom of the income distribution, we dampen the loss so thoroughly that movement stops. Then policymakers and commentators express surprise that the first group leaves, the second group arrives, and the third group remains where it is. Yet all three are doing exactly what the mechanism predicts.

What happened to that third group

In social assistance, unemployment benefit and incapacity benefit, the feedback loop has been systematically severed. The Participation Act of 2015 was intended to strengthen activation. The evaluation by the SCP and CPB in 2019 concluded that the number of people moving from social assistance into work had not increased significantly, while the influx of young people with disabilities into social assistance had risen sharply. The obligation to apply for jobs and the community contribution formally exist, but enforcement has largely disappeared. Research by Divosa shows that a large proportion of municipalities implement the community contribution rarely or not at all. The message received by the recipient is: nothing happens if you do nothing.

For unemployment benefit, the duration has been gradually reduced since 2016 to a maximum of 24 months, and its accrual has been curtailed. On paper, this is a tightening of the incentive. But the labour market is so tight — unemployment is around 3.9 to 4.2 per cent — that anyone who wants to work finds a job almost immediately, while anyone who does not simply serves out the remaining months. The incentive is therefore never tested — the market absorbs both sides.

In incapacity benefit, the trend is most pronounced. According to the UWV, WIA inflow in 2025 was the highest since its introduction in 2006, with strong growth among those over 55 and in psychological diagnoses. In June 2026, the CPB warned that the system would become financially unsustainable without intervention. The UWV’s assessment capacity is so overloaded that people wait years for an examination. During that waiting period there is no reintegration incentive: the status is undecided, but the benefit continues to be paid.

The bill appears on the other side of the balance sheet

Part of the 2.5 per cent growth in government consumption in Q2 2026 — the engine behind the ‘worst-case scenarios have failed to materialise’ figure — consists precisely of the benefits, healthcare spending and public-sector wages that keep the third group in place. The public sector thereby acts as a receptacle for labour that the private market does not want or cannot afford. This is not a criticism of the people receiving that benefit. It is a description of a system that, through consistent damping, has severed the adjustment loop and then adds those costs to the government spending that pushes GDP upwards.

We protect our own people from precisely the pressure the rest of the world uses to move forward, and then import people who have felt that pressure. In net terms, adaptability moves outwards and passivity moves inwards.

What an honest calculation reveals

Companies that find no answer to loss relocate or close. Migrants who have no opportunity to earn a living move themselves. Benefit recipients who feel no loss do not move. All three movements follow the same rule. Only the third is a policy choice, and that choice does not appear in the newspaper headline. It appears in the government consumption item that keeps GDP above zero.

An economy that allows its productive core to shrink, sees its most entrepreneurial companies leave, sees inflow into incapacity benefit rise to record levels, and absorbs all of this by allowing government consumption to grow by 2.5 per cent is not an economy that has ‘escaped the worst-case scenarios’. It is an economy that has switched off the adjustment signal for its own population and records the resulting bill on the expenditure side. The GDP figure conceals precisely the dynamics that drive it.
