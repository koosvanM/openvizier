The complete shift from prototype to commercial maturity — a learning rate of 18 per cent per doubling of production volume — pushes the SOFC LCOE below the Spanish grid price around 2030, without a single euro cent of subsidy. From 2036, the Carbon-Alert hub produces electricity for less than half the grid price. SDE++ could accelerate this. But it is not a prerequisite.

1. Why ethanol — and why now

Three converging developments make bioethanol in 2026 suddenly the most pragmatic energy carrier for decentralised electricity and heat.

One. Since 2026, Nissan has been demonstrating 70 per cent electrical efficiency from an ethanol SOFC in Tochigi — a figure that exceeds fossil-fuel turbines (50–55%) and even combined gas/steam cycles (60%).

Two. Hydrogen as a fuel has commercially collapsed. Bosch halted its SOFC division. Stellantis stopped its entire H₂ programme. Pump prices of €13–19 per kilogram remain structurally unaffordable.

Three. The actual production cost of bioethanol is falling through improved fermentation, BECCS integration and pellet feedstocks to €0,30 per litre in 2036 — without any subsidy. Not an assumption. The learning rate of a centuries-old technology now reaching industrial scale.

1.1 The actual ethanol price curve, without subsidies

The curve shows the actual production cost from the pellet plant to the distillation tower. No government support, no tradable credits — just scale, improved catalysis and lower energy input per litre. €0,55 today. €0,45 in 2030. €0,30 in 2036. From that point, ethanol wins every comparison: per kWh, per kilometre, per MWh of heat.

The black line is the Carbon-Alert SOFC without a single support mechanism. In 2026, it is still twice as expensive as the grid (38 versus 16 c€/kWh). By 2030 it is already cheaper. 3. Architecture — the five building blocks

The hub is not a standalone fuel cell. It is an integrated system in which the Carbon-Alert BiCRS chain (Biomass with Carbon Removal and Storage) and the SOFC unit reinforce each other. Ethanol is produced on site or regionally. The SOFC converts it into electricity, heat and pure CO₂. That CO₂ can be stored or marketed. Nothing in this design relies on public support — the hub operates commercially with €0,30 per litre of feedstock and €1.800 per kilowatt of CAPEX.

3.1 Footprint and flexibility

  • Footprint: 20 m² SOFC + 8 m² balance of plant + 30 m² distillation = 58 m² total
  • Modularity: scalable from 25 kW (housing cluster) to 5 MW (industry or data centre) using the same stack unit
  • Fuel storage: a 2.000-litre weekly tank is sufficient for full autonomy during a grid outage
  • Noise: < 45 dB at one metre — no moving parts, only blower and pump
  • Maintenance: stack replacement every 7–10 years, OPEX 1,2–1,5% of CAPEX per year

5. Why 2036 — and not 2030 or 2045

2030 is too early for electricity to be below the grid price without an unexpectedly rapid fall in CAPEX. Break-even has been reached, but the margin is thin.

2045 is too late. By then, stranded assets in hydrogen infrastructure, battery factories and large-scale grid planning will already have been created and politically locked in.

2036 is the optimum. By then, SOFCs will be at €1.800 per kilowatt (mass production by Doosan, Weichai, Bloom). Ethanol will be at €0,30 per litre. The Spanish grid price will by then be 21,5 c€/kWh due to scarcity and grid investment. At that point, Carbon-Alert will already have been above break-even for five years — no longer marginal, but dominant.

5.1 Strategic sequencing

  • 2026–2028: 25 kW pilot at one site, validation of 70%+ efficiency, financing through private channels
  • 2028–2031: first commercial deployment of 100 kW, break-even reached around 2030 — without subsidy
  • 2031–2036: scaling to 500+ hubs, regional BiCRS chain established, data-centre and industrial contracts
  • 2036–2040: dominance in the Mediterranean mid-load market, export model to Latin America and Africa

Carbon-Alert's structural advantage is precisely that the design is not dependent on the whims of politicians and their subsidy schemes. When an SDE++ scheme collapses, the business plan built on it collapses too. Carbon-Alert's break-even point does not change. Our business case rests on market fundamentals, not public goodwill.

7. Conclusion — what is factually on the table

A 100 kW Carbon-Alert energy hub will deliver electricity in 2036 for 9,97 c€/kWh — less than half the Spanish grid price (21,5 c€/kWh) — with €180.000 CAPEX and a payback period of approximately three years.

All figures are calculated using the actual market price of ethanol (€0,30/litre) without any government support, SDE++, ETS credit or feed-in tariff.

The technology is proven (Nissan 70%, Ceres/Doosan/Weichai 50 MW). The learning curve is documentable (18% per doubling). The competitive landscape is open (Bosch and Stellantis have left H₂).

Carbon-Alert Ltd is technically ready. Economically substantiated. Politically independent. What is missing is implementation capital — not vision. The next three years will determine whether we become the Mediterranean and global leader in decentralised electricity, or watch Doosan and Weichai take our learning curve. The window is open — now.
