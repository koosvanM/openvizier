Consequences Map IV · Netherlands · June 2026

The nature-adapted analysis

What happens to the Dutch cascade when two equatorial biomass pathways replace Brussels climate policy — and how hydrogen compares with both

By Jacobus van Merksteijn · Malta, June 2026

This piece is not an attack. Not a call to action. Not an attempt to persuade you.

It sets out three technological pathways and calculates what they do to the Dutch cascade. BiCRS (a product of Carbon-Alert Ltd) and ethanol as equatorial alternatives, hydrogen as the reference pathway from the official climate plans.

The Silent Analysis III showed what six Dutch scenarios lose under the current Brussels course. This piece shows what those same six scenarios regain when Brussels chooses a different course. What you do with it is your concern.

Three pathways, three analyses

BiCRS — injecting biomass beneath the roots of the crop that produced it — removes carbon permanently from the atmosphere. It is the replacement for the Green Deal and CBAM. Production in the equatorial belt, cost €22–28 per tonne of CO₂, model price €40 per tonne.

Ethanol — fermenting a different equatorial biomass fraction into liquid fuel — replaces natural gas, petrol, diesel and electricity consumption. Production in large factories on site in the partner country, shipment to European ports. Production costs €0.20–0.30 per litre, pump price (excise-efficient) €0.55–0.75/litre. Applied in a micro-CHP unit — a combined heat and power system that produces heat and electricity simultaneously — the ethanol pathway supplies not only the heat for the home but also 170 to 260 per cent of its own electricity consumption, with the surplus fed back into the grid.

Both pathways are equatorial because the plant that provides these yields grows only above 6 degrees Celsius. The two pathways operate separately: a hectare is either a BiCRS hectare with in-situ injection or an ethanol hectare with transport to a factory. What they have in common is the same partner countries, the same geopolitical logic and the same post-Brussels climate philosophy.

This piece examines the three pathways separately. First, the cascade impact of BiCRS implementation. Then, the direct impact of the ethanol transition on household finances. Finally — as a reference point — the calculation for the hydrogen pathway that features prominently in the current Dutch climate plans. We do not add them up into a single figure — they are three independent trajectories, each standing on its own.

First analysis — BiCRS impact on the cascade

“The party you choose harms you precisely to the extent of its pressure index — unless Brussels chooses a different climate policy.”

— the implicit logic of the cascade

The Silent Analysis III calculated the third-order cascade for six Dutch scenarios under nine parties plus a meritocratic reference model. The outcome was confronting: PRO/GreenLeft–Labour produced deeply negative figures for virtually every scenario, not through direct taxation but through the cascade set in motion by its pressure index.

BiCRS implementation changes how the cascade operates. Not by changing the parties’ programmes — those remain what they are — but by removing the heaviest climate costs from the cascade. The Green Deal and CBAM together represent approximately forty per cent of the Dutch climate burden in 2030. When they are replaced by BiCRS at €40/tonne, that burden disappears and the space released flows back to households and businesses.

The effect is independent of party. Whichever coalition governs, BiCRS implementation gives something back in every scenario. But the scale of the effect differs by party — parties with a high pressure index cause more cascade damage, and BiCRS therefore offsets more of it.

The seismic shift in one image

Three observations emerge from this graph:

One — BiCRS implementation provides the greatest compensation under parties with the highest pressure index. GreenLeft–Labour improves from an average of −56% to −27%. D66 improves from −35% to −15%. This is no coincidence; parties with a high pressure index cause more climate damage, and BiCRS removes precisely that damage.

Two — for GreenLeft–Labour, the average cascade outcome remains negative, even with BiCRS. The shift is substantial, but not large enough to neutralise the entire left-wing cascade. Wealth tax, Box 2 taxation and capital-flight effects are not addressed by BiCRS — only the climate component disappears. What remains is the structural damage caused by any ideology that penalises the productive base.

Three — under VVD, PVV, BBB, JA21 and FvD, the average cascade becomes positive with BiCRS. What still caused modest harm under the current Brussels course turns into a modest gain. For a PVV voter, this means not merely a different climate policy — it means a coalition that is, in practical terms, no longer harmful to the average household budget.

The Dutch matrix — six scenarios, ten parties, BiCRS difference column

The BiCRS difference column is consistently green — for all six scenarios. Its size varies from five per cent (Mark & Lisa, average-income family) to twenty per cent (Sandra, on benefits). The greatest relative benefit goes to those on the lowest incomes, because the absolute climate cost represented a larger share of their budget.

For those who have read The Silent Analysis III: you will recognise the figures in the left-hand columns — the same deep red figures under GreenLeft–Labour and D66, the same less severe damage under the centrist parties, the same green Nova Democratia column on the right. The BiCRS column is new, and it changes the entire picture.

By scenario — six stories with figures

Anna (70, retired) sees her loss under GreenLeft–Labour fall from €5,103 a year to +€2,697 — a shift of fifteen thousand euros over four years.

Jacobus (58, owner-director) remains in the red under GreenLeft–Labour — moving from −€22,591 to −€11,791. BiCRS halves the damage but does not resolve every problem. Under VVD, he moves from −€3,079 to +€701: from a modest loss to a modest gain.

Mark & Lisa (35, average income) move from −€13,642 to −€4,642 under GreenLeft–Labour. Under VVD: from −€2,326 to +€824. Under PVV: from −€250 to +€2,600 — a genuinely tangible improvement for the average-income family.

Tom (45, receiving unemployment benefit) loses €49,056 a year in the cascade under GreenLeft–Labour — more than his annual income. BiCRS reduces this to a loss of €40,656 — still devastating. Tom belongs to the group for whom even the most far-reaching climate reform offers no salvation. His unemployment stems from industrial relocation, for which climate policy bears only partial responsibility.

Sandra (38, receiving social assistance) loses €9,618 a year under GreenLeft–Labour — almost half her income. BiCRS brings that down to zero. Under PVV, she moves from +€344 to +€3,384 — three per cent more purchasing power for someone who spends 90 per cent of her income on fixed costs.

Jasper (22, school-leaver) loses €24,739 a year under GreenLeft–Labour — almost his entire annual income. BiCRS brings this down to €12,739. Under Nova Democratia: from +€2,500 to +€5,500. For the generation that will bear the cascade for the longest, BiCRS is the first serious improvement produced by a Brussels reform.

What the figures say together

BiCRS implementation is not a miracle. It does not resolve all the damage that left-wing ideology inflicts on the Dutch cascade. In scenarios involving deep structural damage — Tom the unemployed worker, Jacobus the owner-director under heavy redistribution — the loss remains considerable.

But it fundamentally changes the picture for most citizens. Under centre-right coalitions that currently cause modest harm, the outcome turns into a modest gain. Under left-wing coalitions, the damage is halved. For the lowest-income groups, the relative benefit is greatest.

This is not an argument against ideology. It is a numerical observation: forty per cent of the Dutch cascade damage in 2030 comes from the Green Deal and CBAM. When Brussels replaces those mechanisms with an instrument that achieves the same climate objective at one-sixth of the cost, the average household regains forty per cent of the space it lost. Regardless of which party it chooses in 2029.

Second analysis — the ethanol transition

“A litre of ethanol at the pump costs thirty per cent of a litre of petrol, and heats a home for half the cost of natural gas.”

— the direct observation at the household budget

Alongside BiCRS, there is a second equatorial biomass application: bioethanol production through fermentation. This is an entirely separate pathway — different hectares, different operations and a different value chain. The biomass is harvested, transported to a large fermentation and distillation plant on site in the equatorial producer country, converted into ethanol and shipped by tanker to European ports.

The production cost of this bioethanol is €0.20–0.30 per litre at the factory gate. After shipping, margins and an excise regime that recognises this as a climate-positive fuel, the pump or delivery price is between €0.55 and €0.75 per litre. By comparison: the current European petrol price is around €1.78/litre, natural gas €1.42/m³, and electricity €0.34/kWh.

The micro-CHP — key to decentralised energy provision

The device that sets the ethanol pathway apart from all other energy choices is the micro-CHP: a small ethanol-powered combined heat and power unit, the size of a central-heating boiler, which simultaneously produces heat and electricity. Investment: around €4,500, comparable to a new gas-fired central-heating boiler. Written off over fifteen years.

The operating principle is that of a conventional CHP unit — widely used in greenhouse horticulture — but on a household scale. Ethanol is burned in a compact internal-combustion engine that drives a generator (electricity production), while the engine's waste heat heats the home. Overall efficiency of the ethanol burned: approximately 90 per cent. Proportion: 60 per cent heat, 30 per cent electricity, 10 per cent losses.

The crucial feature is what the electricity output does in relation to the household's own consumption. A CHP unit sized to the heating demand of a Dutch household automatically produces so much electricity that it comfortably exceeds its own consumption. In each of the six scenarios examined, production ranges from 170 to 260 per cent of household consumption.

Three observations from this graph:

One — Anna reaches 261 per cent. Her CHP unit runs for long periods in winter to provide the equivalent of 1,400 m³ of gas for heating, producing 6,261 kWh of electricity against her own consumption of 2,400 kWh. Surplus: 3,861 kWh fed back into the grid each year. A pensioner becomes a net electricity supplier.

Two — Jacobus the managing director, with the portfolio's greatest heating demand, supplies a surplus of 5,192 kWh. That is enough electricity for two neighbouring households. One CHP unit in every street could create a complete energy correction.

Three — even Jasper, the school-leaver with minimal consumption (the equivalent of 700 m³ of gas and 1,800 kWh of electricity), reaches 174 per cent: he still produces a surplus of 1,331 kWh. No scenario in the portfolio is 'undersized' for household electricity consumption.

Grid congestion reduced to zero — the system effect

The electricity production of the micro-CHP has implications extending far beyond the household itself. The Dutch electricity grid is currently being reinforced to accommodate two growing sources of load: heat pumps (an evening winter peak, when everyone comes home and the heat pump switches on) and electric cars (an evening charging peak). Together, they quadruple peak demand per household from approximately 3 kW (current) to around 12 kW — hence the €220 per household per year for grid expansion in the hidden-cost table for wind and solar.

The CHP reverses this logic. Not only is the additional load from heat pumps and EVs avoided — the home itself begins supplying electricity to the grid on winter evenings. Precisely during the hours when the government pathway causes the greatest peak demand, the CHP pathway provides the greatest peak supply. The result for the Dutch electricity grid is not merely that reinforcement is unnecessary — it even gains headroom through decentralised generation.

Four practical consequences:

One — no grid reinforcement is needed. The €220 per household per year for TenneT and Liander grid expansion between 2026 and 2035 disappears. At national level: €1.8 billion per year from the Climate Fund that would otherwise have had to be spent.

Two — no grid-scale batteries are needed. The CHP unit is itself a kind of 'chemical battery' — the ethanol tank at the home contains several thousand litres that can be deployed as required. Wind and solar require grid-scale batteries to compensate for their intermittency; ethanol CHP does not.

Three — no reserve gas-fired power stations are needed. Decentralised CHP installations provide continuous, controllable output. During calm conditions: the CHP continues to operate normally. During peak demand: the CHP operates with surplus capacity. There is no need for centralised peaking capacity.

Four — surplus energy without grid reinforcement. A network of 8 million CHP units in the Netherlands would together produce 35–50 terawatt-hours of surplus per year — approximately one-third of Dutch electricity consumption. This surplus could be used in energy-intensive industrial processes, or supplied via interconnectors to Germany, Belgium and the United Kingdom. Without a single metre of additional high-voltage cable.

Mobility via ethanol

In addition to heat and electricity, ethanol also serves as a fuel for road transport. Modern cars run on E85 without modifications; older models can be converted for a few hundred euros. This replaces petrol and diesel on a one-to-one basis. No investment in electric vehicles is needed, no additional electricity-grid load, and no lithium-ion battery supply chain with its associated strategic dependence on Chinese production.

From a climate perspective, this is a closed short-term carbon cycle: the plant absorbs CO₂ as it grows, and combustion in the engine releases the CO₂ again. Net zero over the cycle, comparable to the economics of wood-burning stoves but on an industrial scale. Not as good as BiCRS (which permanently removes atmospheric carbon), but as good as wind-and-solar electrification and at considerably lower cost.

The total energy bill — what a household pays per year

Three observations from the comparison:

One — the government pathway (heat pump plus EV plus wind/solar infrastructure) is more expensive for every household than the current situation. This is counter-intuitive — the government presents this transition as cost-neutral or even cheaper — but when all hidden costs are included, an average household pays approximately €1,000 more per year for energy. For Anna (€2,700 → €5,025) and Sandra (€1,824 → €4,333), the increase is most dramatic.

Two — the ethanol-CHP pathway is cheaper for every household than the current situation and considerably cheaper than the government pathway. Jacobus currently pays €7,021 (gas, petrol and electricity combined), would pay €8,094 under the government pathway (heat pump, EV, plus additional electricity purchases), and pays €3,870 under the CHP pathway — a halving. For Mark & Lisa: €6,318 currently, €7,528 under the government pathway, €3,337 under CHP.

Three — the difference between the government pathway and the CHP pathway varies from €3,500 to €4,200 per household per year. This is on top of the hidden wind and solar costs of €1,135 per household incorporated into the government pathway. For lower-income groups, this represents a larger share of their budget; for middle-class families, it remains a substantial sum.

The hidden costs of wind and solar

The government pathway appears advantageous at first sight: the electricity price is stable, billions are provided in subsidies, and the 'green narrative' is dominant. But when you calculate what is actually being paid — directly or indirectly — an amount rarely shown in the annual accounts emerges.

Five of these items have been known for years to those who have looked into the matter: the SDE++ subsidies embedded in the electricity price, the grid expansions by TenneT and regional network operators, the grid-scale batteries needed to manage intermittency, the reserve gas-fired power stations that CANNOT be decommissioned as long as wind and solar provide the bulk of supply, and the fifteen-year depreciation cycle for turbines and panels.

The sixth item is rarely discussed: recycling and decommissioning. Turbine blades are made of composite materials (glass fibre, carbon fibre and epoxy resin) that are currently not economically recyclable — worldwide, 85 per cent of decommissioned blades end up in landfill. Solar panels contain silicon, silver, EVA film and traces of heavy metals — recycling requires energy-intensive thermal processing. Lithium-ion grid batteries require the separation of cobalt, lithium and nickel. None of these supply chains is mature.

Dutch policy has never included a reserve for these decommissioning and recycling costs. When the first generation of wind farms (around 2030–2040) and the first generation of solar farms (around 2035–2045) reach the end of their service lives, that bill will still have to be paid. Estimated per household per year, spread over this cycle: €145.

The ethanol pathway does not carry a comparable hidden cost structure. The micro-CHP unit is a simple device that can be recycled using conventional steel-processing methods. Ethanol tanks are standard liquid-fuel tanks. No special grid extensions are required — the existing filling-station network can dispense ethanol directly; the same pipelines that currently carry petrol could carry ethanol tomorrow. No battery infrastructure, no reserve capacity, no deferred waste stream.

For the six scenarios:

Anna (pensioner): +16.9% vs government pathway

Jacobus (owner-director): +5.0% vs government pathway

Mark & Lisa (average income): +5.1% vs government pathway

Tom (unemployed): +10.5% vs government pathway

Sandra (on social assistance): +17.1% vs government pathway

Jasper (school-leaver): +12.6% vs government pathway

The pattern repeats what was visible in the BiCRS analysis: the greatest relative benefit goes to the lowest-income groups. Sandra, with an income of €21,000, retains more than €3,500 a year that she would lose under the government pathway — two months' income. Anna, with her pension, gains an additional €3,600 in financial breathing space. For both, a substantial part of that benefit comes from selling surplus electricity generated by their CHP unit: they become energy suppliers rather than energy consumers.

What the figures say together

The transition to ethanol is not an addition to the implementation of BiCRS — it is a second, independent reform. But it shares the same origin: equatorial biomass, partner countries in the Congo Basin, the Indonesian archipelago and the fringes of the Brazilian Amazon. What it introduces differently:

BiCRS is a climate-policy reform — the Green Deal and CBAM removed, with in-situ biomass injection in their place. Its effect on household finances comes through the cascade (industrial remigration, a falling ETS price and the disappearance of CBAM administration).

Ethanol is an energy-system reform — natural gas and petrol removed, with equatorial ethanol in their place. Its effect on household finances is direct (lower heating bills, lower fuel costs and no need for a heat-pump investment).

Together, they represent two independent improvements that can be implemented. One requires Brussels to abolish the Green Deal and CBAM — politically difficult but achievable within a single Commission term. The other requires only that the European excise regime recognise that equatorial bioethanol is not technically a fossil fuel in climate terms and should therefore be treated differently. No Treaty amendment, no ECB decision — only an excise regulation adopted by qualified majority.

Third analysis — hydrogen as a reference pathway

“The official plans rely on hydrogen for heating, freight transport and peak power. No one has clearly set out the total cost this will impose on each household.”

— the implicit gap in the Climate Agreement

The National Hydrogen Programme and the Climate Agreement designate hydrogen as a climate pillar in several areas: industrial process heat, heavy-duty transport, domestic heating through hybrid hydrogen boilers in pilot neighbourhoods, and peak-power provision through hydrogen power stations. The figures communicated by the government usually concern total investment amounts at national level, and rarely the annual cost per household.

This chapter calculates that cost — in exactly the same way as the ethanol chapter does. Six household types, three cost components: heating via a hydrogen central-heating system, mobility via a fuel-cell car, and the hidden infrastructure costs concealed in electricity prices and general taxation.

The price point of green hydrogen

According to IRENA, production costs for green hydrogen through electrolysis are expected to lie between four and eight euros per kilogram in 2030, depending on electricity prices, economies of scale and electrolysis technology. At the meter — after transport, storage, distribution and margins — BloombergNEF expects €10–15 per kilogram. This document uses the midpoint of €12/kg as a realistic 2030 delivered price.

Green hydrogen production in 2030 (IRENA): €4–8/kg

Delivered price at the meter in 2030 (BNEF): €10–15/kg

Model assumption in this article: €12/kg

Equivalent for replacing natural gas: €3.66/m³ of gas

Equivalent for replacing petrol: €0.12/km — 7× current cost

A household that currently consumes 1,500 m³ of natural gas per year would need approximately 458 kilograms of hydrogen for the same useful heat output (taking into account the slightly higher efficiency of an H₂ boiler). At €12/kg: €5,500 per year for heating alone — compared with €2,130 at the current natural-gas tariff. Almost a tripling of the heating bill.

Four pathways compared — what a household would pay

What stands out is that the hydrogen pathway would cost Sandra (on social assistance) €5,667 per year — more than a quarter of her income on energy alone. For Anna, €7,554 — 35 per cent of her income. For Mark & Lisa, €10,017 — twelve per cent of the household income. None of these figures appears in a government publication; none of them is communicated to voters before decisions are taken on the hydrogen pathway.

The hidden costs of hydrogen infrastructure

Eight hidden cost items:

One — boiler conversion or replacement. Existing Dutch central-heating boilers are designed for methane, not hydrogen. Hydrogen burns hotter and has a different flame structure — H₂-ready boilers costing €5,500 are required, depreciated over twenty years: €280/household/year.

Two — replacing neighbourhood pipelines. The Dutch gas network is made of steel and cast iron. Hydrogen causes hydrogen embrittlement in metal: the H₂ molecule penetrates the metal crystal structure and makes the material brittle. Replacing the entire local network with special steel (X42, X52) or polyethylene pipelines would cost €310/household/year.

Three — storage in salt caverns. Hydrogen requires three times the volume of natural gas for the same energy content. Gasunie is investing in underground salt caverns at Zuidwending, Bergermeer and Norg — €185/household/year.

Four — compression and transport. Hydrogen must be stored at 350–700 bar or as liquid hydrogen at −253°C. Both options require energy-intensive compression or liquefaction facilities — €165/household/year.

Five — SDE++ electrolysis subsidy. In 2026, green hydrogen is not yet viable without subsidy. Current SDE++ rates for green hydrogen amount to €3–4/kg in subsidy, rising through to 2035 — €420/household/year.

Six — reserve capacity. Electrolysis plants operate on intermittent green electricity from wind and solar; during periods without wind or with cloud cover, a hydrogen buffer must be available — €175/household/year for reserve capacity.

Seven — safety and leak detection. Hydrogen is the smallest molecule; it leaks through seals, valve connections and gaskets that contain methane. Leak detection requires modifications to every household connection — €90/household/year.

Eight — recycling of fuel cells. Fuel-cell stacks contain platinum catalysts, perfluorinated membranes (Nafion) and heavy metals. The recycling chain is not yet mature — €85/household/year reserved.

Total hidden hydrogen infrastructure costs: €1,710 per household per year. Compare this with the hidden wind and solar costs of €1,135 identified in the previous chapter — 50 per cent more. And the hidden total for the ethanol pathway: zero euros. Ethanol infrastructure uses existing filling stations, existing pipelines and existing engines.

Energy-poverty threshold — what the hydrogen pathway does to household finances

Under the hydrogen pathway, four of the six scenarios exceed the energy-poverty threshold. Anna spends 35 per cent of her income on energy; Sandra 27 per cent; Tom 22 per cent; Jasper 19 per cent. For Mark & Lisa (average income) and Jacobus (owner-director), the figure remains below the threshold but stands at 12–14 per cent — two to three times what they pay now.

Under the ethanol pathway, every household remains well below the poverty threshold. Anna 9 per cent, Sandra 6 per cent, Tom 6 per cent, Jasper 5 per cent. The average household, Mark & Lisa: 3 per cent. For those least able to afford it — the lowest-income groups — the difference between hydrogen and ethanol is existential.

Chain efficiency — why hydrogen loses so much

The fundamental weakness of hydrogen lies in the chain. From green electricity generated by wind or solar power to useful heat at the end user:

Electrolysis efficiency (green H₂): 75%

× compression to 700 bar: 90%

× transport through dedicated pipelines: 95%

× hydrogen boiler efficiency: 88%

= Total end-use heat efficiency: 56%

Comparison: heat pump using direct electricity: 280% (COP 3.5)

Comparison: ethanol pathway to heat: 88%

In other words: per kWh of green electricity, hydrogen delivers just 0.56 kWh of useful heat, while direct electrification via a heat pump delivers 2.8 kWh — five times as much. This is not a Dutch problem but a fundamental thermodynamic fact: hydrogen is an energy carrier with intrinsic conversion losses.

For mobility, the picture is similar. Fuel-cell car: 28 per cent end-use efficiency from primary electricity. Electric car: 75 per cent. Ethanol car: 30 per cent. For mobility, hydrogen is only marginally better than the current petrol economy (25 per cent), while an EV is four times better.

What hydrogen does well — an honest qualification

Hydrogen is not pointless. For three applications, it remains the best climate solution:

One — industrial process heat above 800°C. The steel industry (Tata IJmuiden), glass industry and ammonia production. No heat pump or combustion engine can compete there; only hydrogen or the direct combustion of fossil fuels works in those applications.

Two — heavy freight transport over very long distances. Cargo ships, international lorries and a replacement for kerosene in aviation. The volumes of ethanol required per kilometre are impractical there; hydrogen or synthetic fuels are the reasonable options.

Three — long-duration seasonal storage. To compensate for summer–winter differences in solar production, hydrogen is a more chemically viable form of storage than batteries (which discharge themselves).

For these three applications combined, the Netherlands would require approximately 0.8–1.2 million tonnes of hydrogen per year — a modest volume compared with the 4–6 million tonnes envisaged by the National Hydrogen Programme for the full scenario. According to this document, the remaining three to five million tonnes should not go to households and light mobility — ethanol works better there thermodynamically and economically.

What the figures collectively show

The hydrogen pathway for households and light mobility, as envisaged in the National Hydrogen Programme, would represent a financial catastrophe for Dutch voters. Four of the six scenarios would exceed the energy-poverty threshold. In the other two, energy bills would double or triple.

This is not an argument against hydrogen as a technology. It is the numerical finding that hydrogen is being planned for the wrong applications. It is valuable for industrial processes, heavy transport and long-duration storage. For heating homes and powering the average passenger car, however, it is — for thermodynamic reasons — approximately three times more expensive than necessary.

The ethanol pathway does the same job at one third of the cost. With the same climate effects (CO₂-neutral across the cycle), without energy-poverty effects, and without the massive infrastructure investment hydrogen requires.

What the figures collectively show — four observations

Three analyses. Six scenarios. Three technological pathways. One conclusion.

First observation. The Silent Analysis III showed that the Dutch cascade was negative under virtually every party, with the sharpest damage under GL-PvdA and D66. What it could not show, because it was not yet foreseeable at the time, is that most of this damage is directly connected to European climate policy. Forty per cent of the third-order cascade comes from the Green Deal and CBAM. When these disappear, forty per cent of the damage disappears. That is the seismic shift demonstrated by the first analysis.

Second observation. Outside the cascade lies a second layer — the direct impact on household finances. The current government pathway (heat pump + electric car + wind/solar infrastructure) is more expensive for every household than the current situation, even when the hidden costs (subsidies, grid expansion, batteries, reserve capacity, replacement and recycling) are not charged directly to the household. The ethanol pathway is cheaper than the current situation for every household and considerably cheaper than the government pathway.

Third observation. The hydrogen pathway envisaged in the current National Hydrogen Programme is outright destructive when it comes to household applications. Four of the six scenarios would exceed the energy-poverty threshold; in two others, energy bills would double or triple. The thermodynamic choice to convert green electricity into hydrogen first, and then back into heat or motion, consumes approximately three times as much primary energy as direct electrification or ethanol combustion. Hydrogen deserves its place in industrial processes and heavy transport, not in the Dutch central-heating boiler or the average passenger car.

Fourth observation. The two equatorial reforms together fundamentally change the picture for Dutch voters. Under the current Brussels course, a loss of 5–15 per cent of the household budget becomes, under the nature-adjusted course, a stable or even slightly improved position. For the lowest-income groups — Sandra on benefits, Anna the pensioner and Jasper the school-leaver — the relative improvement is greatest.

This is not an argument for any particular party. It is a numerical observation: for Dutch voters, the choice between the current Brussels model and a nature-adjusted Brussels model has a concrete price. Per household, per year, in euros that are directly visible in the bank account.

Methodology and sources

This piece builds on the three-step model of Silent Analysis III, with an added BiCRS layer and a separate ethanol analysis.

BiCRS layer — first analysis

For each scenario and party, a BiCRS benefit was calculated according to the formula: base_benefit × pressure_multiplier × scenario_sensitivity × cycle_year. The base benefit (€1,500/year) represents the average household benefit from the removal of Green Deal and CBAM pass-through costs in 2030. The pressure multiplier (1 + pressure_index/15) takes into account the fact that parties with a higher pressure index would have caused greater climate costs — and that BiCRS therefore removes more of them. Scenario sensitivity varies between 1.3 (pensioner, primarily energy bill) and 2.4 (automotive supplier, everything benefits).

Anchor: the Brussels Consequences Map — BiCRS quantified the EU-wide costs of the Green Deal package at 3.5–4% of EU GDP per year and CBAM at 0.7–1% of EU GDP per year in 2030. Prorated for the Netherlands based on its share of EU GDP and the number of households: an average climate cost of €5,180 per household, of which €3,500 could be removed through BiCRS implementation.

Ethanol analysis — second analysis

For each household type, heating and mobility costs were calculated under three pathways:

  • Current pathway: natural gas €1.42/m³ × consumption + petrol €1.78/litre × (km/15)
  • Government pathway: heat pump (electricity × 2.78 kWh/m³-equivalent + investment depreciation) + electric car (0.18 kWh/km × electricity price + depreciation of additional cost) + hidden wind/solar costs of €1,135/year/household
  • Ethanol pathway: micro-CHP (1.7 litres of ethanol per m³ gas-equivalent × €0.55/litre + investment depreciation) + ethanol car (1 litre per 11 km × €0.55) + micro-CHP depreciation of €233/year

Hidden wind/solar costs consist of six items: SDE++ subsidy €380, grid expansion €220, grid-scale batteries €95, reserve gas-fired power stations €130, replacement on a 15-year cycle €165, and recycling and decommissioning €145. Sources: PBL Climate and Energy Outlook 2025, TenneT Investment Plan 2026–2035, Eurostat ETS data 2026, and specialised studies on Li-ion recycling (JRC 2023) and composite wind-turbine blades (WindEurope 2024).

Hydrogen analysis — third analysis

For each household type, hydrogen heating and hydrogen mobility were calculated on the assumption of a delivered price of €12/kg (BloombergNEF 2024, median value). Conversion per m³ of natural-gas equivalent: 0.305 kg H₂ (based on 35 MJ/m³ of gas, 120 MJ/kg H₂, and a central-heating efficiency of 88 per cent for H₂ versus 92 per cent for gas).

Fuel-cell mobility: 1 kg H₂ per 100 km, based on real-world measurements of the Toyota Mirai and Hyundai Nexo. Hidden infrastructure costs based on eight items from the Climate Agreement, the Gasunie investment plan 2025–2035, SDE++ rates for green hydrogen from RVO 2024, and specialised studies on hydrogen embrittlement of steel (TNO 2022) and fuel-cell recycling (JRC Joint Research Centre 2024).

Chain efficiency according to the IEA Global Hydrogen Review 2024: electrolysis 75% (current state of the art: alkaline and PEM), compression to 700 bar 90%, transport via a converted pipeline 95%, end use via central heating 88%. Cumulative: 56% thermal efficiency from primary electricity — versus 280% via a heat pump (COP 3.5) and 88% via the ethanol chain.

Limitation and invitation

All three models have deliberately been kept transparent. The Python files scenarios_NL_BiCRS.py, scenarios_NL_ethanol.py and scenarios_NL_waterstof.py will be made available on gevolgenkaart.nl once the platform goes live. Anyone who disputes the figures can reproduce the calculations.

What this piece is not. Not a prediction — policy outcomes depend on implementation and context. Not an appeal to any party — the figures speak for themselves. Not an attack on wind or solar power as technologies — they remain useful in the mix, but not as the mainstay of the Dutch energy transition.

What this piece is. The question of whether there is a cheaper, cleaner and fairer path than the current government pathway. The answer, based on modelling and figures verifiable by everyone, is yes. Two paths, in fact — separate from one another, each standing on its own.

Methaan herzien — wat de massabalans en de tijd doen

Het GWP-cijfer (28–30 over 100 jaar) overschat het werkelijke klimaateffect omdat het de kortlevende eigenschap van methaan uitsmeert over een eeuw. De fysica is duidelijker:

  • Atmosferische halfwaardetijd: ongeveer 9 jaar. Na 10 tot 15 jaar is het overgrote deel van het methaan geoxideerd door de hydroxylradicaal (·OH) in de troposfeer.
  • Reactie: CH₄ + 2·O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O (via meerdere tussenstappen).
  • Massabalans: 1 gram CH₄ (16 g/mol) wordt 1 × (44/16) ≈ 2,75 gram CO₂ plus ~2,25 gram H₂O. Het methaan is daarna verdwenen; alleen de CO₂-fractie blijft over.

Een rationele klimaatboekhouding telt methaan dus tweemaal: één keer voor zijn directe forceringseffect over de eerste 10–15 jaar, en daarna niet meer als methaan maar als ~2,75 g CO₂ per oorspronkelijke gram CH₄. Wat na 15 jaar nog op het methaan-conto staat is een rekenkundige fictie.

Conclusie: methaan is niet het grootste probleem. CO₂ is dat wel. Methaan is een korte piek die zichzelf afbreekt; CO₂ blijft 100 tot 1.000 jaar in de atmosfeer aanwezig en kan alleen worden teruggebracht via directe verwijdering. Daarom is BiCRS — koolstof permanent vastleggen in de bodem — de zinvolle hervorming, en niet het reguleren van een gas dat zichzelf binnen vijftien jaar omzet in precies datgene wat al het echte probleem is.

Beleidsconsequentie: de huidige Brusselse en Nederlandse methaan-doelstellingen behandelen methaan alsof het eeuwig in de atmosfeer blijft. Dat is in strijd met de chemie. Methaan-emissies die nu gebeuren zijn rond 2040 niet meer methaan — ze zijn geoxideerd tot CO₂. Wie veehouderij of moerassen aanpakt vanwege GWP-100 verschuift het probleem niet, maar tovert het ook niet weg: de echte hervorming is CO₂ uit de atmosfeer halen, en dat doet BiCRS.
