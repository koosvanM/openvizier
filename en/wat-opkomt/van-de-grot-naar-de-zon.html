From the cave to the sun, from the earth to orbit, from 4D to 7D

Three steps, two thousand four hundred years, and one movement — on Plato, Copernicus, and why a real breakthrough never comes from new arguments, but from a new perspective.

By Jacobus van Merksteijn · Malta, July 2026

Philosophy has never changed

There is something strange going on with the human mind. Since Plato wrote his Politeia around 380 before Christ, we have made countless inventions, waged wars, discovered continents, split atoms and built machines that think. But the core question of philosophy — what is real, and how do we know? — has not moved a millimetre in twenty-four centuries.

We read Plato and recognise ourselves. We read Socrates and hear our own doubts. We read Aristotle and catch ourselves still using his categories. Descartes added an "I think, therefore I am", Kant drew the limits of reason, Nietzsche struck the idols with a hammer, and yet: the question has remained the same. What does man really see? And why does he so stubbornly think that the shadow on the wall is the real thing?

This is not because the philosophers have not done their best. It is because philosophy itself is practised inside the cave. Words refer to shadows. Arguments pile on other arguments. Books comment on books. And the prisoners look at each other and praise whoever names the shadows most sharply.

Whoever really wants to leave the cave must do something other than philosophise. He must add a dimension to the one in which he looks.

Plato: the first to point at the wall

Plato's allegory of the cave, written in the seventh book of the Politeia, is no fairy tale. It is a structural diagnosis of how knowledge works.

Prisoners have been chained from an early age in a cave, with their backs to the entrance. Behind them a fire burns. Between the fire and them runs a wall, and over that wall objects are carried. The shadows of those objects fall on the back wall — and those shadows are the only thing the prisoners have ever seen. They have invented names for them, they discuss their movements, they honour whoever best predicts the sequence.

When one of them is freed and turns towards the fire, it hurts. He is blinded. He wants to turn back to the wall — to the "intelligible reality", as Plato calls it. Led outside, into the bright sunlight, he can at first see nothing. Only after a long time does his eye grow accustomed to the light and he recognises that the shadows in which he believed his whole life were merely projections of something much more real.

And then comes the sharpest part of Plato's story, which is often forgotten: the freed man must go back into the cave. He must tell the others. And they will laugh at him, find him less clever than before (because he no longer sees the shadows so sharply), and if he insists, they will want to kill him.

That last is no literary exaggeration. Plato wrote this after his own teacher Socrates had been sentenced to death by Athenian democracy — precisely because he asked too many questions about the shadows.

Plato's message is therefore not: "think better." His message is: **step out, look from another level, and accept that the return will hurt.**

Copernicus: the first to actually shift perspective

Almost two thousand years later Nicolaus Copernicus did something structurally identical to what Plato described — but this time not in an allegory, but with a concrete astronomical model.

Before Copernicus the geocentric worldview of Ptolemy was the standard. The earth stood still, the sun and the planets turned around it. This model worked. It predicted the positions of the planets with astounding accuracy. But it had a problem: to achieve that accuracy, the astronomers had to introduce epicycles — circles within circles, auxiliary loops upon auxiliary loops, because the planets sometimes seemed to run backwards across the sky. Each new measurement required a new correction. The edifice grew ever more complicated.

In 1543 Copernicus did something radical. He discovered no new planet. He found no new formula. He looked at **the same data from a different centre**. If you place the sun in the centre and the earth as one of the planets orbiting it, the epicycles disappear of their own accord. The apparent backward motion of Mars is no longer a mystery — it is simply what you see when a faster inner planet overtakes a slower outer planet.

The epicycles were not wrong in their predictions. They were redundant as soon as you shifted the perspective.

What Copernicus did is exactly what Plato describes in the cave. The astronomers before 1543 were the prisoners describing the shadows on the wall with ever more refined language. Copernicus turned around and saw the fire. And from that moment returning to the old model became as impossible as returning to the belief that shadows are real.

Note: the measurements did not change. The sky continued to do what the sky did. What changed was the coordinate system.

7D: the same movement, one layer deeper

Today, in 2026, we find ourselves once again in an epicycle crisis. Modern physics works brilliantly — until it no longer does. And where it no longer does, we patch it up:

  • Dark matter — a particle said to form 85 per cent of all matter, which we have searched for over sixty years, and which we have never found.
  • Dark energy — a force said to make up 68 per cent of the universe, without known mechanism or cause.
  • Cosmological constant — a number we insert to make the equation add up, without knowing why it has that value.
  • Singularities in black holes — points of infinite density where the laws of physics "break", which nearly always means the theory itself is incomplete, not that nature is infinite.
  • Sixty years of fusion research — billions invested in machines that must reach a hundred million degrees to break the Coulomb barrier, and still no commercial energy production.

These are our modern epicycles. They work — in the sense that they somewhat fit the existing data — but they are complicated, undemonstrable and unsatisfying. Whoever criticises them is regarded as someone who "does not understand science". Precisely as the Ptolemaic astronomers no doubt thought of anyone who asked why so many epicycles were needed.

The 7-dimensional framework does what Copernicus did. It adds no exotic particles, no new forces, no unfindable fields. It adds three dimensions to the coordinate system in which we describe reality:

  • G — size / scale acceleration. Locally determines how fast things grow or shrink. Mass is not fundamental, mass is *proportional to G. The speed of light is not universal, it depends on G*. Where G passes through zero and becomes negative, the direction of time reverses and electromagnetic forces flip — meaning that the Coulomb barrier does not have to be overcome, but inverted.
  • W — value. An axis from antimatter to matter. Things with a different W-value than ours are invisible to us but do exert gravity — what we call "dark matter" is ordinary matter at a different W-position. But W reaches further: in living systems W becomes a measure of coherence, quality, and whether something contributes to life or to decay. W is the bridge between physics and ethics.
  • N — plurality. An index for multiple universes. Every black hole is not a dead end but a universe. Our universe originated from the interior of a black hole at a higher level. The "Big Bang" was our birth within something larger.

What standard physics describes with three separate inventions (dark matter, dark energy, singularity), the 7D model describes with a single geometric extension. To carry through the metaphor, we have placed the sun in the middle.

For those who want to check it technically, the full framework — with the metric tensor, the definition of G, the emergent quantities — is at 7-dim.com. For those who do not want it technical, there is Plato. Everyone has his own way in. But the movement is the same.

What we can learn if we step to a new dimension

Once you have added a dimension to how you look, everything changes.

Problems become simpler, not more complicated. This is the paradoxical mark of a successful perspective change. Copernicus' model was simpler than Ptolemy's. The 7D model is simpler than standard cosmology with its patches and constants. When reality begins to seem too complicated, that is usually a sign that you are looking from too low a dimension.

Apparent contradictions dissolve. In a 2D economy growth and sustainability are at war. In a 3D politics freedom and security are at war. In a 4D physics general relativity and quantum mechanics are at war. As soon as you add enough dimensions, the contradictions turn out to be no contradictions — they were projections of the same object onto screens too small.

What is valuable gets a place. In current science, "value" has no home. Ethics floats somewhere between psychology and culture, without foundation. As soon as you introduce a W dimension, value is no longer an opinion — it becomes geometry. A society moving towards higher W is literally, in coordinates, moving in the same direction as matter organising itself into life. That is not metaphor. That is geometry.

AI gets its place without making man redundant. Artificial intelligence is extremely powerful in dimensions 1 to 4: space, time, data, pattern. But it has no G perspective (scale and context), no W perspective (value and coherence), no N perspective (alternative possibilities). Whoever sees AI as a competitor of man, thinks in 4D. Whoever sees AI as an amplification of man in the dimensions it masters, while man gives direction in the dimensions it cannot perceive, thinks in 7D. The danger is not AI. The danger is a society that hands its W dimension over to a system that has none.

Human connection gets geometry. Why does a room full of hostile people feel different from a room full of friends, before a word has been spoken? In 4D that is "biochemistry". In 7D it is resonance: two configurations aligning across several dimensions at once. Respect is then no longer a social convention but the recognition of someone's full dimensional existence. A society that reduces people to one dimension — money, productivity, status — literally shrinks their reality.

But we must actually DO it

And here comes the point where most philosophy fails. Plato wrote his dialogues, and two thousand four hundred years later we are still reading them — and still sitting in the same cave. Copernicus published his model, and it took more than a century before it was truly accepted. Galileo was hauled before the Inquisition. Bruno was burned.

This is no coincidence. This is the regularity Plato already described: **the prisoners want to kill the freed man.** Not because they are evil. Because his message unhinges their whole worldview, and that is more painful than getting rid of him.

That is why thinking alone is not enough. Thinking without acting is a prisoner who quietly turns around, sees the sun for a moment, and then turns back to the wall because it is warmer there. Perspective changes nothing about the world if it is not **turned into movement**.

What is doing, in this context? Doing means:

  • Using 7D language in conversations, in writings, in decisions — even when the others do not understand what you are talking about. Every time you replace "dark matter" with "matter with a different W-value", every time you replace "GDP" with "movement along the W-axis", you shift the collective frame of reference a little.
  • Building concrete proposals that make no sense within 4D thinking but become perfectly logical in 7D. Fusion via locally negative G. An economy that measures coherence instead of transaction volume. Education that teaches children to think in coordinates instead of subjects. Governance that explicitly optimises across several dimensions at once instead of maximising one — as worked out in Nova Democratia, where ten VMP core fields are simultaneously measured and steered instead of the one-dimensional struggle for seats and positions of power that dominates current party politics.
  • Absorbing the pain of not being understood. Of being catalogued as "eccentric". Of colleagues who stare blankly, of publishers who reject, of institutions that ignore your application. That is not proof that you are wrong. That is proof that you are doing precisely what Plato described.
  • Do not deny the cave. Whoever pretends the prisoners are "simply stupid" has understood nothing of Plato. They are not stupid. They are chained. Their chains are not of iron but of habit, upbringing, media, education, fear. The freed man does not blame the prisoners for their chains — he tries to make the chains visible.

For the technically interested reader the framework is at 7-dim.com, with formulas, metric tensor, and applications. For those who prefer to start with human history there is Plato — the Politeia is still available in every bookshop, in every language. For the physicist there is Copernicus. For the administrator, Plato's Laws. For the child, the tale of the goldfish in the bowl.

**Everyone his own entrance. But no one stays in the cave once he has stood up.**

Philosophy has not changed in two thousand four hundred years, because philosophy cannot change itself. What changes is not what we think, but from which dimension we look. Plato showed the wall. Copernicus showed the sun. The 7D framework shows three dimensions that were always already hidden in the data.

The rest is up to us. And "up to us" is not a philosophical statement. It is an instruction.
