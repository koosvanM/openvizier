The growth the government is spending itself

A quarterly figure of 0.4% and the headline that left out the composition.

Jacobus van Merksteijn

On 30 July 2026 Statistics Netherlands (CBS) reported that Dutch gross domestic product in the second quarter grew by 0.4 per cent compared with the first quarter, and by 1.3 per cent compared with the same quarter a year earlier. The next morning the newspapers reported that the «feared disaster scenarios» had failed to materialise and that there was a «small growth acceleration». The chief economist of CBS called the figure «not bad, actually» to news agency ANP. What none of those headlines contained does appear in the CBS table itself: the largest engine under this growth is the government.

What CBS added

In the CBS press release of 30 July, the spending components that drive the annual growth of 1.3 per cent are explicitly named. Government consumption in Q2 2026 was 2.5 per cent higher than in Q2 2025. Household consumption was 1.3 per cent higher. Investment grew by 0.4 per cent. Government consumption therefore grew roughly twice as fast as household consumption and more than six times as fast as investment. What the government spent more on, CBS formulates in two words: «care and wages».

On a quarterly basis, the sector «government, education and care» grew by 0.4 per cent. At the same time, sectors belonging to the productive core contracted:

  • Mining and quarrying: −5.2%
  • Energy companies: −3.5%
  • Financial institutions: −1.1%
  • Construction: −0.8%
  • Agriculture, forestry and fisheries: −0.1%

The sectors that did grow are trade, hospitality, transport and storage (+1.8%) and industry (+1.5%). All other growth sectors are services or government.

What the press made of it

The dominant frame on 30 July was not the composition, but the total. BNR: «Dutch economy grows 0.4 per cent: ‘feared disaster scenarios fail to materialise’». NRC: «Dutch economy grows by 0.4 per cent: the ‘disaster scenarios’ are not so bad, says CBS chief economist». De Aandeelhouder spoke of a «small growth acceleration» — a shift from 0.3 to 0.4 per cent. ABN AMRO’s research desk headlined «Strong half year for the Netherlands and the eurozone». In August, Rabobank titled «Households spent more in geopolitically volatile times», shifting the cause of the headwind abroad.

With consumer confidence, the same movement. The figure moved from −39 in June to −35 in July. The twenty-year average is −11. The headline became «improving again». A level 24 points below normal, with an improvement of 4 points within that level, is presented as an upward trend.

What the headlines pushed aside

CBS itself published a second release on 31 July: «Economic picture somewhat more negative in July». Of the thirteen indicators in the Business-cycle Clock, nine scored below their long-term trend. The clock reading fell from −0.3 in June to −0.33 in July. That release did not make the front page of most titles.

Inflation in July came in at 3.2 per cent, above the eurozone reference. Motor fuels were 22 per cent more expensive than a year earlier. De Nederlandsche Bank had already lowered its GDP growth forecast for 2026 in its spring projection from 1.2 to 0.8 per cent. According to the same DNB projection, the budget deficit will come out at 3.3 per cent of GDP — above the European norm of 3 per cent for the first time since the corona year 2020.

What DNB and the Budget Memorandum name as drivers of that rising deficit: higher government spending on care and social security, a one-off expenditure of some 8 billion euros for the reform of military pensions, and the broader build-up of defence spending. Those same expenditures sit on the other side of the balance in the government consumption that pulls GDP up.

What the philosophers said

Epictetus wrote that it is not things that trouble us, but our judgements about them. The useful part of that sentence for these figures is not consoling but analytical. If judgement moves with what was possible instead of with what is desirable or factual, the norm disappears from view. Then any figure above zero becomes good news, and every absent disaster an achievement.

Seneca observed that man learns to endure poverty by reminding himself how bad it could have been. That is a personal virtue. Applied to national figures it becomes a trick. The comparison shifts from «how well are we doing» to «how bad could it have been», and the question of the composition of the figure falls away.

The shopping basket is growing, financed by debt and out of a production machine that is simultaneously shrinking. The headline «disaster scenarios fail to materialise» does not describe that dynamic. It hides it.

What an honest headline would have said

«GDP grows 1.3 per cent year-on-year, of which government consumption 2.5 per cent. Construction, energy and mining shrink. Deficit above EU norm.» That is exactly what CBS reports. It just does not make the front page.
