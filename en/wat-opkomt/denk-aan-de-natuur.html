Think about nature, with nature as the foundation

An integrated climate, industry and trade proposal for the Dutch government

By Jacobus van Merksteijn — Het Open Vizier, August 2026

The Netherlands has the knowledge, industry, ports and trading position to become Europe’s first climate-negative modern economy within twenty years. What is missing is not technology, money or land — but a coherent policy vision that combines four proven strands: tropical co-cultivation in partner countries for fuel and animal feed, long-lasting biobased plastic for carbon storage, cement and wood replacement, and superior insulation made from polymers. This proposal aligns these four strands and makes clear where political intervention is needed.

1. Where we stand

The Netherlands emits approximately 145 megatonnes of CO2 per year. The Climate Agreement calls for a 55 per cent reduction by 2030 compared with 1990, and climate neutrality by 2050. Wind, solar, heat pumps and insulation alone will not achieve these targets — this is now widely acknowledged across the sector. An additional strand is needed that removes CO2 from the atmosphere on a large scale and stores it over the longer term.

The official EU approach is CCS: capturing CO2 at industrial sources and injecting it into depleted gas fields beneath the North Sea. Cost: €60 to €120 per tonne, with permanent monitoring, the risk of leakage and public opposition. The Netherlands is investing billions in this route — and there is a cheaper, safer and more robust route alongside it.

All four strands share the same raw material: carbon, extracted from the atmosphere by two tropical plants. 3. Strand 1: Tropical co-cultivation

Two plants grow together on equatorial land. Giant Juncao is a giant grass that reaches five metres in height in two months and produces up to 140 tonnes of biomass per hectare per year — ten times more than a Dutch cereal field. Moringa is a tree with protein-rich leaves that grows among the Juncao and enriches the soil with nitrogen.

This co-cultivation produces four products per hectare per year:

  • Approximately 30,000 litres of bioethanol as fuel for passenger cars.
  • Four to six tonnes of protein-rich animal feed, making Brazilian soya meal redundant.
  • Raw material for biobased plastic (via ethanol → polymer).
  • Sixty to 120 tonnes of CO2 stored permanently underground.

To run all petrol cars in the Netherlands on this bioethanol, 313,000 hectares would be needed — two-thirds of the province of Groningen, in Africa. In countries where agricultural land is used marginally and young people are unemployed. For Uganda or Mauritania, this would provide 100,000 to 170,000 jobs and restore degraded soil.

4. Strand 2: Plastic that stores CO2

This is the least familiar but most powerful part of the proposal. It runs counter to intuition and to much prevailing environmental policy, so it warrants an explanation.

Plastic consists of roughly 78 per cent carbon — it is one of the most carbon-rich materials made by humans. When plastic is burned, that carbon goes directly into the air as CO2. When plastic is buried or disposed of in a sealed, chemically inert environment — for example, 200 metres deep in a sealed lignite mine — that carbon remains stable for thousands of years. Polyethylene film from the 1950s is still being found intact in soils today.

The implication is significant: if we make the plastic from carbon extracted from the atmosphere (biobased), and store it in the ground at the end of its life, we have removed CO2 from the atmosphere overall. Every tonne of biobased polyethylene treated in this way removes approximately 3.1 tonnes of CO2 permanently from the air.

This is neither new nor theoretical. In 1994, the Netherlands made a comparable policy shift: fruit, vegetable and garden waste could no longer be burned, but had to be sent for composting and biogas production. That decision changed one rule in the waste legislation and fundamentally made the Dutch waste system more sustainable. For plastic, precisely the same rule change is needed: move it from ‘household residual waste’ to ‘certified underground storage under CRCF’.

What we should not do: compostable bioplastic

Much environmental policy promotes compostable bioplastics (PLA, PHA). For carbon storage, this is a mistake. These plastics are designed to break down within years or decades — at which point they return their stored carbon to the atmosphere. For single-use applications this may appear to be a solution, but it is a loop that solves nothing: carbon circulates but does not leave the atmosphere.

For CO2 storage, we need precisely the opposite: long-lasting, non-degradable biobased plastic. Bio-polyethylene (Braskem already produces this commercially in Brazil), bio-polypropylene, bio-PET and polyethylene furanoate (PEF, under development by Avantium in Delfzijl). Chemically identical to their fossil-based counterparts, but with carbon from the air.

The fiscal dimension

Since 2021, the Netherlands has paid the EU a penalty of €0.80 per kilogram of non-recycled plastic waste — in 2024 this amounted to €235 million. At the same time, we pay a further €99 million in ETS charges for the CO2 emissions from plastic incineration. Total: €334 million per year in punitive levies on something for which we should receive a carbon certificate worth €99 million. Fiscal reversal: approximately €433 million per year.

5. Replacing cement and wood

Cement is responsible for 7 to 8 per cent of global CO2 emissions. Every tonne of cement emits more than 900 kilograms of CO2. Much of what we make from cement can be replaced by polymers from the Juncao chain: tiles, pipes, window frames, panels, roof tiles and street furniture. Load-bearing structures (foundations, columns) will remain reinforced concrete, but non-load-bearing cement elements can be replaced with 50 to 90 per cent fewer CO2 emissions.

Wood, too, can be replaced more intelligently. A wooden garden chair produces 224 kilograms of CO2 during manufacture; an equivalent chair made from recycled polyethylene produces 18.6 kilograms — a factor of 12 difference. In addition, the plastic chair lasts 25 to 50 years, compared with 3 to 8 years for softwood. For the Dutch garden-furniture market, a switch would mean annual CO2 savings of approximately 200 to 300 kilotonnes and the use of around one billion recycled plastic bottles.

Wooden disposable cutlery on aircraft — increasingly used since the EU directive on single-use plastics — presents a specific problem: catering waste from aircraft must legally be incinerated (ICW category 1). Sorting it for composting is not permitted. Wooden cutlery on aircraft is therefore, by definition, a CO2 emitter: carbon from felled trees is sent straight into the atmosphere via the incinerator. Biobased plastic cutlery linked to geological storage under the CRCF could instead deliver a net CO2 removal.

Paper is a third category in which the prevailing policy logic is backwards. When forest loss, production, transport, recycling processes and end-of-life incineration are fully accounted for, paper is often 8 to 20 times more polluting than recycled plastic. Newspapers that switch to plastic packaging instead of paper substantially reduce their CO2 footprint, despite public perception suggesting the opposite.

6. Super-insulation made from plastic

Aerogel is the most efficient insulating material we know: it consists of 90 to 99 per cent air in nanopores. Traditionally, aerogel is made from silica, but polymer aerogels also exist: made from polyurethane, polyimide or PVC. For the same insulation value, the layer is 40 to 60 per cent thinner than mineral wool or polystyrene.

Biobased polymer aerogel can be produced from the Juncao chain. Service life in a building: 50 years or more. Afterwards, geological storage under CRCF. Every building thus becomes an active CO2 store as well as a super-insulated structure, with thinner walls and more usable floor space.

For renovating existing homes (where thicker insulation often does not fit), this is decisive. The Netherlands has approximately 8.4 million homes, a considerable proportion of which were built before 1990. Aerogel renovation could substantially reduce the average gas consumption of 987 cubic metres per home per year.

7. What the government must do

Six concrete steps. They can begin in the next parliamentary term.

Step 1: Establish a certification framework

Commission the RIVM or an independent certification institute, in cooperation with Puro.earth or Verra, to develop an accredited methodology within twelve months for carbon storage through biobased plastic in geological repositories. Dutch universities must not be contracting parties (see Step 6). Without a methodology there are no certificates, and without certificates there is no financing.

Step 2: Amend plastic-waste legislation

Amend the Decree on the Landfilling Ban for Waste Materials: plastic demonstrably destined for certified geological storage will no longer be classified as ‘residual waste’. Phasing: 20 per cent in year 1 (2027), 40 per cent in 2028, 100 per cent in 2031. Analogous to the biomass relocation of 1994.

Step 3: Pilot in Uganda or Mauritania

Financing through the Climate and Transition Fund for a 2,000-hectare Juncao–Moringa co-cultivation pilot. Implementation: the Netherlands Enterprise Agency, the Ministry of Foreign Affairs and the Ministry of Agriculture of the partner country, together with local cooperatives and the Chinese Juncao Institute (FAFU) as technical partner. No Dutch university as a contracting party.

Step 4: Negotiations in Brussels

The Netherlands must advocate four changes in the EU:

  • Expand the CRCF (Carbon Removals Certification Framework 2024) to include plastic in geological storage as a recognised carbon removal. It currently recognises biochar, DAC and Bio-CCS; plastic storage is absent for no physical reason.
  • Differentiate the 2021 plastic levy: the levy on incinerated plastic should be retained or increased, while geologically stored plastic should be exempt and receive a carbon certificate.
  • Introduce an exemption to the EU Single-Use Plastics Directive (2019/904) for biobased, long-lived plastic linked to the CRCF.
  • Introduce an exemption to Regulation 1069/2009 (animal by-products, Category 1) for certified biobased plastic from aircraft catering destined for direct CRCF storage.

Step 5: National biobased polymer fund

Use the fiscal reversal of €334 million per year (the plastic levy plus avoided ETS costs) to establish a fund for three purposes: price compensation for biobased, long-lived polymer in products, R&amp;D into direct catalytic conversion (biomass to monomer without fermentation), and the construction of certified geological storage facilities. Dutch industry (Chemelot, Sittard-Geleen, Delfzijl) already has the basic infrastructure.

Step 6: Warning about IP risks involving Dutch universities

Before any other steps: no Dutch university should be a contracting party, IP holder or main contractor in this programme. This is not an ideological position but follows from a specific documented precedent.

In August 2022, Carbon-Alert (Hengelo, represented by the undersigned) published the core of this proposal through PR Newswire, ANP and BNNVARA Vroege Vogels: Giant Juncao as CO2 storage through deep injection and a peatland analogy. In that report, Albert van den Berg, director of MESA+ at the University of Twente, was quoted as a consortium partner. On 24 February 2025, the UT Climate Centre published two pages on utwente.nl presenting precisely these core claims as its own research — without mentioning Carbon-Alert, the undersigned, the Chinese developer Lin Zhanxi (FAFU), or Lei Xuejun (Central South University), who discovered the CO2 uptake capacity.

This is not an isolated incident but follows from the national IP regulations of Universities of the Netherlands. External knowledge shared with a Dutch university systematically risks being brought under university ownership claims. For a programme that will deploy hundreds of millions of euros in public money on this knowledge, that is an unacceptable risk. Certification assignments should go to the RIVM or independent institutes; technical partnerships should be established directly with FAFU (China) and the KZN Department of Agriculture (South Africa). If a Dutch university is nevertheless involved for specific analyses (for example, an LCA), this should be exclusively under contract research in which intellectual property explicitly remains with Carbon-Alert / the programme.

This is not a replacement for all climate policy. It is an addition that addresses four problems at once: climate targets, energy security, the industrial future and the trade position. For the Netherlands, with its ports, chemical industry and trading history, this package is a better fit than for any other EU country.

9. Why now

There is a window of perhaps five years. China has been developing the Juncao plant since 1983 and now has 45 varieties. South Africa has been cultivating it in KwaZulu-Natal for 15 years. The EU CRCF was established in 2024 and its implementing details will be filled in over the coming years — now is the time to add plastic storage to it. The carbon certificates market is moving towards €125–150 per tonne, which will make cultivation and storage financially self-sustaining after the initial 2,000-hectare pilot.

If the Netherlands enters now, we will set the standards. If we wait until Germany, France or the US takes the initiative, we will become an importer of their technology and pay their prices.

10. Conclusion

The Netherlands has two options. The first: continue on the current path. Billions on hydrogen infrastructure, CCS in gas fields, electric cars running on mixed electricity, and hoping that by 2050 we achieve something close to climate neutrality.

The second: recognise that nature has been removing CO2 from the air and storing it underground for 200 million years — that process is called peat formation — and that all we need to do is accelerate it with two tropical plants and one rule change for plastic. The carbon from the air first becomes fuel, then feed, then packaging, then a building component, then insulation, and ultimately is stored permanently underground. A closed cycle. With economic returns instead of costs. With employment in developing countries instead of oil imports from unpredictable regions.

The calculation is complete. The technology is proven. Only the political will is still lacking.
