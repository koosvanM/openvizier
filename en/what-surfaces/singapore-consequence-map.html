Consequence Map · Singapore · June 2026

The Singapore Consequence Map

Fifteen scenarios, ten Singapore federal mechanisms. What your share of Singapore in 2030 yields under cumulative MOF/MAS/MOM policy — when CPF reform, GST 9%, Pillar Two, BTO+Prime, work permit quotas, ABSD, COE, Carbon Tax and the MAS S$NEER band compound at third order.

By Jacobus van Merksteijn · Malta · June 2026

If the Brussels Consequence Map plotted the EU's hidden compounding, and the Swiss version did the same for Bern, then the Singapore Consequence Map asks how a small, highly-engineered city-state compounds its own choices. Singapore's policy stack is exceptionally legible — every mechanism is documented in MOF Budget statements, CPF disclosures, MAS quarterly bulletins and HDB framework papers. The third-order compounding, however, is rarely shown in one frame.

This map plots fifteen Singapore scenarios against ten federal mechanisms in force in 2026. Each cell is the % effect on income (citizens) or revenue (businesses) by 2030 under cumulative policy. Read column by column to see what each lever does. Read row by row to see what one life or business absorbs.

What the map shows

Each row is a Singapore scenario — from Mr Tan, the 67-year-old retiree in a 4-room HDB in Bedok, through Daniel the software engineer in Bishan, to a semiconductor multinational in Tuas. Each column is one federal mechanism, from CPF Life reform through the GST 9% increase, Pillar Two for multinationals, the BTO + Prime/Plus flat regime, work permit quotas, ABSD, COE, the carbon tax progression and the MAS S$NEER band. The rightmost column is an alternative MOF reference — what the same scenarios would yield under a more SME-friendly, more affordability-anchored course.

The ten mechanisms

Social + fiscal

  • CPF Life reform — the 2024-2026 adjustments to the Basic Retirement Sum, Full Retirement Sum, and CPF Life Escalating Plan payout profile.
  • GST 9% (2024+) — the GST step-up from 7% to 8% in 2023 and to 9% in 2024, with offsets via the Assurance Package.
  • Pillar Two DTT/IIR — Singapore's Domestic Top-up Tax and Income Inclusion Rule for large multinationals, implemented from 2025.

Housing + labour

  • BTO + Prime flat policy — the 2024 BTO classification framework with Prime, Plus and Standard categories, MOP rules, and subsidy clawback.
  • Work permit quotas — MOM's tightening of S-Pass, Work Permit and EP quotas across sectors.
  • ABSD stamp duty — Additional Buyer's Stamp Duty for second/third properties and foreign buyers.
  • COE auto quota — Certificate of Entitlement quota system for vehicle ownership.

Climate + monetary

  • Carbon Tax S$25→S$80 — the carbon tax progression from S$25/t (2024) to S$45 (2026-27) and S$50-80 (2030).
  • MAS S$NEER band — the Monetary Authority of Singapore's nominal effective exchange rate policy band, currently on a modest appreciation path.

Reference

  • MOF reference — what the same scenarios would yield under an alternative course (more aggressive SME relief, slower GST/CPF step-up, broader housing affordability anchor).

How to read the numbers

These are third-order modelled effects — direct cost, behavioural adjustment, secondary equilibrium — under cumulative policy. They are not forecasts. They are constructed from MOF Budget statements 2024-2026, CPF Annual Reports, MAS Quarterly Bulletins, HDB BTO Prime/Plus framework documents (2024), MOM Work Permit framework (2025), and the Carbon Tax Act revisions (2025).

What the map does show is which scenarios are net winners and which are net losers under cumulative Singapore federal policy as it stands in mid-2026 — and how the MOF reference course (a softer GST/CPF step-up, broader SME relief, deeper housing affordability anchor) shifts the picture across nearly all rows.

Singapore's policy stack is the most legible in this series. The compounding, however, is the same as everywhere else: a citizen or business reads each policy on its own and finds it manageable. Read together — as the map does — the picture changes.

What's next

The Singapore Consequence Map is published in parallel with the Swiss Consequence Map. Both are first iterations of a methodology that travels well: take a documented policy stack, plot fifteen-twenty representative scenarios against ten-twelve mechanisms, model third-order compounding, and reference against an alternative course.

The point is not to forecast anyone's 2030 result. The point is to put cumulative policy in one readable frame, before the budgets and elections that decide them.
