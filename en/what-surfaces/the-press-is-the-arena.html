Palma, 19 July 2026 · Political-sociological diagnosis · Edition 2 · Nova Democratia Edition

The press is the arena

How polarisation is manufactured before the crash — six historical precedents, five mechanisms, five countries

Jacobus van Merksteijn

  • Author --- Jacobus van Merksteijn
  • Date --- 19 July 2026, Palma, Mallorca
  • Section --- Political-sociological diagnosis · Edition 2 · Nova Democratia Edition
  • Basis --- Six historical precedents (France 1789, Russia 1917, Weimar 1932, Cambodia 1975, Iran 1979, Venezuela 2013) · Five mechanisms · Five countries
  • Sources --- Skocpol (1979), Goldstone (1991), Furet, Schama, Kershaw, Chandler, Abrahamian, Corrales; party archives CDA/PvdA/VVD 1980-2026; French mayors data 2020-2026; systematic frame analysis of Volkskrant/NRC/NOS/Le Monde/FAZ/BBC 2020-2026.

The crash is coming. That can no longer be argued away from the five-country curves. France in 2028-2031, the United Kingdom shortly after, Italy and Germany in 2033-2036, Spain last. The Netherlands sits between them with the thinnest bottom of all. But the crash itself is not the interesting subject — it has been amply substantiated by now. What is interesting is what happens between now and the crash to the people who will have to absorb it. And there we see exactly the pattern that preceded every system collapse of the last 240 years: the press, the talk-show, and the intellectual class produce polarisation rather than diagnosis, and drive apart the citizens who would otherwise be allies. This is no accident. This is precisely what France 1789, Russia 1917, Weimar 1932, Cambodia 1975, Iran 1979 and Venezuela 2013 went through — and in all six cases the winners of the crash were not those with the best diagnosis, but those with the best organisational capacity. Whoever recognises this polarisation and sees through it can prepare something. Whoever lets themselves be labelled ‘an extremist’ for saying what their parents took for granted stands alone in the end.

1. The stage is the arena, not the parliament

Someone who has not watched Dutch television for ten years and comes back sees immediately what has changed. Political decision-making has left the House of Representatives. It has moved to the studio. Op1, Nieuwsuur-panel, Buitenhof, WNL Op Zondag, Beau, De Vooravond, Sophie & Jeroen. Six chairs, two ‘camps’, a presenter who confronts rather than analyses, an applause moment for the sharpest jab. This is the arena of 2026.

Parliament is still there. Formal votes still happen. But what parliament decides is set before the debate begins — because it was already determined by who won, lost, or ‘blundered’ in last week’s talk-show. Politicians are no longer chosen on programme. They are chosen on talk-show skill. Who has the face, the fast jab, the tears at the right moment. Yesilgöz, Omtzigt, Van der Plas: their political ceiling is set by the table, not by the voter.

This has a historical precedent, and it is disturbingly precise. France 1791-1794 knew the Jacobin clubs: public halls where citizens gathered, orators spoke, and the sharpest polemicist received applause. Robespierre understood the format sooner than Danton and thereby survived three years longer — until he too was consumed by the same format. The decisions formally taken in the National Convention had long been settled in the club on the Rue Saint-Honoré. Rome under Augustus did the same thing with the arena. The Senate continued to exist, but political power was traded between bread and games.

What we now call a talk-show is the Jacobin club with thirty years of television history behind it.

2. What used to be centre is now ‘extremism’

To see this pattern you must do something forbidden in current debate: place a programme of the past next to a programme of today.

Take the Dutch Labour Party (PvdA) election programme of 1986, under Joop den Uyl, Wim Kok, Ad Melkert — a party then winning between 30 and 35 seats. What did it literally say?

  • Full employment for Dutch nationals first, with labour migration strictly regulated.
  • Protection of Dutch industry against ‘unfair competition’ — explicitly named: steel, textiles, shipbuilding.
  • Border control as a state duty, migration ‘controlled and limited’.
  • The family as cornerstone — explicitly so named — with financial support.
  • National energy policy with Groningen gas as ‘the heritage of the Dutch people’.
  • Criticism of Brussels regulation that ‘undermines national democracy’.

This is 1986. A social-democratic programme. Today: each of these six points would be dismissed in the quality press as PVV, FvD, JA21, or ‘far-right populism’. Volkskrant would run a profile on how shocking it is that a party still believes in ‘borders’. Nieuwsuur would form a panel with three opponents against one supporter. Op1 would personally destroy the leader on style.

What happened? Not that PvdA became ‘far-right’. The centre of the political spectrum shifted. What used to be the middle — a working, saving, family-forming, nationally-loyal-but-Europe-connected citizen — is now the right edge.

What used to be radical-left — Ria Beckers, Bram van Ojik, parts of PSP — is now institutional. Climate obligations without economic underpinning. Borders as moral problem rather than statecraft instrument. Virtue ethics above pragmatism. Criticism of own national history as measure of civilisation. This was radical in 1986. It is standard in Volkskrant, NRC, NOS, and most universities in 2026.

The centrist Dutchman of 1990 stands where he always stood. The field has turned around him.

This is not uniquely Dutch. France made the same turn under Mitterrand, Chirac, Sarkozy, Hollande, Macron. The classic French socialism of Mitterrand 1981 would today be Rassemblement National programme. Germany made the turn under Schröder and Merkel. The German SPD programme of 1990 would today be AfD programme on all core points — except stance on Russia.

Crucial to understand: the people voting PVV, RN, AfD, Reform, Vox today are not radically new. They are the social democrats of 1985 who did not go along with the institutional leftward shift. They stand where their parents stood. Everyone around them has moved.

3. The five mechanisms of press polarisation

The press does not go off the rails by accident. It runs on five technically identifiable mechanisms, each with historical parallels.

Mechanism 1 — The talk-panel format as polarisation machine

Six chairs, two ‘camps’, a presenter who confronts rather than analyses, an applause moment for the sharpest jab. This format does not reward the best diagnosis. It rewards the sharpest rhetoric. Those who speak most polarised win minutes. Those who nuance are interrupted.

Historical parallel: the French Jacobin clubs 1791-1794. Robespierre had no political power from cabinet positions. He had it from oratory at the club. He understood that whoever spoke most uncompromisingly received applause. Applause translated to votes in the Convention. Votes translated to decrees. Decrees translated to guillotine lists. Danton tried to nuance — he was beheaded within two months.

Op1 works identically. Whoever is sharpest at the table about Baudet, Wilders, Van der Plas, wins a return table next month. Whoever seeks nuance (there are factual problems with nitrogen measurements, there are factual problems with migration figures) is no longer invited. The format itself selects for polarisation.

Mechanism 2 — Framing asymmetry

A farmer protesting on the A28 is an ‘extremist’ ‘undermining the rule of law’. A climate activist blocking the A12 is a ‘concerned citizen’ and ‘heard voice of the future’. A citizen naming migration figures is a ‘racist’ or ‘Islamophobe’. An activist saying ‘the Netherlands has no culture’ is a ‘stimulating intellectual’ and ‘refreshing voice’. The same act — occupying public space, controversial speech — receives opposite treatment depending on which side.

Historical parallel: the German press 1848 and again 1928-1932. In Weimar, Vorwärts (social-democratic) framed communist strikers as ‘workers’ resistance’ and Catholic strikers as ‘clerical reactionism’; the Völkische Beobachter reversed this. What no newspaper did was frame workers of different convictions together as ‘citizens in crisis’. Precisely for that reason they stood against each other in 1933 rather than beside each other against Hitler.

What we see today is Weimar framing digitised. Farmer and climate activist, entrepreneur and civil servant, native and newcomer — all are systematically framed as opponents, while objectively facing the same crisis.

Mechanism 3 — The denied coherence

TenneT outages leaving whole regions without power for days. Climate Fund projects that verifiably neutralise nothing. Heat-pump obligation of €4,000 at wages that cannot bear it. Defence shortfall despite years of GDP-per-head decline. Education where 33% of school leavers are functionally illiterate. Care shortage with documentable waiting-list deaths.

Each of these topics gets its own panel. Each panel gets its own ‘two sides’. What never happens is the sum. What never happens is: this is one system breaking.

Historical parallel: Weimar 1928-1932. The Frankfurter Zeitung wrote well on hyperinflation and well on unemployment. The Berliner Tageblatt wrote well on political instability and well on industrial decline. No German newspaper brought it all together. The German citizen read the paper and thought: there are annoying problems, but individually they can be solved. Only when Hitler came to power in January 1933 did the average German understand: it was one system that broke. By then it was too late.

We are at that same point today. The Dutch public broadcaster NOS treats grid congestion, migration, climate, education, health as separate ‘dossiers’. They are not separate. They are one diagnosis: the Dutch state can no longer manage its core functions, and the cause is structural-fiscal, not incidental. This diagnosis is structurally not made in Dutch mainstream media.

Mechanism 4 — Character destruction as distraction

Every politician trying to name long-term economic coherence is answered with character investigation instead of substantive rebuttal. Baudet’s destruction was not substantively necessary — he also said factually incorrect things — but his takedown targeted his person, not his figures. Yesilgöz, Omtzigt, Van der Plas, Wilders: each currently dismissed on character, not programme. What they say about migration figures, nitrogen, EU regulation, defence — is not substantively refuted. Instead their style, tone, followers, clothing get discussed.

Historical parallel: Trotsky’s destruction 1926-1929. Trotsky wrote in 1927 an economic programme for the Soviet Union that was, in hindsight, demonstrably better than what Stalin ultimately did (slower collectivisation, more industrial import, no liquidation of the kulaks). His programme was never substantively refuted. What was done is destroy Trotsky personally: he was ‘arrogant’, ‘an intellectual’, ‘unreliable’, ‘a traitor’. His programme disappeared with his person. Russia paid with 6-8 million extra dead.

Mechanism 5 — The viewer replaces the member

Where political parties once had membership meetings with substantive motions, we now have talk-shows with chairs. Member democracy has evaporated. CDA had about 155,000 members in 1980; now 26,000. PvdA in 1985 about 96,000; now 40,000. VVD in 1990 about 65,000; now 22,000. The parties still exist but their membership base is hollowed out by 60-80%. The decision-making body is no longer the meeting, it is the talk-show table.

Viewer democracy instead of member democracy. The viewer has no voice — only confirmation or rejection of what he was already served. He may ‘participate’ via Twitter, but that is deliberately fleeting and fragmenting, not consolidating.

Historical parallel: the Roman Empire from Augustus onward. The Senate continued to exist into the 5th century, but became politically irrelevant from about 27 BC. The real political centres became the arena, the forum, the circus. Bread and games. Whoever could make the crowd cheer, won. Whoever turned the crowd against himself — Vitellius, Domitian, Elagabalus — was literally torn apart. We are there. Our arena is called Op1.

4. Six historical precedents of press polarisation before the crash

These five mechanisms are not new. They have been fully visible six times in the last 240 years in the run-up to a phase-3 system collapse. Every time in the same way. Every time with the same outcome.

4.1 France 1789-1794

The press just before: Le Père Duchesne (Hébert, ultra-radical), L’Ami du Peuple (Marat, Jacobin), Les Actes des Apôtres (royalist, mocking), Le Moniteur (semi-official). Four main streams, none delivering a diagnosis of the whole. Each framed the other side as traitors.

Who fled: about 150,000 émigrés — mainly nobility, higher clergy, upper bourgeoisie, senior officers. They rebuilt in Vienna, London, Koblenz, St Petersburg. Many did not return until after 1815.

Who stayed and opposed: about 17,000 formally guillotined, 40,000 died in Vendée and civil war, 500,000 arrested. Known names: Louis XVI and Marie-Antoinette, Danton (nuancing Jacobin), Robespierre himself in the end, Lavoisier (chemist: ‘the Republic has no need of scientists’), Malesherbes (defender of the king), Bailly (first mayor of Paris), Condorcet (philosopher, suicide in prison).

Who won: first Robespierre; then Thermidor against Robespierre; then Directory; then Napoleon. From 1789 to a workable French republic: 82 years (Third Republic 1871).

4.2 Russia 1917-1922

The press just before: Novoye Vremya (conservative), Rech (Kadets, liberal), Iskra (Menshevik), Pravda (Bolshevik), Rech of the SRs, Zemshchina (far-right). Six streams, none capable of uniting citizens against what was coming.

Who fled: 1.5-2 million ‘White emigration’ — nobility, officers, industrialists, intelligentsia, Jews, Cossacks. They rebuilt in Paris, Berlin, Belgrade, Harbin, Shanghai, New York. Most never returned.

Who stayed and opposed: about 200,000 in the Red Terror 1918-1922 directly executed; another 5-10 million dead in civil war and famine; under Stalin from 1929 another 700,000-1 million executions plus 15-20 million Gulag deaths. Known names: the Tsar’s family (July 1918), thousands of Orthodox priests, Nikolai Berdyaev (expelled 1922), Nikolai Bukharin (executed 1938), Osip Mandelstam (died in camp 1938), Isaac Babel (executed 1940), Nikolai Vavilov (died in prison 1943).

Who won: first Lenin; then Stalin; then Khrushchev; then stagnation to 1991. From 1917 to a workable Russia: not yet, after 109 years.

4.3 Weimar Germany 1918-1933

The press just before: Vorwärts (SPD), Rote Fahne (KPD), Frankfurter Zeitung (liberal), Berliner Tageblatt (liberal), Kreuzzeitung (conservative), Völkischer Beobachter (NSDAP), Stürmer (radical antisemitic). Seven streams, none delivering the shared diagnosis that the German citizen was in a systemic crisis. Each framed the other side as traitors.

Who fled: about 500,000 between 1933 and 1939 — mainly Jews, social democrats, communists, intellectuals. They rebuilt in the US, UK, Palestine, Latin America. The great names: Einstein, Mann, Adorno, Horkheimer, Fromm, Marcuse, Brecht, Feuchtwanger, Zweig, Werfel, Wilder, Lang.

Who stayed and opposed: 6 million Jews murdered in the Holocaust; about 200,000 political opponents died in camps; unknown numbers in daily repression. Known names: Bonhoeffer (executed 1945), Sophie and Hans Scholl (executed 1943), Von Stauffenberg (executed 1944), Ossietzky (died in camp 1938), Erich Mühsam (died in camp 1934).

Who won: Hitler until 1945. Then Allied occupation. West Germany emerged with Erhard 1948 — but only because American tanks stood there and Marshall Aid was available. Without external rescue there would have been no Wirtschaftswunder.

4.4 Cambodia 1970-1979

The press just before: largely military-controlled after 1970. Free press disappeared with the Lon Nol coup. Khmer Rouge propaganda from the jungle. French press out of Phnom Penh reaching only foreigners.

Who fled: about 300,000 between 1975 and 1979, mainly to Thailand and later US/France/Australia. Large numbers had fled before 1975 due to civil war.

Who stayed and opposed: about 1.5-2 million out of 7.5 million — 20-25% of the entire population. Every Cambodian with secondary education, every civil servant from before 1975, every Buddhist monk, every doctor, every teacher, every ethnic Vietnamese or Chinese Cambodian. Wearing glasses was cause for execution. Known names: Sirik Matak (executed 1975), Long Boret (executed 1975), Hu Nim (executed 1977), Hou Yuon (disappeared 1975), Prince Sisowath Sirimatak (executed 1975).

Who won: Pol Pot until 1979. Then Vietnamese occupation. Cambodia is still not recovered — the knowledge class was lost for a generation.

4.5 Iran 1978-1985

The press just before: Kayhan (regime-loyal), Ettela’at (liberal), Ayandegan (secular-left), plus religious pamphlets from Qom and Najaf, plus tapes of Khomeini mass-copied. Four to five main streams, none making the shared diagnosis that a revolution was coming in which none of these streams would survive.

Who fled: 3-5 million between 1979 and 1985 — mainly secular middle class, monarchists, communists, Bahá’í, Jews, Armenians. They rebuilt in Los Angeles (‘Tehrangeles’), Toronto, Paris, London.

Who stayed and opposed: about 4,000-10,000 executions in the first two years; tens of thousands imprisoned; another thousands in the 1988 mass executions. Known names: Amir Abbas Hoveyda (Shah’s prime minister, executed 1979), thousands of army officers, Sadegh Ghotbzadeh (revolutionary turned unloyal, executed 1982), Sadegh Sharafkandi (Kurdish leader, murdered 1992 in Berlin).

Who won: Khomeini until 1989. Then Khamenei. Iran is 47 years later still not workable; there have been active protests against the regime since 2022.

4.6 Venezuela 1998-present

The press just before: El Universal (liberal-conservative), El Nacional (mainstream), Últimas Noticias (popular), Panorama (regional), plus TV stations Globovisión (critical) and Venevisión (mainstream). Diverse press, but in 1998 largely against Chávez — and thus easily labelled ‘oligarchy’ by Chávez himself.

Who fled: 7.7 million by 2024 — the largest refugee flow in the Western Hemisphere ever. Large share of middle class, professionals, entrepreneurs. To Colombia, Peru, Chile, US, Spain, Italy, Netherlands.

Who stayed and opposed: about 10,000 documented political murders; tens of thousands of political prisoners; complete opposition systematically dismantled. Known names: Leopoldo López (imprisoned 2014-2020), Juan Guaidó (exiled 2023), Óscar Pérez (killed 2018), María Corina Machado (currently underground).

Who won: Chávez until 2013. Maduro since. Venezuela has lost 80% of its GDP since 2013. Not yet recovered.

5. What the six precedents prove together

Put the six cases side by side and the pattern jumps out:

  • First: in all six cases there was, just before the crash, a fragmented press unable to deliver a shared diagnosis. There were four to seven main streams, all with their own framing, all framing the others as enemies.
  • Second: in all six cases those who could afford it fled. They saved their lives and their capital, but their rebuilding abroad had nothing to do with the country of origin. They did not contribute to reconstruction.
  • Third: in all six cases the stayers who opposed were either murdered, or imprisoned, or used as martyrs by the regime. Not one of these six cases led to a situation in which the stayers-who-opposed could implement their vision as a group.
  • Fourth: in all six cases reconstruction came either from outside (Germany 1948 via America), or from a small group who had prepared something before the crash (ordoliberals in Switzerland, Solidarność-analogues in the Iranian diaspora), or not at all (Cambodia, Russia, Venezuela — still not).
  • Fifth and crucial: in all six cases the phase-2 endgame was marked by the exact five mechanisms we see today. What we see today in the Netherlands, France, Germany, UK, Italy, Spain is not ‘just a polarising time’. It is the diagnostic early signal that has preceded phase-3 six times in the last 240 years.

6. What we see now — five countries, one pattern

Based on these six precedents we can indicate per country how advanced the press-polarisation machine is.

France (2026)

Comparable to Weimar 1929. Four major press streams (Le Monde / Libération / Le Figaro / Valeurs Actuelles), plus talk-show culture on CNews, BFM, TF1. Bardella at 43%. Four prime ministers fallen in twelve months. Mayors resigning under threat: 1000+ since 2020. The crash comes per the curve analysis in 2028-2031. France is in phase-2-end. The window to prepare closes within 24-30 months.

United Kingdom (2026)

Comparable to Weimar 1930. Tabloid culture (Sun, Mail) against quality press (Guardian, Times), plus BBC panel shows. Reform UK at 27.6%. NEET youth at 1,012,000. The crash comes in 2029-2032. Phase-2-mid. Window: 30-40 months.

Netherlands (2026)

This is special. Dutch press polarisation is the most developed form of talk-show democracy in the world. Op1, Nieuwsuur, WNL, Buitenhof, Sophie & Jeroen, Renze, Beau. Daily polarisation production. Very strong framing asymmetry. Character destruction of Baudet, Wilders, Yesilgöz, Omtzigt, Van der Plas. Party membership bases 60-80% evaporated since 1990. The crash comes per the curves in 2032-2033. The Netherlands looks fiscally strongest, but the bottom is thinnest. Window: 60-72 months — but the bottom is too thin to build anything on after the crash. Action is thus more urgent now than the longer timeline suggests.

Italy (2026)

Less advanced. Meloni coalition stable. Press polarised (Corriere / Repubblica / Il Giornale / Libero) but not with the same intensity. Family-business culture still present. The crash comes in 2033-2036, but Italy still has real bottom in Emilia-Romagna. Window: 78 months.

Germany (2026)

Subtler polarisation. ARD/ZDF behave differently from Dutch talk-shows — more analysis, less pure emotion. But AfD normalisation is accelerating. Zeitungskrise (papers closing in smaller cities). Ordoliberal tradition still present. The crash comes in 2033-2036. Germany still has an industrial memory layer. Window: 78 months.

Spain (2026)

Least advanced. Sánchez wobbles, Vox at 17%, but polarisation is regional (Catalonia, Basque Country) more than press-technical. The crash comes only in 2035-2038. Window: 102 months.

7. What the citizen who sees through this can do

If you read this and think: this fits, then there is one thing you must NOT do: join the arena. Do not sit at the table. Do not tweet against the framing. Do not try to win the talk-show.

What does work is based on what historically worked in West Germany 1948, Ireland 1987, South Korea 1998:

First — you refuse to be categorised

When the press tries to file you as ‘extremist’ for saying what your parents took for granted, you refuse to be filed. You do not become PVV because you are told so. You do not become radical because a presenter says so. You stand where your parents stood. Others have moved.

Second — you build the landing place

Four layers — governance (Sark, Balearics, Nova Democratia), productive (juncao, hydrolysate, ethanol, botanical spirit, CO2-at-source), knowledge (outside universities, via masters and documentation), network (people who acted before the crisis). Each must exist as a running demonstration, not a concept, within the next 24-72 months. Openvizier.org is one of the places where the knowledge layer is built. Nova Democratia with VMB-EGS is the governance layer. The juncao-Moringa line is the productive layer. The network is you — if you read this and act.

Third — you recognise the allies

The farmer protesting nitrogen and the entrepreneur naming the housing crisis and the pensioner seeing care collapse and the teacher seeing PISA fall and the parent seeing the energy bill — these are not opponents. The press wants you to see them as opponents. That is Mechanism 2: framing asymmetry. These people see the same crisis from four different sides. They are your allies. If you do not recognise them as such, the Netherlands repeats Weimar 1932.

Fourth — you act before the crash, not after

Historically there is no case in which stayers-who-opposed effectively implemented their vision after the crash. Cambodia, Russia, Weimar, France, Iran, Venezuela: in each, the stayer-who-opposed was either dead, imprisoned, or used as a martyr. Act now, in the last 24 to 84 months in which it is still possible.

Closing thesis

The press is the arena. The talk-show is the modern Jacobin club. The five mechanisms are not new — they are 240 years old and have been applied six times before. In all six cases the fragmented press led to a citizenry that no longer recognised its allies, did not act coordinated, and absorbed the crash unprepared. In all six cases the winners were not those with the best diagnosis, but those with the best organisational capacity.

The crash comes. The question is not whether you can stop the polarisation — you cannot, it has become institutional. The question is whether you can see through the polarisation, and whether in the remaining 24 to 84 months you can help build a landing place where something new can land when the old has finally stopped.

The people you see in today’s talk-show — the sharpest jab wins — are Robespierre at his best moment. In fourteen months Robespierre himself stands under the blade. That is how this format works.

Whoever plays the game, loses. Whoever sees through the game and builds something else, may yet pass on what the others did not survive.
