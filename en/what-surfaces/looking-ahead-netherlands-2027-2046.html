Looking ahead: the plan for the Netherlands 2027–2046

Control-theoretic diagnosis, control system, zero-base tax system, six policy levers, the yield per four-year period and comparison with the system before 1970. As at October 2026.

Core

  • Problem. The Netherlands pays €77.9 in taxes, contributions, pension and taxes included in prices for every €100 of labour costs. A worker sees about 30% of that on a tax assessment. Citizens ask the government to provide services without seeing the price; politicians take them on because a hidden burden costs few votes. The loop is open.

  • Diagnosis. In the EU-15 control-theoretic model, the rise in the tax burden on labour after 1970 accounts for 3 to 10.7 percentage points of the shortfall in GDP per capita relative to the US in 2004. This is a model result, not a proven cause.

  • Intervention. An outer loop with a horizon of 20 years, hard constraints, a zero-base recalculated every four years, and the learning rule x(k+1) = x(k) + 0.2 · (z(k) − x(k)). Six levers close the loop: visibility, a price tag for each task, work and dismissal, sickness, doctors and administration, and a budget for each task.

  • Yield from the levers. Businesses save €1.5 to €5.3 billion a year in 2030 (midpoint €3.6 billion), rising to €4.6 to €11.4 billion in 2046 (midpoint €8.5 billion). The collective savings from the levers themselves remain small: €0.00 to €0.56 billion in 2046.

  • The total. With corrected Swiss expenditure (36.6% of GDP) as the benchmark, net government spending falls by 4.9 percentage points of GDP over 20 years, about €61 billion a year. The chain then falls to about €67.4.

  • Back in time. On this path, the collective tax burden falls below the level of 1970 in 2034 and, from 2038, is lower than in any year since 1970. In 2046 it reaches roughly the level of 1967. The allocation of responsibility for sickness also returns to the system from before 1994. In two respects, the design goes further rather than returning to the past: dismissal without review existed only before 1940, and healthcare, the AOW and the successor to WAO remain.

  • Social consequences. Workers keep €22.1 for every €100 of labour costs; in 2046, this is about €32.6 on this path. The state taking responsibility for sickness removes the incentive to select on the basis of health. The risks lie with a WW at minimum level without broad supplementary provision, with a low basic payment in the first week of sickness, and with informal care, which can no longer be carried by the family model of the sixties.

  • Everyone takes part. Everyone takes part according to their ability, including people who do not currently work: 413,000 people on social assistance, 838,000 receiving disability benefit and 1.2 million people with untapped work potential. Three pathways (work immediately, work according to ability, protected) with one rule: people keep at least 50 cents of every additional euro they earn. Obligations and sanctions apply only when a suitable offer is made.

  • Young people. Youth policy follows De Kosaris, Book Three: the state does not bring children up, but restores three things that have disappeared. Time for one present adult in the first seven years, places outside the home where a child can encounter challenges, and age-appropriate mistakes between 14 and 21: a regular workshop day from 14, apprentice, journeyman, master.

  • Accelerated variant. With 2/3 instead of 1/5 in the first two periods, 67% of the spending gap has been bridged in 2030 and 89% in 2034 (baseline: 20% and 36%). The average worker keeps about €6,300 more per year in 2030. This requires gross spending to fall by €73.6 billion in 2027–2030; if it falls short, a deficit of up to €30 billion a year arises in 2030.

  • The three pathways financially. For the government, the three pathways yield a net +€0.5 billion over 20 years in the middle scenario (range −€25.1 to +€44.7 billion). They bring almost nothing extra into the export core (NEPK). In the middle scenario, the burden on what the core brings in does not fall (−€3.6 billion over 20 years): pathway 1 and reassessment reduce it, while programmes in pathway 2 increase it, unless they raise the chance of work by at least about 19%. The assessment process is the bottleneck, with about 5 times the current number of UWV assessments.

  • Condition. The path requires spending to fall as quickly as the tax burden and the effects of ageing (about 1.1 percentage points of GDP every four years) to be offset. Gross, that is €21 to €32 billion per period. Without that spending reduction, the result is limited to the yield from the levers.

Types of figures

  • Measured or sourced: directly from a cited source, with year.

  • Source model: an estimate or model result from another institution, such as the CPB.

  • Reconstructed: calculated by me from cited source figures; the calculation is shown.

  • Own calculation or model result: from my own model or calculations, with inputs stated.

  • Assumption or design choice: a calculation choice or zero-base value without measurement; always stated alongside the calculation.

  • Scenario: a result that depends on assumptions; always given with a low-to-high range.

Part A — Diagnosis

The European shortfall as a control problem

The first model follows the EU-15 relative to the US from 1950 to 2004 (levels as percentages of the US, purchasing power parities 2002, van Ark, O'Mahony and Timmer 2008) using four stocks: hours, employment, knowledge and capital. The fiscal feedback loop: higher unemployment requires higher taxes, and higher taxes raise equilibrium unemployment. Using the source values, loop gain G = 0.30 to 0.57 (upper bound). The loop is stable, but a shock is amplified by 1.4 to 2.3 times.

Time constants determine what you see after four years: hours respond within a few years, a knowledge gap closes in about 15 years, and capital adjusts with a time constant of about 25 years. A disturbance from the seventies is therefore visible well beyond 2000. A government with a four-year horizon is thus steering a flow, while prosperity resides in stocks.

Why it rises

  • Hidden burdens. After electronic toll payment was introduced, toll rates in the US were 20 to 40% higher (Finkelstein 2009). The less visible a charge is, the more easily it rises.

  • Healthcare and ageing. With unchanged policy, collective healthcare spending rises from almost 11% to over 18% of GDP in 2060 (CPB, source model). In 1960 it was 1% of GDP (ESB). If that growth is paid for through taxes, the chain rises to about €92 (own calculation, proportionality assumption).

  • Rules and forms. 31% of working time in healthcare and social care is spent on administration, 36% in general practices and hospitals (CBS 2025). Tax burdens cost SMEs €5.53 billion a year (Panteia 2024).

The learning rule

x(k+1) = x(k) + 0.2 · (z(k) − x(k))

x is the setting, z the zero-base for period k. Every four years, the zero-base is drawn up again using the measurements from that period. Settings without budget risk, such as information and procedures, are switched over all at once in the first period.

The zero-base tax system

  • Labour: one rate of 40% on all income per person. Two flat tax credits instead of nine. Allowances withdrawn at a combined rate of no more than 10%.

  • Profit: corporation tax of 19% on all profits; Box 2 25.9%, so distributed profits also bear 40%. Retained profits bear only 19%.

  • Capital: actual returns only, on realisation: interest and rent 40%, dividends and share gains 25.9%.

  • Implementation: mandatory e-invoicing and pre-completed tax returns; the Tax and Customs Administration calculates payroll tax and contributions from the payroll return.

The 40% follows from the fiscal key table 2026: at 39.6%, the static effects are budget-neutral (own calculation, keys cannot be added together). If sickness, WIA and WW are transferred in full to general revenue, approximately €60 billion shifts from labour costs to taxation and the rate comes to around 51%. That is the same burden, now visible. Only a smaller volume reduces it.

Part C — The six levers

Figure 3. On the left, the current system with an open loop; on the right, the new system with annual accounts, a price tag and a learning rule.

Lever 1: visibility

Every citizen receives one annual account for each type of tax: what they paid, including what their employer and prices pay on their behalf, and what it was spent on. Visible share of the chain: from approximately 30% to 100%, all at once in the first period.

Lever 2: a price tag for each task

Every proposal for a new government task states its annual cost per household and the associated rules and forms. That amount appears on the annual account.

Lever 3: work and dismissal

Dismissal is possible any day, without a transition payment and without a dismissal review, except for the prohibition on dismissal on grounds of discrimination, pregnancy or sickness. The WW is a state responsibility with its own budget based on the minimum wage; anyone who wants more takes out private insurance. The employer does not contribute. In 2020–2022, employers paid a total of €13.4 billion in transition payments, averaging €4.47 billion per year (SEOR, evaluation Wab, from CBS microdata).

A private top-up requires a waiting period of 6 to 12 months, exclusion of announced redundancies, and a register of the WW history. Without such rules, it is difficult for a market to emerge (Hendren, NBER); with exclusions and shared data, it can be profitable (J. Public Econ 2023).

Buffer. Reconstruction from the UWV time series (ongoing benefits at year-end multiplied by the average annual benefit): €5.0 billion in 2010, rising to €8.4 billion in 2015, €4.2 billion in 2019; average €6.3 billion. With a fixed budget set at that average, the cumulative difference grows to a deficit of €4.3 billion and a surplus of €2.5 billion. The buffer must therefore be €4 to €7 billion.

Lever 4: sickness

The state pays a basic benefit. During the first week, a person reports themselves sick. A top-up follows only if an independent doctor appointed by the state determines that it is a genuine illness, with a reassessment date for each diagnosis (for example, 1 or 3 months) and reassessment by another doctor appointed by lot.

Volume. Mandatory medical substantiation after 8 weeks reduced sickness absence in Norway by 23 to 24%. In the Netherlands, the Gatekeeper Improvement Act reduced inflow into WAO by 30 to 40% (Koning and Lindeboom 2015), affecting long-term absence but not short-term absence. The Netherlands therefore already has some of the Norwegian type of monitoring; the design assumes 0 to half of the Norwegian effect.

Lever 5: doctors and administration

The Netherlands has about 770 insurance doctors; UWV is short of more than 200 doctors each year (Nivel, House of Representatives). At the top of the pay scale, an insurance doctor earns about €58 per hour, a medical specialist €79 (UWV, AMS 2025; own conversion). Simple reassessments are assigned to social medical nurses, and there will be one digital form per diagnosis. Administration in healthcare takes 31% of working time; the target is 20%; the learning rule gives 28.8% in 2030.

Lever 6: budget per task

Each task is given a budget for four years. An overspend is not silently added to the tariff, but shifts the zero-base for that task for the next period. Total expenditure is carried along in an outer loop: 1/5 of the difference from the target per period.

  • Continued pay. €20.0 billion in 2023 (TNO 2025). Distribution of absence days from one table (KWI, table 2.5): 16.5% in the first week, 68.9% in the first six months, 86.2% in the first year. One possible explanation is that the ministry includes employers' costs; this has not been confirmed. The figures are therefore more likely to be too low than too high.

  • Occupational health service. 8.6 million employees × €100 to €200 per year (MKB Servicedesk) less assessments: 51% of employees were absent in 2023 (TNO), 29.4% of cases last longer than a week (CBS 2025), two assessments at €175 to €250 (Arbolink price list). People and cases are mixed here; the number of assessments is more likely to be too low.

  • Transition payment. €13.412 billion in 2020–2022 (SEOR, from CBS microdata), so €4.47 billion per year; in 2030 1/5 of that. The state already compensates €398 million per year for long-term sickness (SZW 2024); that compensation will also end.

  • Tax burdens. €5.53 billion in tax burdens for SMEs (Panteia 2024). Assumption: pre-filled tax returns and e-invoices reduce them by 25 to 50% (point of reference: e-invoices reduced the burden of the invoicing requirement by about 36%). Additional costs for the Tax Administration have not been estimated.

  • Collective. Sickness Benefit safety net €3.175 billion (UWV 2026), volume effect only. WW: €4.469 billion over 170,500 benefit-years (UWV 2026); the distribution by daily wage has not been published and has been assumed (lognormal, spread 0.35 to 0.65). The lower cap affects virtually nobody in 2030; the higher benefit (71%) costs about the same.

  • Not added together. Employers paid €2.0 billion in premiums for sickness absence insurance in 2020 (DNB). Most of this reimburses continued pay and is already included in the €20 billion; the cost and margin component is unknown and has not been added.

What the figures show

  • Full employment had a different cause. Unemployment of 0.4 to 0.8% in 1961–1970 coincided with a labour shortage after post-war reconstruction (CBS). The burden level was not the only cause. A lower burden level now will therefore not, by itself, bring that labour market back.

  • Poverty then belongs to the system from before 1965. In 1965, an estimated 25 to 45% of the population were poor; older people often lived on support from family or poor relief. The AOW (1957) and the General Assistance Act (1965) changed that. In 2024, 3.1% are poor according to a different measure, and older people are the least likely to be poor because of the AOW (SCP). The plan retains AOW and social assistance. The poverty of 1965 will therefore not return.

  • Low burdens rested on a different kind of family. The low burden level of 1960 rested on a family in which the man worked and the woman stayed at home to provide care: 6.8% of married women were in paid work. Now 64% of women work. The care for older and sick people that was provided at home then must now be provided by someone else, or by those same women alongside their work.

  • High burdens went hand in hand with equality. The burden level rose from 1970 to 1987 mainly because of benefits that were only then being built up: the WAO (1967), the AAW (1976) and the linking of the AOW to the minimum wage. In 1977 the burden was 41.1% of GDP and inequality was low (Gini 0.240). A high burden level and low inequality thus went hand in hand; redistribution roughly halves the Gini now, from 0.54 for primary income to 0.29 for disposable income (CBS, 2022).

What the system from before 1994 did socially

The plan returns the division of responsibilities for illness to the system from before 1994. That system has been well documented, and it teaches us three things.

  • Shifting the burden onto the collective. The number of people receiving incapacity-for-work benefits rose from 133,000 (1960), via 295,000 (1970), to over 900,000 in 1991 (AWVN). Sickness absence rose from about 6% in 1962 to 10% in 1978–1979 (UWV). The parliamentary Buurmeijer inquiry (1993) found that employers and trade unions used the WAO as a way to shed older and less productive workers during reorganisations, and that the assessment system worked very inadequately (Parlement.com).

  • What this means for the design. The cause was not that the state paid, but that the parties carrying out the assessments had an interest of their own: social partners ran the industry associations. Under the plan, an independent state doctor carries out the assessment, a different doctor conducts the reassessment, and an employer pays a surcharge if its inflow into the WIA is high. The latter two measures, in particular, prevent a repeat.

  • Less health-based selection. After sickness risk was privatised, more employers selected on the basis of health: from 15% of companies in 1993 to 25% in 1995, while the share requiring pre-employment medical examinations rose from 43 to 57% (University of Amsterdam). If sickness risk returns to the state, that incentive disappears. People with chronic illnesses and older workers are therefore expected to find it easier to get hired. The scale has not been measured.

  • Working while ill. The lower sickness absence after 1994 is partly a genuine gain and partly a shifted burden. If employees drag themselves to work while ill because they receive less pay, they infect others. In the US, flu rates fell by 5 to 40% in cities that introduced sick pay; in Germany, a reduction led to more infectious presenteeism (Pichler and Ziebarth 2017). Under the plan, everyone receives a basic amount during the first week of 75% (middle) or 50% (high). At 50%, this risk is greatest, especially for low-paid workers in the service sector.

Dismissal and income during unemployment

Dismissal without review existed before 1940. Its social consequences depend on what happens after the dismissal.

  • What easier dismissal does. The OECD finds that employment protection has little effect on the level of employment, but does affect flows: fewer dismissals, but also fewer hires and less voluntary departure. Strict employment protection pushes companies towards temporary contracts; in Italy it accounts for approximately 20% of temporary work (OECD 2013). Without dismissal review and a transition payment, that incentive disappears, along with part of the flexible workforce.

  • Security lies in the benefit, not in the job. Denmark has little employment protection, but a WW that replaces on average about 80% of net income, especially for lower incomes, and actively helps people find new work (IMF; Social Policy & Administration). Danish workers therefore feel secure: 44% strongly disagreed with ‘my job is secure’, compared with an average of 70% in the OECD (FAOS). Countries with little employment protection and a low benefit, such as the US, fall into the group with little security (Andersen and Svarer).

  • Where the plan stands. The plan combines little employment protection with a WW at the level of the minimum wage. That is not Danish but American, unless the private supplement is taken out widely. Anyone who does not take the supplement falls back to the minimum when dismissed.

Responsibility and informal care

The plan returns responsibility to citizens and businesses. In the sixties, families, churches and around ten thousand social work organisations bore a large part of that responsibility. That safety net is now smaller.

  • Fewer people providing care. In 2024, 14% of those aged 18 to 75 provided informal care, fewer than in 2016, and the proportion of heavily burdened informal carers increased (CBS). The number of recipients of informal care is projected to rise by about 70% from 2018 to 2040, while the number of providers rises by less than 7% (SCP).

  • Little saving. Informal care does not reduce total public care costs; it only shifts the type of care (research for the House of Representatives, 2026). Anyone who shifts tasks from the state back to the family therefore saves little public money and places the burden on people who often also work.

  • Implications for the plan. Care has no separate phase-out measure in this plan. That decision must be made separately, using the burden on informal carers as the measure.

What workers take home

Of every €100 in labour costs, a worker now keeps €22.1 for personal spending. On the path set out in this plan, that is €25.2 in 2030 and €32.6 in 2046: 47% more per euro of labour costs. This applies only if the reduction in the burden falls entirely to workers and spending really does fall. Own calculation.

That is the plan’s most important social gain. Those who work keep more and can see what they pay. The risk falls on those who cannot work or lose their job without a supplement: their benefit falls to the minimum, while the burden on workers falls.

The carrot: what everyone concretely gets in return

Nothing changes without an incentive. An incentive works only if four things are right: the reward is concrete in euros, it is personally visible, it comes quickly, and it is guaranteed. A promise of ‘a better economy in 2046’ does not meet those conditions. The reward is therefore set out below in euros, by group and by period. The amounts are own calculations based on the path in this plan; the guarantees are design choices.

The core of the reward

Every euro saved by the zero-base goes back to whoever contributed it, as a lower rate or lower labour costs, and appears on the annual statement as an amount of their own. It may not go towards new spending. That is the release rule. Without that rule, the reward is not credible, because savings would then disappear quietly into a new task.

A politician now has an incentive to conceal costs: hidden costs rise faster (Finkelstein 2009). The plan turns that around. With the annual statement, the reduction in costs per household becomes a visible result of a government. Whoever reduces costs gets recognition at the election; whoever wants a new task must state its price per household.

  • No free money. The €6,400 is not extra money from nowhere. It is the price of collective tasks that are discontinued or become cheaper, returned to those who paid for them.

  • Not the same amount for everyone. Those who do not work, or lose their job without private supplementation, do not receive a reward from lower costs but a benefit at the minimum level. For this group, the incentive to work is greater, but so is the risk. See the chapter on Social consequences.

  • Conditional. The amounts apply only if expenditure falls at the pace set out in the path. If it does not, the result is limited to the proceeds from the policy levers for employers and a much smaller reward for workers.

What the research says

  • Participation works for those who can work. In Denmark, the threat of a compulsory programme increased the rate at which men left benefits by 25%, as much as halving the benefit, with no evidence of worse jobs (quasi-experiment, Denmark). Compulsory activation with a reduction for refusal led to 30% more people returning to work (Graversen and Van Ours). Moderate sanctions also increase the transition to work (Svarer 2011).

  • Coercion harms those who cannot work. Among long-term social assistance recipients in Denmark, additional activation after 10 years had no effect on employment. Antidepressant use was 35% higher and 24% more disability pensions were awarded than in the control group (Aarhus University, 2026). Coercion without appropriate help harms this group.

  • What has been measured in the Netherlands. In the Dutch social assistance experiments, exempting people from the job-search requirement did not reduce the rate of leaving benefits; more intensive guidance and a higher earnings disregard sometimes led to more partial exits, but nowhere to more full-time work (CPB). What is effective above all is intensive personal contact, temporary wage subsidies for those far from work, and sanctions for those close to it; for people with a serious mental health condition, workplace support works consistently (IPS) (CPB/SCP).

  • The poverty trap. A sole breadwinner on social assistance who takes a job at the minimum wage is 5% better off; a single mother is 13% better off (House of Representatives, via ESB). A single person earning the minimum hourly wage keeps an average of 34 cents from each additional euro when working between 27 and 38 hours (government annex on marginal tax rates). Asking someone to work when it pays so little is the weakest point of the current system; reintegration coaches say so themselves (parliamentary paper 31322).

  • What does not work. A wage-cost subsidy for hiring older people has no demonstrable effect (CPB). What holds employers back is mainly the risk in the event of illness. Under this plan, that risk shifts to the state.

Youth policy: Book Three as its foundation

The foundation of youth policy is De Kosaris, Book Three: The Book of Anthea (Het Open Vizier). The book describes how a person is brought up to become someone capable of exercising their own judgement. It follows the layers in the order in which they emerge: seven days, seven months, seven years, fourteen, twenty-one, and seventy-seven years before what one person has learned returns in someone who did not know them. Each layer can emerge only once the layer beneath it has solidified.

  • Source: De Kosaris — Book Three — The Book of Anthea: https://openvizier.org/nl/leesboek-boek-drie/

What the book establishes

  • The lowest layer. "The upper layer changes through conversation. The lowest layer changes over seven years, and never afterwards." In those seven years, the four pillars are not taught but experienced: someone comes when I call; what is said is consistent with what happens; I may break something and am still welcome; someone thinks I am worth spending time on.

  • The second line. The line of capability runs to about fourteen. The line of judgement runs from fourteen to twenty-one and is learned only "by getting it wrong for seven years in things that matter a little".

  • Mistakes in proportion. "A mistake should cost enough to hurt and little enough to be put right." Punishment teaches concealment, nothing teaches indifference, consequences teach you to bear them.

  • Why the line now comes late. "We have filled the years in which a person is supposed to get things wrong with learning." The second line then begins around twenty-five, "with a family and a debt and a job", and then "there is no mistake that is proportionate".

  • Places beyond the home. In the past, the street, the field and the workshop, "where a fourteen-year-old boy stood alongside a man", put things right outside the home. "They are gone, nothing has taken their place, and so it all falls on the parent. One person cannot do that."

  • The screen. A screen takes away three things at once: depth, scale and time. "Everything at half a metre, everything the same size, everything now." A child among screens has no measure beyond themselves.

  • The final three dimensions. Magnitude, value and number are not developed by schooling but taken away: everything goes through the same counter, value becomes whatever fits on the report, and there is a sense of one but not of a thousand.

  • The only political point in the book. "When a society makes its houses so expensive that both parents have to work, it has decided how its children are brought up. That decision was never made."

  • The limits of policy. "This book cannot be implemented." "You cannot bring up children. You can only be someone yourself, with a child present, for long enough."

Anthea herself says where she has not measured it. For two of the three children she guided, the book held true; for one, it did not. That remains in this chapter: the book is a foundation, not a measured effect.

What that means for the state

The Afterword establishes the limit: the state cannot bring up children and cannot prescribe how they are brought up. What the state can do is restore three things that the book describes as having disappeared: time for one present person in the first seven years, places beyond the home where a child encounters another child and the world, and proportionate mistakes between fourteen and twenty-one. Youth policy therefore consists of conditions, not prescriptions. All the measures below are design choices derived from the book; none has been measured for its effect.

The workshop from 14 to 21

This is the heart of youth policy. The book describes the old sequence “apprentice at seven, working independently at fourteen, master at twenty-one” as something that “emerged in workshops, through trial and error, over hundreds of years”. The workshop provides what school does not: an immediate judgement, as the sea judged “not gently but immediately”, and a mistake that hurts but can be put right.

  • One day a week. A fixed day each week from 14, in every type of school, with an employer or in a school workshop. The tradesperson assesses the work, not a mark given afterwards.

  • Apprentice, journeyman, master. Apprentice from 14, journeyman as soon as the tradesperson approves their work, master around 21 when someone can assess another person. Pay follows the step, not age alone.

  • A first mistake that can be put right. Anyone who starts something before 21 and fails does not end up with lifelong debt. A first venture gets a simple legal form with a cap on personal liability. This is the eighteen-year-old boy in the book who lost two years and started again at twenty-four.

  • No barrier for the employer. For employers, an apprentice or journeyman does not count towards sickness risk (which, under the plan, is borne by the state) and is not covered by dismissal protection until 21. The plan removes the barriers that currently keep young people out of the workshop.

  • For the productive core. The workshop brings craftsmanship back to industry: precisely factor α (industry) of the productive core. The amount has not been measured.

The screen

The book does not say that screens should be banned. It says that a screen takes away depth, size and time, and that a child needs in their place a fixed point of reference outside themselves: the pier, the church tower, the width of the street. The research supports this approach more than a ban. An initial study of the smartphone ban in schools finds little difference in mental health; one researcher describes screens as an accelerant for underlying pressure (Nieuwsblad, 2026). Problematic social media use in the Netherlands is half as high as internationally, while complaints among girls rose just as sharply (SCP, HBSC). The policy therefore restores what the screen takes away: the outdoors, the ground, animals and the workshop. In the classroom, screens are used only for something that lies beyond the screen.

Parents and homes

The book does not blame parents. “There is no generation that knew.” It identifies one political cause: expensive housing that forces both parents to work, leaving the child “with five people” and giving them five names for one feeling. The plan gives working people more money to spend as they wish (the tangible reward): in the baseline scenario €1,890 a year in 2030 and €6,354 in 2046, and in the accelerated scenario around €6,300 as early as 2030. Provided that this room is not swallowed up by higher house prices, it makes a single income possible again during the first seven years. The housing market itself is not part of the plan.

Why it pays for itself

The book is honest that there will be no proof, “because it takes seventy-seven years”. What can be calculated is the cost of the second line not emerging. Applications for the WIA among people in their twenties rose between 2018 and 2025 by 84% (men) and 121% (women), mainly because of mental health problems. Someone who enters the WGA at twenty-five receives the average annual benefit of €22,958 (UWV, 2025) for about forty years, until AOW: approximately €0.92 million per person, undiscounted (own calculation). Every 1,000 young people per year who do not enter reduce the burden on productive earning capacity after ten years by about €230 million a year, and after forty years by about €0.92 billion a year. That is 3.3% of the NEPK of 2022.

How it is monitored

The book warns: “Anyone bringing up a child sees nothing.” The council therefore measures not upbringing, but whether the conditions are in place, and tracks outcomes with the delay described in the book.

  • Conditions, annually. Number of young people aged 14 to 21 with a regular workshop day; traffic-free play space in each neighbourhood; schools with a plot of land of their own; households with a child under 7 and one income.

  • Outcomes, with a delay. Entry into the WIA and the Wajong among those under 30, by diagnosis; young people not in work or education (6.1% of 18–24-year-olds in 2025); psychosomatic complaints in the HBSC youth survey. These will not respond for at least seven years.

  • What does not add up. As Book Two prescribes, cases that do not work out are recorded separately and not omitted. The plan's learning rule fits the book: “A layer is not complete when it works. It is complete when it lasts.” A measure does not stop the moment it appears to work.

  • The limit. What appears in this table is a condition. The book says: as soon as you do it because it is written here, you are not doing it. The state provides places and time; what happens there is left to the people who are there.

  • Less room for error. At g = 2/3, the path overshoots as soon as a step has 1.5 times the planned effect, and becomes unstable at 3 times. At g = 1/5, this only happens at 5 and 10 times.   • Deficit if expenditure falls short. If expenditure achieves only half the step while charges fall in full, a deficit of about €30.0 billion per year (2.4 percentage points of GDP) arises in 2030, and one of €40.0 billion in 2034. That is why the reduction in charges each year must wait until the expenditure reduction achieved in the previous year is known.

  • Delay. Investment and behaviour respond to lower charges only after several years. In 2/3, the second zero-base is set before the effect of the first is visible. The measurement in the stock account must therefore be annual, not every four years.

  • Implementation. The tracks are not part of the learning rule. In the baseline, assessments already require about 5 times the current UWV capacity. A faster reduction in expenditure on WW, sickness and WIA increases the pressure on assessments and support in the first period.

What it does for the core

Only through the charges, and only arithmetically: if τ in the factor contract falls by as many points as the tax burden in the plan (an assumption; the definitions differ), with E_tv, α and φ held equal, the NEPK goes from 2.79% of GDP to 3.01% in 2030 and 3.10% in 2046. In the baseline, that is 2.86% and 3.02%. Accelerating brings the yield forward, but the ceiling remains about +11%. More requires higher α and φ: industry and ownership returning.

Financial model of the three tracks

This chapter models the three tracks year by year, from 2027 through 2046. Amounts are in millions of euros, in constant 2026 prices and undiscounted. The calculation model is an Excel workbook with formulas (Financial model of the three tracks); every input value is listed there with its unit, type, source and URL. What the core brings in

What matters is what the economy earns from outside: the export-linked productive core. According to the fixed definitions, this is NTPK = E_tv × α × (1 − τ), and the share in Dutch hands is NEPK = NTPK × φ. For 2022, this gives €40.7 billion NTPK and €27.8 billion NEPK (GDP 2022 × factors, reconstructed). GDP also includes everything transacted domestically, including imputed rent, calculated bank services and government services valued at cost; none of that brings anything in from outside.

Benefits are also paid out of the core. In 2022, this amounted to €154.7 billion in cash benefits to households (CBS according to the fixed definitions), 5.6 times the NEPK. The tracks are therefore measured in two ways. First: do they bring more in? Second: do they reduce the burden on what the core brings in (benefits, administration and wage-cost subsidies)? Tax on domestic wages is not included in the second measure, because that is money circulating domestically.

About the sources

The inputs consist of two kinds of numbers. Counts and prices (numbers of benefits, budgets, rates, average benefits, the core’s factors) are records from administration and statistics. Effects (how many more people go to work because of activation or support) come from studies; there, the researcher’s and model’s choices do matter.

The effect studies do not all point in the same direction. The Danish studies find that compulsion works for those who can work; the Dutch social assistance experiments find no effect from dropping the job-search requirement; and IPS works in every study. The model therefore sets the extremes alongside one another and calculates a medium scenario. The test is not left or right, but what remains of what the core brings in.

What is not included in the model

  • The lower WW due to the WW at the minimum level; that is included in the proceeds from the policy levers. The WW saving here is therefore an upper bound.

  • The effect of the state bearing sickness risk on the hiring of sick and older people; it has not been measured.

  • The sectors in which the additional workers will end up; the share in the export core is an assumption.

  • The production of those who turn out to be able to do more after reassessment, and effects on healthcare, benefits, poverty and displacement of other jobseekers.

  • Individuals and households are mixed together in the social assistance figures: the saving is calculated in benefits (households), the assessment in people.

  • The NTPK and NEPK are the annual values 2022; for later years, E_tv is missing. The model holds the core constant at 2022.

What employers will do differently

  • Hire more quickly. Strict employment protection discourages restructuring and job changes (Schoefer, ECB 2025). Lower dismissal costs work in the opposite direction.

  • Less agency work. The temporary employment sector had turnover of €47.2 billion in 2024 (FlexNieuws).   • Risk. Employers may shift temporary dips onto the WW. UWV measures the return to the same employer within 6 months; if this is substantial, the next zero-base recalculates the design.

Accountants and tax audits

Tax audits are already the responsibility of the state. Accountants audit the annual accounts for shareholders, banks and creditors (about 20,860 statutory audits in 2024, AFM) and handle bookkeeping and tax returns for businesses. The latter part will shrink if the state pre-fills tax returns; the former will not. The sector had turnover of €15.6 billion in 2024 (CBS/Eurostat); statutory audits for non-public-interest-entity clients amounted to €1.485 billion (AFM). What this plan counts is the €5.53 billion in tax burdens on SMEs (see above), not the disappearance of accountancy firms.

Measurement and adjustment

  • Marginal tax burden: no worker above the period’s threshold (58% in 2030, towards 50%).

  • Reinforcing loop: G < 1, target G < 0.5 in 2046.

  • The stock account: hours, capital, knowledge and new businesses per inhabitant rise, including in comparison with China, India and the US.

  • Absence by diagnosis, judgements by doctor, reassessment outcomes and inflow for WIA.

  • Budget per task: budget alongside actual expenditure; an overspend shifts that task’s zero-base.

Research gaps

  • Expenditure for WW by daily wage has not been found in published sources; the calculation for WW is based on an assumed distribution.

  • The claims ratio of sickness absence insurance, and consequently the cost component of the €2.0 billion premium.

  • The calculation method behind the ministry’s estimate of €1.4 billion, and whether the €20 billion in continued wage payments includes employers’ costs.

  • The sickness top-up for employees without a collective agreement and for collective agreements between 70 and 100%.

  • The number of sickness absence cases per year (not people).

  • The distribution of the €5.53 billion in tax burdens between time spent by businesses themselves and accountancy costs, and the additional costs at the Tax and Customs Administration.

  • Turnover from statutory audits of public-interest entities.

  • An official margin for temporary employment agencies and a consistently defined share of agency work for Denmark.

  • A labour share using the same definition for Denmark and Sweden, and a complete chain calculation for other countries.

  • The tax burden for 1960 as a percentage of GDP has been reconstructed from percentages of NNI; a CPB series as a percentage of GDP before 1970 has not been used.

  • Other accounting differences between the Netherlands and Switzerland besides health insurance premiums.

  • Dutch reassessment intervals by diagnosis: no published standard by diagnosis was found.

  • The control-theoretic model is calibrated for the EU-15, not the Netherlands; the extrapolation after 2004 has not been tested.

  • The effect on low incomes of a single rate of 40% has not been calculated; this requires the CPB microsimulation model.

  • The plan’s effect on poverty and inequality has not been calculated; an Gini and a poverty figure for 1960 using the current definition were not found.

  • How much less employers select on health when sickness risk is transferred to the state has not been measured.

  • The coverage rate of a private WW supplement with automatic enrolment is not known for the Netherlands.

  • The distribution of social assistance across the three tracks has not been measured; the financial model uses an assumption of 30 to 50% in track 1.

  • The number of single parents receiving social assistance has not been investigated.

  • The effect of a workshop day from 14, space to play without traffic and one income before 7 on later inflow into WIA has not been measured; the cost of a workshop day for all pupils has not been calculated.

  • The research did not establish whether the cause of the rise in psychological ill-health among young people lies mainly in screen use, prolonged education or other factors.

  • Whether an expenditure reduction of about €18 billion per year for four years is feasible has not been compared with historical rounds of spending cuts.

  • There is no measured Dutch effect of mandatory activation on exits from social assistance; the model draws on Danish studies and Dutch experiments (no effect).

  • The cost of an assessment by a doctor and occupational expert has not been found; the model uses consultation prices from an occupational health service.

  • The number of workers who would receive a supplement under variant B of the rule of 50 cents has not been calculated.

  • The share of activation under WW that is already paid for, and the share of additional exits that lead to work, have not been measured.
