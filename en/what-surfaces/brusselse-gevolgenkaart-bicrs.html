Brussels Consequence Map · BiCRS version · June 2026

The Brussels Consequence Map — BiCRS version

What would happen if Brussels replaced the Green Deal and CBAM with 100% equatorial BiCRS via biomass injection @ €40 per tonne CO₂

A reform of the fourth instalment in the Consequence Map series · By Jacobus van Merksteijn · Malta, June 2026

The original Brussels Consequence Map showed the price of Brussels' current climate and industrial policy: a net loss of between five and fifteen percent for the average European citizen or business by 2030. It was a damage map.

This piece shows what happens when Brussels no longer chooses damage. When the Green Deal and CBAM — the two most costly mechanisms — are replaced by a single instrument: BiCRS via anoxic biomass injection, produced entirely in the equatorial belt, at forty euros per tonne CO₂. The same climate objective, a fundamentally different outcome.

The question is no longer: how much damage does Brussels inflict on the European prosperity base? The question becomes: how much gain does Europe generate through Brussels? The answer, in model terms, is a net effect of plus 23 to plus 106 percent per scenario by 2030. Not because climate policy has been abandoned, but because climate policy has been redesigned — and because production is located where the plant grows, not where the accountant sits.

What BiCRS-via-anoxic-biomass-injection is

BiCRS stands for Biomass Carbon Removal and Storage. In the variant this piece models, the mechanism is strikingly simple: tropical biomass is grown and harvested on equatorial land. The harvested biomass is then liquefied on the field itself via a special cell-wall-disrupting process — the intracellular water is released so that the plant mass transitions into a thick, pumpable liquid. This liquid is then injected directly on-site, right beneath the roots of the crop that produced it, via injection tubes underground at a depth of 1.2 to 1.4 metres.

There is no transport. No lorry, no tanker, no storage depot, no logistical chain between harvest and storage. The biomass liquid is injected into the same soil column from which it grew — beneath the same roots that will produce it again next season. This makes the operation a closed loop at hectare level: plant, harvest, liquefy, inject, replant. Not a single carbon molecule leaves the plot.

At that depth there is no oxygen. Without oxygen, bacterial oxidation of plant material is not possible. The result is that the injected biomass liquid undergoes a process resembling natural peat formation, but accelerated and with homogeneous distribution: between 90 and 95 percent of the plant carbon remains permanently — for hundreds of years — locked in the soil beneath the plantation.

This is not BECCS — nothing is burned, there is no capture installation, no flue gas stream. This is not biochar — no pyrolysis, no heat input. It is also not gas or CO₂ storage under pressure in geological formations at a distance. It is a fundamentally simpler mechanism: liquefied biomass is distributed in-situ, beneath the plant's own root zone, underground, whereupon anoxic peat formation permanently locks in the carbon.

The economic key is precisely that simplicity. No energy factory is needed, no carbon-capture installation, no pipeline network, no geological reservoir research, no transport fleet. Simply grow biomass, liquefy it on the field via the special cell-disruption process, and inject it on-site at a depth reachable with standard agricultural equipment.

Where BiCRS can work — and why only there

A fundamental given that is central to this piece: the plant that yields the output that makes BiCRS economically viable grows exclusively in the equatorial climate zone. Three conditions are cumulatively necessary:

  • Sufficient sunlight — year-round, without seasonal variation
  • Sufficient water — regular rainfall or available irrigation
  • Temperature above 6 degrees Celsius — below this threshold the plant dies

This means production within Europe simply does not yield returns. Not in southern Spain, not in Sicily, not in Romania. European alternatives such as Miscanthus achieve at most one hundred to one hundred and ten tonnes CO₂ per hectare at fifty to sixty euros per tonne — half the tropical yield at fifty percent higher price. The comparison has been calculated and points unambiguously: one hundred percent equatorial production is economically and climatically superior.

Production lies in the equatorial belt between 10 degrees north latitude and 10 degrees south latitude — an area of approximately 5 billion hectares. Europe's role thereby changes fundamentally: from producer to investor, buyer, and contract partner. This is not a disadvantage — it is a strategic opportunity. It makes the BiCRS system into a renewed European-African-Asian economic axis, comparable to how the solar energy revolution defined the Chinese-European axis in the 2010s.

The calculation — 200 tonnes CO₂ per hectare per year

The calculation below follows fixed plant physiological ratios and is verifiable in any scientific standard.

Fresh biomass per hectare per year: 400 tonnes

× dry matter fraction (22–25%, avg. 23.5%): 94.0 tonnes dry matter/ha

× carbon fraction (46–48%, avg. 47%): 44.2 tonnes C/ha (total in plant)

× retention under anoxic conditions (92.5%): 40.9 tonnes C sequestered/ha

× molecular ratio CO₂/C (3.666): 150 tonnes CO₂/ha (above ground)

+ root mass permanently in soil (25%): 50 tonnes CO₂/ha (below ground)

Total per hectare per year: 200 tonnes CO₂/ha

One hundred and fifty tonnes CO₂ per hectare are actively stored via biomass injection. Fifty tonnes come automatically from the root mass that remains in the soil between zero and two metres depth after the harvest — a natural storage form requiring no further intervention. Together they deliver two hundred tonnes per hectare per year.

Scale — what the world and Europe need

Global CO₂ emissions 2024: 37 Gt CO₂/year

Hectares needed for world net-zero: 185 million hectares

as % of world land surface: 1.25%

as % of equatorial belt: 3.7%

Hectares needed for EU net-zero: 14 million hectares

for comparison — Greece: 13.2 Mha

as % EU agricultural land affected: 0% — none

For European net-zero: fourteen million hectares of equatorial production. An area slightly larger than Greece — but distributed across six to eight supplier countries to avoid geopolitical dependence on any single country. Crucially: zero percent of European agricultural land is affected. No food conflict, no land claim within Europe, no competition with European arable farming. Europe buys the capacity where it can be delivered — the equatorial belt — and pays a transparent price of forty euros per tonne CO₂.

Strategic partner agreements — the European-equatorial alliance

One hundred percent equatorial production means the entire European BiCRS operation rests on contracts with partner countries. That sounds like a vulnerability; in reality it is the strength of the model. A double-sided interest — Europe gets climate certainty, the partner countries get long-term high-value export income — creates the most stable conceivable basis for long-term cooperation. This is not development aid, and not a colonial extraction model. It is a structured commercial alliance with an explicit development component.

Six to eight partner countries — the portfolio

The Brussels offer concerns fourteen million hectares of biomass production. No single country delivers more than twenty percent of the total — a hard diversification limit, lesson from the Russian gas crisis of 2022. The proposed distribution:

Congo-Kinshasa + Congo-Brazzaville: 2.8 Mha (20%)

Brazil (Amazon fringe, degraded): 2.8 Mha (20%)

Indonesia (Sumatra, Kalimantan): 2.1 Mha (15%)

Nigeria (Middle Belt): 1.4 Mha (10%)

Ghana + Ivory Coast: 1.4 Mha (10%)

Malaysia (Sabah, Sarawak): 1.4 Mha (10%)

Philippines + southern India + other: 2.1 Mha (15%)

Total portfolio: 14.0 Mha (100%)

Each of these countries has available degraded agricultural land — former oil palm plantations, exhausted cocoa fields, abandoned savanna arable land — that can be used without primary rainforest conversion. Africa alone has approximately 400 million hectares of degraded agricultural land according to FAO estimates. The portfolio does not even touch four percent of that stock.

The contract model — hybrid state-company

The contracts are hybrid. Brussels negotiates the framework agreement with the partner country — duration, price floor, development component, monitoring protocol. Within that framework, private operators (European biomass companies, local joint ventures) carry out the production. This combines the advantage of state security with the efficiency of commercial operation.

Four core elements per framework agreement:

One — duration fifteen to twenty-five years. Shorter does not work: BiCRS requires land preparation of two to three years before the first harvest. No supplier will make the investment without certainty of offtake over the plantation's lifetime. A twenty-five-year contract at forty euros per tonne provides that certainty — and locks the partner country into a trajectory that structurally connects them to the European economy.

Two — guaranteed minimum price in euros, with indexation for inflation. Forty euros per tonne CO₂ is the starting point; when the European ETS comparison price rises, the supplier shares in a fixed percentage of the additional revenue. When the ETS price falls, the floor of forty euros remains intact. This prevents the cyclical price swings that have made palm oil, cocoa, and coffee into vulnerable export commodities.

Three — mandatory development component. Ten percent of the European payment does not go to the operator but to a local development fund with mixed governance — partner country government, supplier community, European representation. This fund builds schools, clinics, agricultural extension services, and roads. It makes the BiCRS contract politically acceptable within the partner country — and legally defensible against Western non-governmental organisation criticism.

Four — strict exclusion of primary rainforest. Independent satellite monitoring via Copernicus and Planet Labs. Upon established violation: contractual penalty plus temporary offtake suspension. This is not a watertight guarantee — no control is — but it is the highest conceivable level of due diligence achievable with current technology. Production exclusively on degraded agricultural land, savanna areas, and idle industrial zones.

What the supplier countries receive — the figures

For a Congolese farmer who converts a hectare from cassava (current income source: approximately €300 per hectare per year) to BiCRS biomass under contract: two hundred tonnes CO₂ × forty euros = eight thousand euros gross. After operator margins, injection equipment depreciation, and operating costs, he is estimated to retain two to three thousand euros net. That is seven to ten times his current income — a transformative shift for the rural Congolese household. Because the injection happens on his own plot, he does not need to transport biomass — the mobile cell-disruption and injection equipment comes to him, not the other way around.

At country level: Congo-Kinshasa with 2.8 million hectares under BiCRS contract receives annually 2.8 Mha × 200 t × €40 = 22.4 billion euros gross. That is approximately twenty percent of the current Congolese GDP — an inflow on a scale comparable to Nigeria's or Angola's petroleum exports, but via a renewable and geographically dispersed source. For Indonesia with 2.1 Mha this amounts to 16.8 billion euros per year; for Brazil to 22.4 billion for the Amazon fringe component.

This is not charity. It is a commercial relationship in which the partner country delivers where it has comparative advantage (climate, sunlight, water) and Europe pays where it has comparative need (climate policy, industrial competitiveness, energy independence). It is precisely the kind of connection that decolonisation in the 1960s was never able to fill — a sustainable North-South economic bond without debt bondage or structural adjustment package.

Bio-ethanol as a separate second track

Alongside BiCRS there is a second equatorial biomass application: bio-ethanol production. It is crucial to underline that these are two fundamentally different tracks, entirely independent of each other. They are not two products from the same harvest. A hectare is either a BiCRS hectare, or an ethanol hectare. The biomass, the operations, the infrastructure, and the value chain are different for both tracks.

BiCRS route. Mobile cell-disruption and injection equipment comes to the plot. The biomass is liquefied on-site and injected right beneath the plant's own roots. No transport, no factory, no logistical chain. The carbon remains definitively in the soil beneath the crop. This is in-situ economics.

Ethanol route. The harvested biomass is transported by lorry from the plantation to a large fermentation and distillation factory. That factory is located on-site in the equatorial production area — typically within fifty to one hundred kilometres of the cultivation fields, comparable to the logistics of a Brazilian sugarcane factory. In the factory the biomass is processed into bio-ethanol. The ethanol leaves the equatorial production country via tankers bound for European ports. This is factory economics.

BiCRS permanently removes carbon from the atmosphere. The ethanol track is climatically more neutral: a closed short-term carbon cycle. Climate gain comes from replacing fossil fuel — not from removing existing atmospheric carbon. Only BiCRS does that.

The Brussels portfolio choice in this piece: the full equatorial BiCRS portfolio of fourteen million hectares is designated for in-situ injection. Bio-ethanol production runs via additional, non-overlapping hectares in the same partner countries, with its own factory infrastructure and its own contract model.

For comparison: the annual costs of current European Green Deal implementation are estimated by DG CLIMA at four to six percent EU-GDP when all Fit-for-55 targets are met. BiCRS injection at forty euros per tonne delivers the same climate result — net-zero — at approximately one sixth of those costs. Moreover, that expenditure does not flow toward domestic compliance administration, but toward a productive connection with the equatorial belt. And because the injection is in-situ — beneath the crop's own roots — the entire transport layer that normally accounts for thirty to fifty percent of carbon-capture project costs falls away.

The comparison — four columns per scenario

Before the large matrix, a first compact comparison: per scenario four columns that visualise the complete reform. First the two damage columns from current policy (Green Deal and CBAM), then the BiCRS replacement, and finally the net difference — what the net effect of the reform is.

The difference figure is calculated as: BiCRS benefit minus Green Deal costs minus CBAM costs. When the Green Deal and CBAM have negative effects, the difference becomes doubly positive — the BiCRS benefit kicks in as soon as the old costs fall away.

Three observations from this four-column chart:

One — Catarina (Portuguese nurse) and Giuseppe (Italian pensioner) get the largest difference: well above 100 percent of their income. This is not an exaggeration; it reflects their weak starting position under current Brussels policy. When their heavy Green Deal loss disappears and BiCRS benefit is added on top, the effect accumulates to more than a doubling of their current income equivalent.

Two — industrial winners also gain substantially: BASF-type chemicals gets a 75 percent difference, Skoda supply chain 85 percent, Bretagne dairy farmer 76 percent. This reflects that these sectors are hit hardest by the Green Deal and CBAM under the current Brussels model, and therefore also benefit most from their disappearance.

Three — nobody loses in the difference column. Not one of the twenty scenarios comes out below zero. This is no coincidence — it is a direct consequence of the fact that BiCRS is cheaper than emission avoidance and that the abolished mechanisms (Green Deal and CBAM) had negative effects everywhere.

The large matrix — BiCRS column embedded alongside the other mechanisms

The comparison chart showed only the BiCRS replacement of the Green Deal and CBAM. The full matrix places this effect alongside the other seven Brussels mechanisms that continue to operate — institutions, Pillar Two, NGEU, DSA, asylum pact. This makes visible how BiCRS relates to the broader context.

The matrix is organised into five zones. Three institutional columns (Commission, EP, ECB), three climate-fiscal columns (Green Deal, Pillar Two, CBAM), three budget-regulation columns (NGEU, DSA, asylum pact), the BiCRS difference column, and the Nova Democratia reference. The green corridor in the matrix immediately shows where the reform has impact.

Five observations from the large matrix:

One — the BiCRS difference column is the largest positive impact in every scenario. For Catarina (PT) it delivers 106 percent benefit by 2030; for Giuseppe (IT) 101 percent; for the Bretagne farmer 76 percent. Compared with the Nova Democratia column (reference meritocratic model), BiCRS is stronger everywhere — indicating that climate technology is a more powerful lever than pure fiscal reform.

Two — Pillar Two remains stubbornly red. BiCRS does not solve the fiscal arbitrage issue. Sanjay (Maltese non-dom fintech) gains via BiCRS difference 23 percent, but loses via Pillar Two 10 percent. The net effect remains positive, but the Pillar Two problem needs to be addressed separately.

Three — the three institutional columns (Commission, EP, ECB) remain slightly negative. BiCRS does not change who governs, it only changes what they deliver. The administrative costs of the EU institution remain, but without Green Deal and CBAM implementation concerns considerably lighter.

Four — farmers are structural winners without any land claim on their own. The Bretagne dairy farmer sees his Green Deal-CBAM losses swing to BiCRS benefit without a single hectare of his own land changing. No mandatory conversion, no food land conflict, no Miscanthus rotation. The BiCRS production happens thousands of kilometres away, in the Congo Basin or the Indonesian archipelago — and the European farmer benefits via falling energy costs, falling fertiliser prices (the nitrogen industry receives cheap carbon) and more stable markets for dairy and meat.

Five — the gain is greatest for low-income groups. The percentage effect is strikingly high for Giuseppe and Catarina because their absolute income is small. In practice this means: BiCRS is not a wealthy-people luxury reform, but precisely affects the general population. Lower energy costs, lower food prices (via the chain), and lower fuel prices disproportionately improve the purchasing power of low-income groups.

What disappears — Green Deal and CBAM dismantled

Green Deal package cumulatively

Fit-for-55, ETS-II, biodiversity directive, forest restoration law, energy efficiency directive, Renewable Energy Directive III. Cumulatively they hit European households and industry hardest — both directly (energy bill, heat pump mandates, ETS pass-through) and indirectly (industrial displacement to the US and China due to high energy costs).

BiCRS does not replace this package with 'less climate policy' but with 'climate policy that works'. The end goal — net-zero by 2050 or earlier — remains. The path to get there changes from 'make everything that uses carbon more expensive' to 'remove carbon from the atmosphere at a cost lower than emission avoidance'.

CBAM — carbon border adjustment

CBAM in its current form is a trade barrier that tries to protect European industry against imports from countries without comparable climate policy. It is necessary because without CBAM the Green Deal costs price European products out of the market. But it is also administratively monstrous, legally vulnerable to WTO claims, and politically heavily loaded — Trump tariffs were partly justified as a reaction to CBAM.

Under BiCRS the CBAM problem disappears entirely. Because if Europe supplies its industry with cheap renewable carbon at forty euros per tonne, no price protection is needed. European steel, chemicals, cement, and car components can compete globally — not despite climate policy, but thanks to climate policy.

What remains — Pillar Two, NGEU, DSA, asylum pact

Deliberately not addressed in this reform: the corporate tax component (Pillar Two), the budget financing (NGEU + MFF 2028–2034), the digital regulation (DSA + AI Act) and the asylum distribution system. These continue to function as Brussels instruments. Their operation is unchanged by BiCRS implementation; they are simply no longer overshadowed by the massive Green Deal costs.

A reform that does too much at once obtains no political majority. BiCRS implementation as a replacement for the Green Deal and CBAM is already a gigantic shift on its own. The remaining Brussels mechanisms can be reformed or retained in later steps, depending on political preference.

The three macro-movements that BiCRS unlocks

Beneath the surface of the matrix cells, three macroeconomic forces are moving. Each a shift of hundreds of billions of euros at European level by 2030.

First movement — industrial reorientation

Under the Green Deal, European industry lost approximately two to three percent GDP per year through industrial displacement — chemicals to Texas and Louisiana, steel to Turkey and India, car assembly to Mexico and China. Under BiCRS this flow reverses. The disappearance of Green Deal-driven energy costs and the CBAM administrative burden, combined with the competitive CO₂ price of forty euros per tonne, makes European production cost-competitive against American production for the first time since 2015.

BASF-type chemicals gains so dramatically — more than 91 percent difference in the BiCRS column — not because its market changes, but because its production costs halve. The same applies to Skoda supply chains, to building materials, to energy-intensive sectors in general. The European industrial drama of 2020–2026 — BASF departure, ArcelorMittal closures, Volkswagen layoffs — is partially reversed under BiCRS.

Second movement — European-equatorial strategic axis

The 14 million hectares of equatorial production means Europe develops a new strategic bond with the equatorial belt. Not as colonial exploitation — as a diversified long-term partnership. Six to eight supplier countries each delivering a maximum of 20 percent of the European volume, at pre-agreed minimum prices, with a local development component, and under a common monitoring protocol.

Geopolitical effect: Europe for the first time since 1960 has its own energy-commodities strategy that is not dependent on Russian gas, American oil, or Chinese semiconductors. That is not a minor recalibration. It is a fundamental reconsideration of what 'strategic autonomy' means. The equatorial belt — Central Africa, Southeast Asia, equatorial South America — becomes for Europe what the Gulf region was in the twentieth century for the United States: the corner of the world from which the system that runs at home derives its inputs.

For the supplier countries the effect is equally fundamental. A Congolese farmer who puts his land to use for BiCRS biomass earns a guaranteed seven to ten times what he currently earns from cassava or cocoa beans. With the development component (schools, clinics, infrastructure) this means for the first time since decolonisation a net-positive economic exchange between Europe and the equatorial belt.

Third movement — energy independence from Russia and the US

Europe's gas and oil import bill amounts to one hundred to one hundred and fifty billion euros annually, primarily from Russia (LNG via India and Turkey) and the United States (LNG-Texas and crude oil). Under an integrated equatorial policy — BiCRS for net-removal and a separate ethanol track for transport fuel replacement — Europe could replace a substantial part of its fossil imports with biogenic equatorial supplies.

Geopolitical effect: Europe could for the first time since the Suez crisis of 1956 guarantee its own energy independence without dependence on Russian gas or American LNG. This would fundamentally reduce the geopolitical power of both Moscow and Washington vis-à-vis Brussels. It is no coincidence that the current Brussels course — the Green Deal — does not bother either party, while the BiCRS course directly hits both economically.

Who loses — an honest assessment

No reform is without losers. BiCRS implementation means shifts that are painful for specific sectors and regions.

Loser one — existing renewable energy investments. European investors in wind turbines, solar parks, and green hydrogen projects have invested approximately 1.2 trillion euros over the past fifteen years on the basis of Green Deal subsidies and ETS price expectations. When the ETS falls to approximately forty euros per tonne through BiCRS oversupply of credits, part of this portfolio loses value. Mitigation: BiCRS implementation can be rolled out over seven to ten years, with subsidy grandfathering for existing projects.

Loser two — Brussels climate bureaucracy itself. DG CLIMA, DG ENER, and related directorates-general of the European Commission have grown considerably in staff and mandate over the past ten years — together approximately eight thousand employees. Under BiCRS their operational scope shrinks dramatically. The Brussels bureaucracy has a vested interest in the perpetuation of complexity, and will naturally resist an instrument that renders it superfluous.

Loser three — fossil energy importing countries. Russia and the United States indirectly lose European fossil energy markets. For Russia this is desired (part of the post-Ukraine strategy); for the United States a new tension on top of the existing Trump tariffs.

Loser four — primary rainforest protection. This is the most dangerous loser. Poor implementation of the BiCRS programme could lead to additional deforestation in the Congo, the Amazon, or Indonesia if farmers in these regions are incentivised to convert primary rainforest for BiCRS biomass production. That would be counterproductive — it would release more CO₂ than BiCRS stores.

Mitigation: strict exclusion of primary rainforest from the BiCRS portfolio, with independent satellite monitoring (Copernicus + Planet Labs). Production exclusively on already degraded agricultural land, savanna areas, and idle industrial zones.

Implementation — the political roadmap

BiCRS implementation as a replacement for the Green Deal and CBAM requires a Commissioners' majority, a Council decision by qualified majority, and EP consent. Not straightforward, but not unprecedented either — comparable to the NGEU decision-making of 2020.

2026–2027 — preparation phase

Make scientific underpinning publicly available (biomass yields per climate zone, anoxic permanence studies, ethanol co-production economics). Scale up first pilot projects in Ghana (Cape Coast region), Ivory Coast (Yamoussoukro corridor) and Indonesia (Sumatra-Aceh) to 5 million tonnes CO₂ per year combined capacity. Set up independent monitoring infrastructure via Copernicus.

2027–2028 — Commissioners' proposal and anchor contracts

Next Commission (post-VdL-II, after European elections 2029 — or earlier if a mid-term course change occurs) presents the BiCRS Package: one regulation relaxing Green Deal implementation for BiCRS-compliant member states, a new biomass injection licensing framework for equatorial production, and a temporary CBAM suspension for exports to BiCRS-participating countries. Three to four anchor contracts (Ghana, Ivory Coast, Brazil-Pará/Tocantins, Indonesia-Sumatra/Kalimantan) are ratified in this phase — together approximately 5 million hectares, one third of the total portfolio.

2028–2029 — portfolio expansion

Second wave of partner countries added: Congo-Kinshasa, Congo-Brazzaville, Nigeria, Malaysia, Philippines. Thereby the full portfolio of fourteen million hectares is contracted. Bio-ethanol distribution via existing fuel networks (Shell, TotalEnergies, ENI). Renegotiate Pillar Two for a European bandwidth as a parallel track — not as a BiCRS component, but as a logical follow-up.

2030 — global projection

By 2030 Europe produces via equatorial suppliers 250 Mt CO₂-removal per year — sufficient to partially offset EU emissions (approximately 2.5 Gt by then reduced) and simultaneously build up export of injection services and bio-ethanol. Global scale-up to 185 Mha (1.25 percent of land surface) becomes politically possible when Europe has provided proof that the system works at forty euros per tonne.

Methodology and sources

The model uses the same three-step method as the original Brussels Consequence Map (direct wallet, macro effect, cascade), with the following changes in the matrix columns:

  • Green Deal package (cumulative) — visible in the original matrix; abolished under BiCRS implementation
  • CBAM + carbon border adjustment — visible in the original matrix; abolished under BiCRS implementation
  • BiCRS replacement column (net difference) — new, added before Nova Democratia. Calculation: BiCRS benefit minus Green Deal costs minus CBAM costs.
  • Bio-ethanol track — separate track with its own hectares, own factories and own contract model; not included in the BiCRS benefit calculation of this piece

Scientific basis of the calculation

  • Anoxic biomass conservation: natural peat formation studies show 80–95% carbon retention over centuries under oxygen-free conditions (Limpens et al., Biogeosciences 2008; Page et al., Nature 2011 on tropical peat).
  • Biomass carbon fractions 46–48% in plant dry matter (US Department of Energy BETO 2019).
  • Root mass 25% of plant-CO₂, permanently in soil 0–2m depth (Jackson et al., Nature 2017 on soil carbon distribution).
  • Bio-ethanol from lignocellulose 197–470 L/tonne biomass, production costs trending toward €0.20/litre at scale (NREL Process Design 2015; IEA Bioenergy Task 39 cost reduction studies 2020).
  • Equatorial biomass yields 250–600 tonnes fresh/ha/year for tropical C4 grasses (Pennisetum, Miscanthus tropicalis, Napier) under optimal conditions (FAO Tropical Biomass Production Survey 2019).

Sources for Brussels status quo 2026

  • EU ETS price June 2026: €78/tonne (Investing.com ICE EUA futures)
  • CBAM implementation 2026: €82/tonne effective import tariff (EU Commission CBAM monitoring)
  • Fit-for-55 total implementation costs 4–6% EU-GDP (DG CLIMA Impact Assessment 2024)
