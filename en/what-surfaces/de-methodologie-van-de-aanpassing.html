What surfaces · Malta, June 2026 · Methodology

The food line

The courage to say it is wrong is absent through lack of self-knowledge — a structural fault, not a character flaw. All of Europe has bricked up its detours; every law is designed to ensure that no real decision can be taken anywhere. The laws made by those who came before us do not know the future; nature does, and must lead again.

By Jacobus van Merksteijn · Malta, June 2026

The food in the supermarket is our nourishment. When we decide that the supermarket earns too much and we dismantle it through price regulation, taxation and compliance — we are not destroying "shareholder profits". We are destroying our food line. Empty shelves, imported scraps from countries where the same rules do not apply, higher prices for lower quality. That is not justice; that is self-amputation.

This principle holds everywhere. The farmer, the entrepreneur, the small business, the exporter, the industrial investor, the training provider: they are all food lines. Whoever says they earn too much, and that their room must therefore be curtailed, is cutting into the circulatory system of the whole. Not out of malice — out of thoughtlessness about how a living organism works.

The supermarket case

What feeds us, and how we destroy it

The Dutch supermarket — Albert Heijn, Jumbo, Lidl, Aldi, Plus — feeds 17.5 million people. Three times a day. Every day. Seven days a week. Nobody complains of hunger; the shelves are full; prices are relatively low; quality is overwhelmingly good.

Margins in the Dutch supermarket chain run from 2.1 to 3.4 per cent — below those of Spanish, German and Italian retail. The large profits do not sit with the supermarket itself; they sit with the supplying oligopolies (dairy, bread, meat processing, soft-drink brands). But in the public imagination — fed by politicians in search of an enemy — the supermarket is cast as the profiteer.

What happens if we cap supermarket margins through legislation, introduce price controls, or impose additional demands through competition authorities? The chain loses investment capacity. Distribution centres go unrenewed. Fresh-food stores close. Logistics are taken over by foreign competitors who do not operate under the same rules. Shelves become less full, less fresh, less varied. The consumer loses more than he gains through price regulation.

Worse still: when the supermarket chain weakens, so do the Dutch farmers who supply it. The sales channel becomes unreliable. Farmers quit. Imports fill the gap. The food line now runs through Romania, Brazil and Vietnam — and it is more expensive, more vulnerable and shorter than before.

This is not a plea for "unlimited profits". It is a warning that the food line which literally feeds us is not an abstraction. It is measurable in calories, kilograms, filled baskets, available choice. Whoever strangles it does not obtain justice — only less food.

• • •

The food line as a law of nature

The supermarket is one food line. But the principle is universal. Everything that lives has a food line. The body has respiration, nutrition and circulation. A community has agriculture, crafts and trade. A business has revenue and labour productivity. A state has a productive sector that generates taxes. A civilisation has an energy system that makes its work possible. Cut into any of those food lines, and the system above it contracts or collapses.

This is not ideology. This is a physiological and thermodynamic fact. Every organisation — from a single cell to a transnational state — requires a net inflow of energy and nutrients greater than its losses. When outflow exceeds inflow, decay sets in. Not tomorrow, not in ten years — but inescapably.

The principle of the food line

Nothing lives without a food line. Not the cell, not the farm, not the business, not the supermarket, not the municipality, not the nation, not the European Union. Whoever demands preservation without protecting the food line that pays for it is engaged in self-dissolution — regardless of intent.

Replacement is what a healthy food line does constantly. Cells are replaced. Crops shift. Businesses are renewed. Technology is succeeded. Not as loss, but as condition for survival. A system that blocks replacement blocks its own food line.

• • •

Three forces, one error of thought

Three political forces in Europe share the same structural error: they want preservation plus more, without acknowledging what that does to the food line.

The Brussels climate authority

It wants to preserve: the climate zones of 1990, the species of 1990, the landscape of 1990. Plus more: a 55 per cent emissions reduction by 2030, biodiversity at 1970 levels, everything net-zero. At the same time: more regulation, more subsidies, more bureaucrats, more compliance.

Food line: the European productive sector. That is being strangled by ETS prices, CBAM tariffs, energy prices, regulation and compliance costs. Result: the German automotive industry is moving to China (500,000 full-time jobs already gone since 2023), energy-intensive industry is relocating to the US, farmers are leaving the sector en masse, prosperity is shrinking. The Brussels climate authority can only reach its goal by strangling the food line that funds its own budget.

The Dutch left-wing parties

GroenLinks-PvdA, D66 in its left-wing variant, BIJ1, Volt: they want to preserve — the welfare state of 1980, the minimum wage, employment protection, the pension. Plus more: more healthcare, more education, more housing, more climate action, more accessibility, more redistribution.

Food line: the Dutch small and medium enterprises, the self-employed, the owner-managed business, the exporter, the innovative industry. That is being strangled by corporation tax, higher levies on investment income, income tax rates, freelancer regulations, permit processes, nitrogen-driven compulsion and taxes on growth. Result: entrepreneurs are leaving for Portugal or Dubai, families with growing wealth are emigrating, SMEs are contracting. The left-wing coalition can only reach its goal by strangling the food line from which it draws its redistribution.

The trade unions

FNV, CNV, sector unions in healthcare, education, construction, industry: they want to preserve — permanent jobs, fixed wages, fixed pensions, fixed working hours, fixed rights. Plus more: higher wages, a shorter working week (32 hours), more holidays, more sick pay, more job retention, more dismissal protection.

Food line: the same employers that pay their members' wages. Those are being strangled by collective bargaining increases that can no longer be absorbed by productivity gains. Result: the employer either automates, goes bankrupt, or relocates — and the very jobs the unions wanted to protect disappear. The union can only reach its goal by strangling the food line from which its members' jobs flow.

The shared error

All three want preservation plus additional demands, in complete disregard of the food line that must pay for it. Not one of them asks: where does the money come from that pays for this, and what happens to that source when we put it in a stranglehold?

When the food line fails, first the additional demands become undeliverable, then preservation itself becomes impossible, and eventually the entire system topples. That is not a prediction — that is how organisms, businesses, municipalities and states have been dying for thousands of years.

• • •

No self-knowledge, no revolution

Here we reach the deepest layer. It is not that administrators are too cowardly to say things are wrong. It is not that they lack backbone. It is that they lack the self-knowledge to know that things are wrong — and that is a structural fault, not a character flaw.

Self-knowledge is the capacity to judge for oneself whether something is right or wrong, independent of what the majority, the law, the curriculum or the committee thinks. Self-knowledge does not come from books. It comes from personally going down the wrong path, personally feeling that a road leads nowhere, personally bearing the pain of a wrong choice, and personally learning from it. That is what gives a person self-knowledge: lived mistakes.

A system that rules out mistakes also rules out self-knowledge. An education that makes every path safe does not make people wiser — it makes them incapable of becoming wise. We have trained three generations in an environment where every decision had already been made for them, every answer already filled in, every fork in the road already signposted. The result: an entire administrative class with not a single genuinely lived mistake to its name. Not because it would have lacked the courage to make one — but because the system never gave it the chance.

The structural fault

Revolution — in the sense of daring to say it is wrong and that there is another way — requires self-knowledge. Self-knowledge requires lived mistakes. Lived mistakes require the right to go wrong. That right has been taken away by three generations of safe education, safe governance, safe procedures, safe career paths.

What remains is an administrative class that takes everything it must do on authority, because it has no inner anchor from which it can say "this is wrong". Not out of cowardice — out of lack of instrument. The instrument is called self-knowledge. It was never granted to it.

This is why criticism from within the system stays at the surface. Whoever has grown up inside the system can only be critical within the system's own vocabulary. He can say "this procedure could be more efficient", not "this procedure as a whole is wrong". He can say "more subsidy" or "less subsidy", not "no subsidy". He can say "net-zero by 2030" or "net-zero by 2035", not "net-zero is the wrong unit of measurement". The radical question — whether the concept itself is sound — he cannot even formulate, because his language was shaped inside that concept.

The revolution that is needed is therefore not political. It is epistemic: restoring self-knowledge as a legitimate source of judgement. Only those who know what they know — because they have lived it, not because a textbook or a committee told them — can say that the emperor has no clothes. Everyone who has drawn all their knowledge from the imperial tailoring factory sees clothes where there are none.

• • •

The system reflexively expels its rebels

Here a second mechanism comes into play that sits one layer deeper. Our entire society — our institutions, our procedures, our decision-making, our education, our whole model of civilisation — is built on evolutionary action and thought. Small steps, consensus-building, gradual change, caution. Whoever moves within it is recognised and accepted. Whoever moves outside it is by definition foreign to the system.

The revolutionary is not someone who goes too far. He is someone who steps outside the logic on which the system itself is founded. He asks questions that cannot be asked within the system. He sees patterns that cannot be seen within the system. He proposes directions that cannot be addressed within the system. That does not automatically make him right — but it does make him indigestible to the system that produced him.

And so something disturbingly human occurs: we expel him without genuinely thinking about him. Not out of anger, not after deliberation, not with a refuting argument. Reflexively. He is marginalised in the press, not cited in research, refused entry to committees, not hired for positions. His ideas are either declared incomprehensible — "I don't understand it, so it must be wrong" — or neatly framed as "conspiracy theory", "extremist", "no scientific consensus", "not realistic", "not feasible". All words that say nothing about the content of what he argues. All words that say only: he does not belong with us.

Three examples from lived experience

BiCRS / ethanol from equatorial biomass: an evolutionary solution to the CO₂ problem — a carbon cycle instead of enforceable net-zero — that already works elsewhere in the world (Brazil, Indonesia) and is systematically rejected in Europe. Not because the numbers are wrong. But because it falls outside the ETS+CBAM paradigm on which the entire climate bureaucracy is built. Whoever proposes BiCRS is not a candidate for an EU post.

VMP — a voluntary mandate party: a political form that is not bound by voters and can therefore take decisions that voters do not like. On paper exactly what is needed for the transition. In practice: unprecedented, unclassified, not in line with "how we do things in the Netherlands". Not refuted — unread.

The Consequence Map: a diagnostic instrument that shows, per voting position, what the welfare drain is. Something that fundamentally surpasses what voter guides can do. But it is not taken up by the existing political science, not discussed in the press, not investigated by committees. Not because of errors in the matrix — because of an inability to think outside the framework.

The pattern is the same everywhere: it is not the content of what the rebel says that is examined, but his position relative to the system. "Who is he, exactly?" — instead of "what is he saying?" "Where does he stand?" — instead of "is it right?" That is not malice; that is reflex. The system has its antibodies, and they work automatically.

The evolutionary trap

Evolutionary action works as long as the environment changes evolutionarily — slowly, predictably, with enough time for consensus-building. We are facing an environment that is changing revolutionarily: AI in months, China-speed in weeks, climate in years-not-decades. At such a moment a purely evolutionary system is not safe but lethal — it has no mechanism to change course rapidly.

At precisely this turning point, we have deprived ourselves of the capacity to recognise revolutionaries. Not because they do not exist — they do, and in growing numbers. But we have developed a reflex against them. The result: the people who could still save us now are being expelled before we have even read them.

This is not a plea to give every outsider the benefit of the doubt. Many rebels are wrong — that is precisely why we built our evolutionary system. But the problem is not that we are sceptical of rebels; the problem is that we no longer investigate. We have replaced scepticism with reflex. A healthy society would test the revolutionary on his arguments. A calcified society expels him on his form.

• • •

Europe has bricked up its detours

Anyone who thinks this is a Dutch problem, a French problem, a German problem, is mistaken. The problem has established itself across the full breadth of the European continent, in such a way that there are no detours left. Not via another country, not via a different coalition, not via a different law. All routes have been bricked up by laws that serve the same purpose: to ensure that no genuine decision can be taken anywhere.

Look at how the pattern runs through every layer of European society:

  • Legislation: every decision must first pass through 27 member states, then through the European Parliament, then through the Court of Justice, then through 27 national parliaments, then through 27 national judges, then through municipalities, then through courts of appeal, then through stakeholders. Ten to fifteen years later the decision has been watered down beyond recognition.
  • Permitting: building a new factory in the Netherlands takes an average of seven years; in Germany five; in France eight. Not because the factory is complex — because the procedure has been made complex by decades of accumulated protective legislation from which nothing can escape.
  • Education: teachers are no longer allowed to fail pupils, schools may no longer be strict, universities may no longer create rankings that cause offence. Everyone is safely guided through the system. No one ever reaches a dead end. So no one ever learns that they were wrong.
  • Labour market: dismissal costs a fortune, hiring brings long-term obligations, automation is fiscally penalised. Employers no longer dare to decide — so no decision is taken, and productive employment emigrates.
  • Governance: ministers may not decide without committees, committees may not decide without consultation, consultations may not decide without peer review, peer reviews may not decide without a hearing. There is no longer any place where a decision actually falls.
  • Climate policy: ETS, CBAM, RED-III, Fit-for-55, Green Deal, Just Transition — every instrument is designed so that no single member state can exit, no parliament can deviate, and no future government can reverse it. Today's laws have already made tomorrow's laws impossible.

A closed system

The pattern is the same everywhere: every law makes it impossible to make the next law differently. We do not live in a democracy where we can change course. We live in a tightening cocoon of laws made by people who could not know the future — and who no longer allow us to discover that future for ourselves.

The laws of those who came before us cannot know the future. They were written for the world they saw. We face a different world. But we are not permitted to decide — because their laws forbid it.

Nature knows more than our laws

If all routes through human law are bricked up, one guiding principle remains that cannot be abolished by committees, lawyers or majorities: nature. Nature has no quorum, no veto, no consultation round. It has taken decisions continuously for three billion years, gone wrong, and learned. It knows far more than any legislator — because it has lived what we can only predict.

What would happen if we took nature as our guide again, instead of the laws of people who cannot know the future?

  • Material cycle instead of net-zero: nature has no "net-zero CO₂". It has a carbon cycle in which every gram that leaves the ground is stored again somewhere — in biomass, in oceans, in soil. BiCRS follows that cycle. ETS and CBAM try to freeze it.
  • Replacement instead of preservation: nature replaces constantly. Cells, leaves, species, biotopes. What is no longer adapted to its environment disappears; what fits gains ground. Our laws try the opposite: preserve everything, at any cost.
  • Diversity instead of monoculture: a healthy soil has thousands of microbial species alongside one another; a healthy economy has thousands of entrepreneurial species alongside one another. Our laws produce monocultures — one type of education, one type of business, one type of employment contract, one type of supplier.
  • Adaptive rapid action instead of endless deliberation: an organism that "deliberates" too long when a predator approaches is dead. Nature rewards speed of decision. Our laws reward delay of decision.
  • Local adaptation instead of central uniformity: in nature, no two biotopes are alike. Our laws impose one rule across all of Europe, from Lapland to Sicily, as if soil, climate and culture did not exist.

The difference between a law and a law of nature is this: the law humans make is contingent — it holds as long as the majority supports it, and can be otherwise tomorrow. The law of nature is necessary — it holds whether you recognise it or not. Whoever governs against gravity falls. Whoever governs against the food line goes hungry. Whoever governs against replacement dies out.

The guiding path back to nature

Europe has placed its laws above nature and has thereby become locked inside itself. The only way out is not better laws — it is letting nature lead again. Not as sentiment, not as religion, not as a "biodiversity target". But as the factual teacher that has three billion years of experience surviving under continuous change.

Our laws must adapt to nature, not the other way round. That is not an anti-modern statement; it is the only remaining chance to know the future — because the people who want to fix it in advance cannot, by definition, do so.

The education case

We are training people for yesterday's world

The Netherlands trains approximately 8,500 engineers annually at its technical universities. These students begin in September 2026, graduate in 2031 or 2032 (bachelor's) or 2034 (master's), and enter the labour market with knowledge that took six to eight years to form — shaped by curricula set somewhere between 2018 and 2023.

In that same period, GPT-4 (2023) was succeeded by GPT-5 (2024), Claude 3 (2024), Gemini-2 (2025), GPT-5.5 (2025), the first agentic AIs, and the first fully autonomous software engineering agents. An AI system in 2026 can already perform more typical engineering tasks than a graduating junior engineer — faster, more accurately, and without sick leave.

That does not mean engineers become superfluous. It means that the type of engineer we are now training — a generic knowledge-carrier, a student of a 2018 textbook — will no longer be viable in 2032. The student of 2026 who graduates in 2032 enters a market where AI is already doing his job. He becomes structurally unemployable, or must retrain into something AI cannot do: judgement formation, ethical negotiation, physical-spatial work, complex multi-stakeholder management.

The Dutch education sector is not keeping pace. Curricula are updated more slowly than technology itself evolves. Universities are institutionally configured for stability, not agility. Teachers' unions resist radical restructuring. The Ministry of Education works with multi-year plans that are by definition behind the curve. The consequence: 8,500 engineers per year delivered to a world for which they are not trained.

The costs of this mismatch are enormous and avoidable:

  • Student grants: €20,000–35,000 per student × 8,500 × 6 years = €1.0 to 1.8 billion per cohort that ends up in the wrong place.
  • Retraining after graduation: €15,000–25,000 per person × roughly 40 per cent of the cohort = €510 million to €850 million in additional costs.
  • Lost productive years: an average of 18 months between graduation and first viable employment in a suitable sector.
  • Social unrest: trained professionals who cannot find work in their field feed political instability.

This can be avoided by looking further ahead. What will we need in 2032? Not generic engineers. We will need: BiCRS process engineering, agro-mechatronics for equatorial plantations, ethanol distillation engineering, complex multi-stakeholder climate project management, elderly care work that AI cannot do, regulatory negotiation, ethical AI auditing. But we are training for what we used to need. The food line of the future labour market is being severed before it has even formed.

This is an acceleration gap that widens every year. China reorients in months where Europe takes years. The United Arab Emirates implements in a working week where the Netherlands needs a full parliamentary term. India builds national infrastructure in two years where we spend fifteen years deliberating.

The cause is structural. Our decision-making runs through:

  • Polder consultation: months of public input, building consensus, stakeholder rounds.
  • Coalition constraints: four parties each able to deploy their blocking minority.
  • Union resistance: every reform proposal fought out in collective bargaining negotiations.
  • Judicial review: Council of State, Supreme Court, European Court — multiple instances, each taking multiple years.
  • Consultation procedures: public disclosure, objection periods, appeal options.
  • Subsidy administration: application, assessment and accountability cycles.

Something can be said in favour of care at each individual step. But taken together they produce a country that is permanently chasing the cart. China decides on Thursday and builds on Monday. We decide after six consultation rounds, three years later, and build — once the permit is in place — only in year seven.

The cost of slowness

Every two years that the Netherlands is slow on a particular transition — while other countries do it in two months — means 22 months of lag. Multiplied across twenty moments of transition in a decade, that amounts to more than four years of permanent arrears. We are never in a position to lead; we follow what others have already invented, set up and produced at scale. The income we lose as a result — as nations, as businesses, as individuals — is structural and cumulative.

The CO₂ reality, free of the methane panic

Finally, a note on the climate itself, because here lies the core misunderstanding that justifies the entire attack on the food line.

Official methodology treats methane as the primary problem because the GWP figure (28–30) sounds high. But methane has an atmospheric half-life of around nine years. After ten to fifteen years, the vast majority has broken down through reaction with the hydroxyl radical — to 1 g CH₄ → 2.75 g CO₂ + 2.25 g H₂O. Today's methane bill is a CO₂ bill by 2040.

CO₂, by contrast, remains in the atmosphere for 100 to 1,000 years. That is the real cost question. Not methane. Not nitrogen. Not livestock. CO₂ is the long-term problem, and CO₂ is what we actually need to remove.

The difference is fundamental:

  • Methane approach = regulating something that breaks itself down within fifteen years. Farmers, the supermarket chain and SMEs pay the price for a gas that, by 2040, will no longer exist as methane.
  • CO₂ approach via BiCRS = removing from the atmosphere what would otherwise linger for 1,000 years. One carbon price (€40/tonne), equatorial plantations, liquid injection on site, market forces — not 580,000 full-time EU bureaucrats strangling the food line.

What the food line demands

Whoever takes the food line seriously arrives at four requirements:

One — the food line must breathe. Cells replace themselves; entrepreneurs renew products; workers learn new skills; crops shift; technology is succeeded. That is not damage — that is health. Preservation subsidies, job-retention packages, sector protection and regulatory accumulation all work against replacement.

Two — the food line must not be constricted. Every additional tax, every additional rule, every additional compliance requirement is a reduction in capacity. An entrepreneur who spends sixty per cent of his week on administration cannot spend the other forty on production.

Three — the food line must be fed through adaptation, not preservation. Making something different today than thirty years ago. Every attempt to fix the current state — farmers at dairy, industry at coal, energy at gas, education at curriculum-2018 — weakens the food line because it cannot evolve freely.

Four — we must be as fast as our global competitors. Not polder-consultation fast. Not coalition-binding fast. Not court-process fast. Market fast. Deciding in weeks, implementing in months, evaluating in years. Whoever is slow loses income, jobs and relevance.

A final comparison

I am 67. My body does what it has always done: it replaces cells, it adapts, it becomes different from what it was. No cream, no treatment, no law can stop that. I can choose to move with that replacement — healthier living, different priorities, different ambitions — or I can spend a fortune on the illusion of staying 35. The second never works; the first does.

Dutch and Brussels politics has been attempting the second for twenty years. They are spending trillions on the illusion that the world of 1990 can be preserved — the climate zones, the species, the agriculture, the industry, the employment, the wage structure, the pensions, the welfare state, the curriculum, the supermarket chain — while the food line that must pay for all of it is being strangled by the combination of climate compulsion, left-wing redistribution demands and union pressure.

The food line is patient, but it is not inexhaustible. When it stops breathing, first the additional demands go, then the preservation projects, and then the whole system. The Germans are learning this now, in real time, with their automotive industry. Farmers are learning it too. The next wave will be the European tech sector. Then European healthcare. Then European pensions. Then the supermarket chain.

Or we turn around. We acknowledge that everything is a replacement market. We protect the food line — supermarket, farmer, small business, exporter, entrepreneur, education — instead of strangling it. We do in two months what others do in two months, not in two years. We train for what we will need, not for what we once wanted. We calculate climate policy on one carbon price instead of a thousand rules. We let farmers shift to what is viable in the new climate. We let industry shift to what is productive. We let the market regulate what in a free economy has always been regulated by the market.

The food line is not an ideological preference. It is the physiological prerequisite for everything we ask for. Whoever ignores it — government, party, union, voter — dies out. And no idealism, no law, no majority can stop that.
