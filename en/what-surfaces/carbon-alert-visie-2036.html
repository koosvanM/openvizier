The curve shows the real production price from the pellet factory to the distillation tower. From that point, ethanol wins every comparison: per kWh, per km, per MWh of heat.

2. The LCOE curve below the grid

The black line represents the Carbon-Alert SOFC without a single support mechanism. In 2026, still twice as expensive as the grid (38 versus 16 c€/kWh). By 2030, already cheaper. In 2036, less than half. That is not an optimistic projection — it is the extrapolation of two known forces: the 18 percent learning curve on stack costs and the steady decline of the ethanol price from €0,55 to €0,30 per liter.

That is a political bonus, not a technological merit. Therefore, all communication here is based on the real figure of 9,97 c€/kWh without subsidy.

3. Architecture — the five building blocks

The hub is not a standalone fuel cell. It is an integrated system in which the Carbon-Alert BiCRS chain (Biomass with Carbon Removal and Storage) and the SOFC unit reinforce each other. Ethanol is produced on-site or regionally. The SOFC converts it into power, heat, and pure CO₂. That CO₂ can be stored or marketed. Nothing in this design leans on public support — the hub is commercially viable with €0,30 per liter of feedstock and €1.800 per kilowatt CAPEX.

3.1 Footprint and flexibility

  • Footprint: 20 m² SOFC + 8 m² balance-of-plant + 30 m² distillation = 58 m² total
  • Modularity: scalable from 25 kW (residential cluster) to 5 MW (industry or data center) with the same stack unit
  • Fuel stock: a 2.000-liter week-tank suffices for full autonomy during grid outages
  • Noise: < 45 dB at one meter — no rotating parts, only blower and pump
  • Maintenance: stack replacement every 7–10 years, OPEX 1,2–1,5 % of CAPEX per year

A Carbon-Alert hub of €180.000 pays for itself in approximately three years — without the municipality, the national government, or the EU having to contribute a single euro. For an industrial cluster with multiple hubs, the payback period drops below 2,5 years due to operational economies of scale. SDE++ could accelerate this to approximately one year — interesting, but not necessary.

5. Why 2036 — and not 2030 or 2045

2030 is too early to be electrically below the grid without an unexpectedly rapid CAPEX drop. Break-even is reached, but the margin is thin.

2045 is too late. By then, stranded assets in hydrogen infrastructure, battery factories, and large-scale grid planning will have already been formed and politically locked in.

2036 is the optimum. SOFC will be at €1.800 per kilowatt (mass production via Doosan, Weichai, Bloom). Ethanol at €0,30 per liter. The Spanish grid will by then be at 21,5 c€/kWh due to scarcity and grid investments. Carbon-Alert is at that point already five years past break-even — no longer marginal, but dominant.

5.1 Strategic sequencing

  • 2026–2028: pilot 25 kW at a single location, validation of 70 %+ efficiency, funding from private channels
  • 2028–2031: first commercial rollout 100 kW, break-even reached around 2030 — without subsidy
  • 2031–2036: scaling to 500+ hubs, BiCRS chain established regionally, data center and industrial contracts
  • 2036–2040: dominance in the Mediterranean mid-load market, export model to Latin America and Africa

The structural advantage of Carbon-Alert is precisely that the design does not depend on the whims of politicians and their subsidy schemes. When an SDE++ scheme is scrapped, the business plan relying on it collapses. Carbon-Alert's break-even does not change. Our business case is built on market fundamentals, not on public goodwill.

7. Conclusion — what is actually on the table

A 100 kW Carbon-Alert energy hub will deliver power in 2036 for 9,97 c€/kWh — less than half the Spanish grid (21,5 c€/kWh) — with €180.000 CAPEX and a payback period of approximately three years.

All figures are calculated on the real market price of ethanol (€0,30/liter) without any government support, SDE++, ETS credits, or feed-in tariffs.

The technology is proven (Nissan 70 %, Ceres/Doosan/Weichai 50 MW). The learning curve is documentable (18 % per doubling). The competitive landscape is open (Bosch and Stellantis out of H₂).

Carbon-Alert Ltd is technically ready. Economically substantiated. Politically independent. What is missing is execution capital — not vision. The next three years will determine whether we become Mediterranean and global leaders in decentralized electricity, or whether we watch as Doosan and Weichai seize our learning curve. The window is open — now.
