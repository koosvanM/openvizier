What surfaces · Malta, June 2026

We must get back to work ourselves

A manifesto on industrial backbone, productive strength and the end of non-commitment

Jacobus van Merksteijn · Malta, June 2026

The Netherlands and Europe have lived too long under the illusion that prosperity, security and strategic autonomy can exist without effort, without risk and without our own productive strength. That era is over. Anyone who still thinks a society stays upright mainly by regulating, importing, subsidising and morally soothing each other has not understood how civilisation is built.

Reality is simpler and harder. A country that neglects its industry, weakens its work ethic, suppresses its capacity for risk and normalises its dependence on others sooner or later loses its backbone. First economically, then socially, ultimately politically. Such a society does not collapse in one stroke — it sinks slowly through the ice, until one discovers that the ground has already disappeared.

All systems follow the same law

Whether it concerns energy, radiation, industry, upbringing, traffic or geopolitical dependence: everywhere the same basic rule applies. A system that is never again loaded, never again builds resistance and never again produces for itself becomes weak. A body without stimuli loses its defences. An economy without its own industry loses its resilience. A society that wants to rule out every risk in advance loses its capacity to carry progress.

This is precisely where the Netherlands and large parts of Europe stand today. Everything has been aimed for decades at avoiding friction. Work had to become lighter. Production was allowed to leave. Physical risk had to disappear. Strategic dependence was sold as “efficiency”. Cheap import was mistaken for clever policy. Safety was confused with comfort. And meanwhile the foundations of a mature economy were dismantled piece by piece.

The price of outsourced strength

Through years of outsourcing and import addiction, Europe's industrial immune system has been weakened. China grew into the factory of the world, while Europe increasingly positioned itself as buyer, regulator and end user. In the short term that yielded cheaper products and higher margins, but in the long term it came at the cost of knowledge, manufacturing capacity, craftsmanship and strategic independence.

That effect is now visible in critical chains: raw materials, batteries, solar panels, pharmaceutical components, electronics and parts of mechanical engineering. In several sectors not only production has disappeared, but also the industrial memory: the people, routines, tools and mentality needed to scale up again on home soil. The conclusion is hard but unavoidable: what has been neglected does not return without damage.

More labour, more productivity, more backbone

Rebuilding economic independence will not fall out of the sky. It demands more work, more discipline and more productive effort from the population. Anyone who thinks everything can still be solved with subsidies, spreadsheets and one more technology programme misses the real shortage: too few people who actually build, make, repair, maintain and organise.

Machines and robots have their place, but they are no miracle cure. In many sectors they are expensive, complex, energy-intensive and dependent on capital, software and chains that are themselves geopolitically vulnerable. The easy automation gains have largely been taken. What remains is the old truth that earlier generations still knew: a society becomes strong when its people are strong, skilled, willing and productive.

Concretely this means: the quantity of serious working hours will have to go up, craftsmanship must regain status, and productivity must no longer be treated only as a technical question but also as a moral and cultural one. The generation of parents and grandparents did not wait for perfect conditions; with limited means, hard work and a high tolerance for discomfort, they laid the foundation on which later generations could build. Anyone who wants to preserve prosperity will have to learn that attitude again.

No prosperity without risk

A society that wants to neutralise every physical, technical or economic risk in advance cuts off its own future. That applies in industry, energy, mobility and entrepreneurship. There is no path to prosperity without strain, danger, responsibility and the possibility of failure.

This does not mean recklessness should become the norm. It does mean there is a fundamental difference between sensible protection and paralysing over-protection. When every project is locked in rules, every physical profession in suspicion, every industrial investment in legal fog and every technological step in fear, prosperity is not protected but thrown out in advance.

That is what now happens too often. The Netherlands no longer holds consensus to combine strength, but to neutralise it. Everything is flattened, slowed, swapped and postponed until nothing remains of the original ambition. The result is not harmony, but standstill.

Energy, industry and adult responsibility

Precisely for that reason the energy debate also deserves an adult tone. Modern societies do not live without risk. Not with fossil fuels, not with nuclear energy, not with international trade, not with infrastructure and not with medical technology. The question is therefore not how all risks disappear, but how a society ranks, concentrates, manages and bears risks.

Nuclear energy fits in that adult frame. Not as a fairy tale without danger, but as technology with high energy density, limited waste volumes and a residual flow that in principle remains concentrated and manageable. That is fundamentally different from a fossil model in which damage is spread diffusely through air, climate and geopolitical dependence.

Here too the same lesson applies as in industry: do not reject everything because it carries risk, but choose systems that combine manageable risks with structural strength. A civilisation can only move forward if it accepts that adulthood does not mean danger disappears, but that one learns to bear it.

What must now happen

The course change must be clear and hard:

  • Rebuild our own industry in sectors that are strategically, energetically and socially essential.
  • Revalue labour — not only as a right but also as a duty and source of civilisational strength.
  • Give physical and technical craftsmanship status again — in education, policy and culture.
  • Name risks honestly, but no longer use them as an excuse to undertake nothing.
  • Systematically reduce dependence on external powers where it touches vital functions.
  • Replace the governmental reflex of endless weighing and braking with direction, discipline and execution.

Closing

The time of non-commitment is over. The Netherlands and Europe can choose: either rebuild a foundation themselves, restore productive strength and accept that prosperity always goes hand in hand with effort and risk; or carry on with consensus-building, shielding, outsourcing and moral soothing until the system drains from within.

Whoever chooses the first path chooses discomfort, discipline and recovery. Whoever chooses the second chooses decline — slow, neat, regulated and ultimately irreversible.

There is no other way. We must get back to work ourselves. Not later. Now.

Jacobus van Merksteijn

Malta

Entrepreneur, engineer and publisher of Het Open Vizier. Writes about industry, governance and the foundations of an adult society.
