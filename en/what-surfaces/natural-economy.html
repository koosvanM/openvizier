Natural economy

Nature as engineer, humanity as learner, and who gets to sit at the table

Jacobus van Merksteijn · Malta, 8 November 2026

Manifesto of Jacobus van Merksteijn. The technical figures and performance claims below are claims and examples from the supplied manifesto, not measurement results independently verified by this publication. I. The proposition

Classical economics cannot account for what nature has invented over 3.8 billion years. And the political system cannot hear it, because it is not at the table.

These are two factual observations. No polemic, no ideology. When a carbon-capture installation charges €80 per tonne of CO2 and a biomass carbon-storage technology does it for €25, that is not a marginal improvement the market will find in time. It is a different system of calculation. The cheaper option uses what plants already do every growing cycle — capture carbon — and adds only the human act of making it permanent. The expensive option does the same work from scratch, using steel, energy and compression.

When an aircraft wing with a micro-surface structure borrowed from bird feathers saves 16.5 per cent in fuel, that is not a refinement of aerodynamics. It is the use of a design principle that has been optimised in birds by selection for millions of years. The aviation industry spent a hundred years calculating without finding the solution, not because it was stupid, but because it was looking in the wrong place. It looked at materials and engines. The solution lay in surfaces.

This manifesto does two things. First, it establishes what nature can do, and what an economy that takes it seriously could measure. Then it establishes why this rarely happens — not because of technical inability, but because of a systemic flaw in the selection of who gets to sit at the table and how far that person is allowed to look ahead. The human role is not to invent. It is to notice, learn, and reproduce industrially what nature has already shown a thousand times. And that is possible only when someone is free of the two filters through which power operates.

Humans are not inventors. We are pupils of a teacher with a lead of 3.8 billion years.

II. What classical economics does not see

The science of economics as it has been built since Adam Smith measures value in terms of labour, capital, scarcity and exchange. That is not nonsense. It is correct for what it measures. But it structurally fails to see three kinds of value.

First blind spot — surface value

Almost all interaction in the physical world takes place on surfaces. Air and wing. Water and hull. Tyre and road surface. Coating and substrate. Foil and wall. Root and soil. An engineer who understands surfaces improves systems by factors — not by percentages. But there is no line item for surface quality in economic accounting. An aircraft is an aircraft, with or without an optimised skin. The economy sees the aircraft; it does not see what the exterior does.

Second blind spot — generative breakthroughs versus incremental improvement

Classical economics rewards incremental efficiency: one per cent better, two per cent cheaper, three per cent faster. Its framework for calculation is built on that. But nature does not work incrementally. It works through generative breakthroughs: the eye, photosynthesis, the respiratory system, the circulatory system. None of these was an improvement on something earlier — each was a new solution to a problem that otherwise remained unsolvable. Those who learn from nature do not deliver a two per cent improvement. They deliver solutions that make entire industries obsolete.

Third blind spot — nature's timescale

Economic calculations are made in years, at most decades. Discount rates reduce long-term value to almost zero. But nature calculates in hundreds of thousands of generations. A pattern that works remains. A pattern that does not work disappears. What remains is always optimal. When we copy a pattern from nature, we get the results of millions of years of testing for free. No economic model can put a price on that, because it would overturn the basic theory of the cost of capital.

III. Why nature is not at the table

What classical economics fails to see is a measurable problem. What the political system cannot hear is a structural problem. And this second problem is more serious, because it determines which solutions are ever seriously considered.

Nature is never at the table when a minister or a council has a problem to solve. At the table are the companies that think they have a solution — and want a share of the subsidy pie.

This is not a complaint. It is an engineering observation about how selection works in decision-making. Those who get to the table do so because they represent an interest for which they are paid or for which they need funding. Lobbyists are paid by companies to represent interests. Policy advisers work for institutions that pay their salaries from established budgets. Ministers are elected by parties that run campaigns with money from organised interests. Everyone at the table has a chimney that must keep smoking.

Nature has no lobbyist. It has no representative, no budget for a smart suit, no place in a working group. Its solutions — which are often cheaper and more effective than what is on the table — never reach decision-making, because they are not invited. Not through ill will. Through selection. The system has no mechanism for admitting input that is not put forward by a paying party.

The second filter — short-sightedness

The second selection mechanism is just as structural, and perhaps even more fundamental. Ministers do not look beyond their short-term horizon, and that is not a character flaw. It is what the electoral and bureaucratic system rewards. A minister's term of office is four years, sometimes less. Election campaigns run for months. Budget cycles are annual. Policy advisers work with scenarios of two to three years. None of this rhythm is aligned with natural timescales.

A tree takes twenty years to reach maturity. A biomass cycle is generational. A chemical change in the soil has effects over a hundred years. A patent that reaches technological maturity often takes fifteen years from the first idea to industrial scale. Anyone who decides now on the basis of what will matter in fifty years loses next year's election. Anyone who frees up capital now for research whose returns will appear only in 2050 will not get a second term of office. Those who do deliver results in four years win — even if those results are a short-term solution that makes the problem worse in the medium term.

The combination is paralysing. Power selects input through two filters, both of which run counter to the laws of nature: representation through paid interests, and a four-year horizon. Nature fails both filters. It has no lobbyist and thinks in centuries. That is why it loses every time, on almost every issue.

Nature has no lobbyist. And the minister cannot see beyond the short-term horizon. That is no coincidence — it is selection.

What follows from this

This explains why the world systematically chooses the wrong solutions to problems nature has already solved. It is not ignorance. It is not a moral failing. It is the result of a selection process that filters input in ways that exclude the laws of nature. A system that admits only solutions put forward by paying parties gets solutions that benefit paying parties. A system that rewards only solutions delivering results within four years gets solutions that deliver results within four years — even when the real problem is a century-long one.

A natural-economy institute must therefore operate outside both filters. Not to take over power, but to bring to the table what would otherwise be missing. Anyone who runs such an institute must be free from dependence on subsidies and free from electoral pressure. Independent inventors and entrepreneurs are one of the few places where this is structurally possible. Other places exist too — religious traditions that think in centuries, scientific institutes with a permanent basis for existence, some family businesses with a generational perspective — but these are becoming rarer in today's social selection.

IV. Four lessons from nature

Those who understand nature as an engineer learn four principles that revise every economics textbook.

1. Form serves function, and function is on the surface

A bird's feather is not a thin plate. It is a microstructure of thousands of barbs and barbules with varying stiffness. Shark skin is not smooth — it has riblets that reduce turbulence. The lotus flower is not water-resistant because of wax, but because of micropyramids that make water bead up on itself. In every example, the function lies not in the bulk, but on the surface. Those who understand the surface master the function.

2. Quality emerges through iteration and selection, not planning

No bird designed its feather at a drawing board. No leaf worked out the rules of photosynthesis. What works remains. What does not work disappears. The quality tools I have used throughout my life — fishbone diagram, control chart, Pareto chart, histogram, stratification, scatter diagram, check sheet — are an industrial implementation of precisely the same principle. Test, measure, keep what works, eliminate what does not. No ideology, no theory. Only iteration and selection. A factory with quality circles works like an ecosystem.

3. Structures show fatigue before they fail

Every tree shows cracks in its bark before it falls. Every tyre wears down in identifiable places before it bursts. Every steel structure changes its vibration frequency before it fails. Those who measure see early. Those who do not measure only see when it is too late. This principle is elementary in materials science—and it is exactly what is missing from public administration. Political institutions show signs of fatigue long before they collapse, but there is no strain gauge to measure this. The Open Vizier series introduces that strain gauge. It is the same method, applied to different material.

4. Nature knows no waste

What one organism expels is food for another. Leaf litter feeds fungi. Dead wood is a home for insects. CO2 from humans and animals feeds plants. That is not rhetoric about circularity. It is literally how the system works. The modern economy produces waste because it designs processes linearly: raw material, product, waste. Nature designs cyclically. An industrial process that produces no waste is not a utopia—it is a return to the standard that has been operating on this planet for billions of years.

V. What this means for the economy

The natural economy is not opposed to markets and ownership. It is a revision of what is measured as value. Four shifts follow directly.

Value is measured in cycles, not transactions

A tyre that lasts 25 years is not just 2.5 times as valuable as a tyre that lasts 10 years. It also produces 2.5 times less waste, 2.5 times less rubber smuggling, 2.5 times less particulate matter, and an unknown but significant difference in social costs. Anyone who counts only the purchase price misses most of the value. The natural economy calculates the full cycle—from raw material to disposal, including externalities.

Patent value comes from observation, not creation

Patent law treats inventions as human creations. But the truly valuable inventions are observations of what already exists in nature, translated into industrial form. A wing inspired by a bird's feather is not a human invention in the philosophical sense—it is an industrial translation of a design that evolution has already made. Society should revise how it values patents for such inventions: not as a reward for creation, but as a reward for accurate observation and successful translation.

Research has a different return from capital

An investment in observing nature does not yield a four per cent return each year. It yields no return for ten years, and then suddenly produces a breakthrough that reshapes entire industries. No venture-capital model can finance that profile. Yet this is exactly how all the great technological leaps come about. The natural economy calls for forms of financing that can patiently wait for breakthroughs that open up new possibilities—not quarterly results. This is precisely where ministers' short-sightedness falls short. A minister cannot wait patiently. An independent inventor can.

Scale comes from transferability, not mass

Classical economics sees scale in production volumes. Nature sees scale in the repeatability of patterns. An insight from nature that works in one industry works in seven others. Biomimetic surface patterns are not specific to aircraft or ships; they work on wind turbines, buildings, vehicles and sensors. A centre that develops such patterns does not produce a single product. It produces transferable principles.

Malta: space for research requires more than a suitable location

Malta seemed a suitable place for an institute for the natural economy. The sea is close by, the climate offers opportunities for materials research, and the scale of the island makes the distance between research and application manageable. Those physical advantages remain. But my initial enthusiasm has diminished considerably because of my experiences with the way things are done there.

A small society is not automatically an accessible society. Even on a small island, bureaucracy can be considerable. In my experience, you can become entangled in procedures, conditions and expectations that increasingly restrict your room to manoeuvre. The proximity of administration and implementation, which initially seemed an advantage, then does not offer the freedom you had expected.

What is particularly problematic for inventing is being held to what you said earlier. What was a thought, a research direction or a preliminary possibility in an initial conversation can later be treated as if it were a definitive commitment. But research begins precisely where the outcome is not yet certain. A hypothesis is not a delivery promise, and an initial design is not a finished product.

Anyone following nature must be able to change direction. An observation can overturn a starting point. An experiment can show that a material behaves differently from what was expected. An application that seemed promising can prove less suitable, while an unexpected application may open up possibilities. That is not a deviation from inventing. That is inventing itself.

When an administrative environment cannot tolerate that movement, a conflict arises with the work it claims it wants to attract. The researcher is then not encouraged to look more carefully, but to stick to an earlier account. Openness becomes a risk: the more you share about possibilities you are exploring, the more your statements can later be held against you. In this way, a system that demands certainty can end up blocking the learning from which useful certainty must arise.

That is why I now judge Malta differently. The sea, climate and human scale are suitable starting points, but they are not enough. An institute for the natural economy also needs an environment that understands the difference between an idea, an experiment, demonstrated functionality and a concrete commitment. It must be possible to adjust course in response to results, without every change of direction being interpreted as a shortcoming.

Malta is therefore no longer the obvious choice of location. The island remains a possibility, but only if its way of working leaves sufficient room for independent research and the development of understanding as work progresses. The location must serve the research, not lock the research into the expectations of the location.

Nature does not abide by what we have said about it in advance. Anyone who truly wants to learn from it must retain the freedom to test their earlier statements against new observations and revise them if necessary.

VII. Conclusion

This manifesto is not a scientific treatise. It is a statement of work. It describes the foundation beneath all the work I have done in my life: twelve patents, two policy models, a political-philosophical series, and the invention of industrial translations of principles from nature for aviation, shipping, mobility, energy, construction and soil chemistry.

The common thread has always been the same, even when I did not yet name it as such: looking at what nature does, understanding why it works, and translating that into what people need. Without idealisation, because not all nature is beautiful. Without reduction, because nature is not only measurable. Without ideology, because nature does not follow a political party.

The diagnosis set out in this manifesto—that nature is not at the table, and that ministers cannot see beyond the end of their noses—does not lead to pessimism. It brings clarity about where the work must take place. Outside the two filters. Through individuals and institutions free from dependence on subsidies and free from electoral pressure. Through those who can look as far as nature can.

Economics will not adopt this quickly. It has invested too much in its existing frameworks for calculation. But reality, fortunately, does not depend on what science writes about it. When carbon capture at €25 per tonne works, it works. When a wing uses 16.5 per cent less kerosene, it does so. When a tyre lasts 25 years, it lasts 25 years. The natural economy is not validated by articles in economics journals. It is validated by what industrial installations demonstrate every day.

And something similar applies politically. When a citizens' assembly finds a solution in six months that a prime minister spends a year searching for—as I wrote earlier about the Netherlands—that is no coincidence. That is what happens when people whose horizons are not limited by election cycles are free to think about what really works. Nature was not at the table. But citizens who had freed themselves from the two filters were. And they could see what ministers could not.

This manifesto is therefore not intended to persuade. It is intended to put things on record. Anyone who reads it years or decades from now will be able to test whether the work has done in practice what the manifesto claims. The test is not rhetorical. The test is empirical.

The work continues, whether this document is read or not. Nature does not wait for recognition. It remains observable. The person who looks remains a learner. The person who measures remains an engineer. The person who does not look is left behind.
