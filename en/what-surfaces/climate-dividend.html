The climate dividend

How excess mortality in the 75+ group yields exactly enough to structurally address the climate problem

Jacobus van Merksteijn

  • Author — Jacobus van Merksteijn
  • Date — 23 July 2026 · Palma, Mallorca
  • Section — What surfaces · Climate-demographic analysis
  • Base — EuroMOMO excess mortality 2022–2026 · Dutch Wlz report · Nature Medicine heat mortality · EU Ageing Report 2024
  • Sources — Santé publique France, EuroMOMO, Nature Medicine, Imperial College Grantham, Eurostat SILC, OECD Health at a Glance

It adds up. The heatwaves of 2022 to 2026 killed approximately 263,000 people in Europe, of whom 190,000 in the 75+ group. What governments and the surviving working population no longer have to pay in pensions, long-term care and pharmacy for this group amounts to €60 to €80 billion per year in the EU. Exactly enough to structurally address the climate problem that caused the excess mortality. A cycle that funds itself.

What the heatwaves cost in human lives

Between 2022 and 2026, climate-related heatwaves in the EU-27 caused approximately 263,000 excess deaths cumulatively. Two thirds — 190,000 — belonged to the 75+ group. Southern Europe bears disproportionately: Italy 68,400 deaths, Spain 47,200, France 38,600, Greece 18,300, Portugal 12,800.

The collective burden the deceased represented

The public burden per 75+ person per year ranges across Europe from €21,180 in Greece to €66,786 in the Netherlands. Differences reflect pension level, long-term care system and curative care costs. EU average: €32,380 per 75+ person per year.

Applied to the 190,000 deceased 75-plus, corrected for harvesting (40% would have died within 6 months anyway) and with an average remaining life expectancy of 10 years, the net deferred public expenditure over 2022–2026 amounts to €33 billion. Structurally per year from 2030: €12 to €14 billion in avoided collective expenditure.

“Each deceased 75-plus saves the Dutch state on average €200,000 in deferred expenditure. Lower for Southern Europe, higher for Germany.”

Freed care labour — the largest item

Beyond directly avoided expenditure, labour capacity is freed up. Informal caregivers return to regular productivity, care personnel can be redistributed, GP time becomes available. For the EU-27:

The €60 to €80 billion is what governments no longer have to spend and thus becomes freely allocable. The rest sits in the private sphere: higher wages, more productivity, higher tax revenues through regular channels.

What can this amount do?

The core question: what happens when the €60 to €80 billion per year is invested directly in climate? The math is positive. Every euro of climate dividend directed to structural climate investment returns multiple-fold in avoided damage, CO₂ removal and long-term stability.

Reformulated: the amount that governments and the surviving working population no longer have to pay in pensions, long-term care and pharmacy for the deceased 75-plus is exactly the order needed to structurally address the climate problem — which caused the excess mortality. A cycle that funds itself.

What official models leave out

The EU Ageing Report 2024 projects pension and care burdens to 2050 without a climate mortality correction. Official models are thus systematically too pessimistic on long-term liabilities. When the climate correction is included, the projection of pension burden as share of GDP falls for nearly every EU country by 0.4 to 0.6 percentage points to 2050.

Actuaries at European pension funds, reinsurers and health insurers have already been embedding these corrections implicitly in their mortality tables since 2023. Public debate lags — leaving policymakers to react to forecasts that ignore the correction.

The core message

Excess mortality is not an accounting curiosity. It is a structural resource flow large enough to address the underlying climate problem without new taxes, without cutting other budgets, and without external donors. If there is one item on the European budget that — beyond any moralism — arithmetically argues for large-scale climate investment, it is this one.
