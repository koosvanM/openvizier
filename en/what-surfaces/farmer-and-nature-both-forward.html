Fewer cows means less income — unless the farmer grows three plants

Why the FD is asking the wrong question: the farmer in the buffer zone does not have to choose between his cows and nature.

By Jacobus van Merksteijn · Palma, Mallorca · 9 July 2026

  • Date — 9 July 2026, Palma, Mallorca
  • Section — Research & analysis · Nitrogen Edition
  • News source — FD, 9 July 2026, "What does a manure norm do for the farmer? 'Fewer cows means less income'" (Nika Buijs & Bauke Schram)
  • Related — Let the politicians first go to the nature school, 5 July 2026

The question the FD asks

Jongeneel summarizes the problem dryly: "The Netherlands is a relatively small player in the total EU market. Our share is 9%. Less supply from our side does not cause the price to rise across the entire Union. It really is fewer cows, less income."

That is an honest analysis and immediately a devastating one. The cabinet has no answer to the simplest farmer's question there is — how do you compensate a forced income drop for a family business that has often worked the same land for three generations? The €215 million is a paltry tip for a farming population sitting with 130.000 cows too many and 50.000 hectare of land too few. Spread across 3.500 businesses, that is slightly more than €60.000 per business to adapt for nine years. For the purchase of one hectare of agricultural land in Noord-Brabant, where the price according to the FD is €120.400 and in the Peel moves towards €206.400, that is insufficient for half a hectare. And then we haven't even mentioned the zone where "extra rules" apply — the buffer zone up to 250 to 1.000 meters around the Natura 2000-areas, where the norm becomes stricter than those 2,6 cows.

The farmer thus has roughly three options, according to the FD: buying extra land, getting rid of cows, or entering into a partnership agreement with arable farmers to get rid of his manure. All three options amount to shrinkage or dependency. Not a single option for growth.

We propose a fourth option. And it is not theoretical.

What the FD map does not look at

The blue-green color on the FD map — 3.500 farming businesses above the 2,6-norm — overlaps almost one-on-one with the sandy soils and peat meadows at the edge of the most important Natura 2000-areas. Zuid-Nederland into the Peel. The Achterhoek. The Drents-Friese Wold. The Gelderse Vallei. These are precisely the plots that, according to the habitat directive, are called "vulnerable", and at the same time the plots that offer the most favorable microclimate conditions for the cold-tolerant, bred Northwest European variant of Giant Juncao — emphatically not the tropical Amazon-form that would not survive the winter here, but the cold variant selected on Malta and Mallorca over the past three years by Carbon-Alert Ltd, supplemented with white clover for nitrogen fixation and stinging nettle for protein density.

The farmer who today sits around Eindhoven with 3,1 cows per hectare — the average the FD mentions for that region — thus has two scenarios before him. The first is: getting rid of a quarter of his livestock to stay below 2,6. Arithmetically 250.000 to 500.000 euro loss in turnover, compensated with a fraction of that from the €215 million cabinet pot, while his land still receives nutrient surplus from the remaining cows. The second scenario is: keeping the same cows, and growing the three plants on fifteen to thirty percent of his hectare, taking a mobile Tier 1 unit onto the plot to make hydrolysaat from it, and feeding that hydrolysaat back into his own stable as a full protein ration. The cow gets more protein from its own land than from soy from Rotterdam. He emits thirty percent less nitrogen and ten to fifteen percent less methane. The manure disposal, which according to the FD now costs the farmer €30 per m3 — tens of thousands of euros per year for an average dairy farm — is halved.

This is not a scenario that starts on the drawing board tomorrow. It has been documented since June on openvizier.org under the name Carbon-Alert NL. The biological insights come from Wageningen, from the Louis Bolk Institute, from Grassa, from Dairy Campus. The machine technology comes from the mobile cold-pressure-explosion unit that Carbon-Alert has already been testing on its own plots for two years. What is missing is not the knowledge, and not the plant, and not the machine — what is missing is a single minister with boots who steps into the pasture.

The yield per hectare — in figures

Let us calculate it as Jongeneel calculates it. One hectare of Dutch dairy farmland in the buffer zone currently yields the farmer roughly the following:

  • Milk from 2,6 cows per hectare: approximately 22.000 kilos of milk per year, which at a standard milk price of €0,45 accounts for €9.900 gross per hectare
  • Costs of concentrate feed (mostly soybean meal from Zuid-Amerika): approximately €1.800 per hectare per year
  • Costs of manure disposal in case of surplus: €30 per cubic metre × seven to ten cubic metres per cow per year × 2,6 cows = approximately €700 per hectare
  • Net margin on that hectare, after concentrate feed and manure disposal but before labour and other costs: somewhere between €7.000 and €7.500

Now we place one hectare of Carbon-Alert NL cultivation on exactly the same ground right next to it, with the same soil, the same climate zone and the same farmer who manages it:

  • Giant Juncao dry matter yield in North-Western European conditions (cold-tolerant variant, no tropical figures): thirty to fifty tonnes per hectare per year, with a crude protein content of ten to thirteen percent — converted to three to six tonnes of crude protein per hectare per year
  • White clover nitrogen fixation: ten to twenty kilograms of new nitrogen from the atmosphere per hectare per year — synthetic fertiliser requirement zero
  • Stinging nettle admixture: crude protein content up to twenty-five percent on dry matter, and a mineral profile (iron, vitamin K, silicon, calcium) that no imported feed matches
  • Hydrolysaat yield after cold pressure explosion: rumen-stable protein concentrate in which dry matter digestibility increases by twenty-four to forty-seven percent compared to unprocessed grass products
  • Substitution value: each tonne of hydrolysaat replaces roughly six hundred to eight hundred kilos of imported soybean meal, which the farmer now purchases for €450 to €500 per tonne

This means — and this is the calculation that appears in no coalition memorandum — that the farmer saves between €4.500 and €6.000 in imported protein inputs on one hectare of Carbon-Alert cultivation, while continuing to fill his own stable with his own cows. And that is before the second and the third revenue streams originating from the same plant are counted: the BiCRS carbon credit attached to the root mass in the soil, and the digestate residue that returns to the soil as fertiliser.

The farmer who today is racking his brains over the question of whether to cull a quarter of his herd can, with fifteen percent of his acreage under Carbon-Alert cultivation, halve his concentrate feed costs and reduce his methane emissions by ten percent. With thirty percent of his acreage, he becomes net self-sufficient in protein. And at that point, the question of which norm the cabinet imposes is no longer existential, but economic — because the cow emits less, and the farmer imports less.

What nature sees in the meantime

The FD article remains silent on the question of what nature thinks of all this. That is understandable — the FD is a business medium and the farmer pays for the subscriptions. But the question cannot be ignored. What happens to the two hundred and fifty thousand hectare of Dutch agricultural land adjacent to Natura 2000 areas if fifteen to thirty percent of it comes under Carbon-Alert cultivation?

Firstly: ammonia emissions from the stable fall by thirty percent per cow, because the protein supply in the ration is more precisely matched to the requirements. Less ammonia in the air above the plot means less ammonia deposition in the adjacent Natura 2000 area. Without a single cow less.

Secondly: the synthetic fertiliser requirement on the remaining seventy to eighty-five percent of the plot drops sharply, because the clover growing on the Carbon-Alert plot exchanges root mycorrhizae with the adjacent pasture along the plot edges and introduces between ten and twenty kilograms of atmospheric nitrogen per hectare per year into the soil — free, organic, without a single molecule of Russian natural gas processed into synthetic fertiliser via Haber-Bosch. The farmer saves between €200 and €400 per hectare per year on synthetic fertiliser, and the surface water flowing to the adjacent nature reserve is measurably cleaner.

Thirdly — and this is the biologically most exciting part — the plot itself acquires a structure much closer to natural transitional vegetation than a homogeneous perennial ryegrass plot. The Juncao forms a tall grass structure that offers cover to small songbirds. The stinging nettle is the host for at least forty species of native butterfly caterpillars — including the small tortoiseshell, the peacock butterfly, and the comma butterfly. The clover attracts bumblebees, honeybees, and hoverflies. What the farmer now sees as "production acreage" simultaneously becomes an insect corridor between his plot and the protected area, without him giving up a single square meter.

The edge does not become a buffer zone in which less and less is allowed — it becomes a production zone in which more and more biological function arises. That is the opposite.

What the €215 million would do elsewhere

According to the FD, the cabinet is allocating €215 million to cushion the effects of the manure norm. For buyout, relocation, rezoning — the usual categories. Divided among 3,500 companies, that is €61,500 per company, one-off, to adapt for nine years.

That same €215 million would finance the purchase of between thirty and fifty mobile Tier 1 units — one per Dutch border region — plus the seed rollout for approximately eight thousand hectare Carbon-Alert pilot. In exchange for that rollout, every pilot farmer receives no buyout compensation, but a production improvement. His concentrate feed costs fall. His manure disposal costs fall. His methane emissions fall. His nitrogen emissions fall. His cow stable remains standing. His son or daughter takes over. His nearby Natura 2000 area receives cleaner water, less ammonia, and more insects.

This is an investment that becomes economically positive for the farmer after three to four years — not as a subsidy, but as recouped productivity. Comparable mobile units in the Braziliaanse sugar cane industry have been running for ten years and pay for themselves within 2.5 to 3.5 years based on substitution effects alone. Under Dutch conditions, with a higher starting level of fertilisation costs and manure disposal costs, the payback period is expected to be shorter.

Two scenarios, then, for the same €215 million: **buying out what no longer works, or investing in what makes both the cow and nature better off**. The FD suggests the first. The farmer who calculates honestly chooses the second.

And that is the difference the cabinet ignores and the FD fails to portray.

What "the farmer and nature both moving forward" actually means

Over the past ten years, the discussion in the Netherlands has been systematically polarised into a choice between two groups: the farmer who wants to keep his business, and nature conservationists who want to maintain the habitat norm. Every coalition agreement, every Rabobank analysis, every farmers' protest on the Malieveld is about who wins and who loses.

The architecture described by Carbon-Alert NL is not a compromise between the two. It is a third option that wins on both sides. Concretely:

The farmer wins because he keeps his cows, halves his concentrate feed costs, drastically lowers his manure disposal costs, reduces his methane emissions (with all the future CO₂ credit implications that entails), and builds a second income stream from hydrolysate on the same land. He is no longer dependent on the Brazilian soy harvest and no longer fears the EUDR audit. His son or daughter takes over the farm with the certainty that it is more productive than their father's farm.

The nature wins because ammonia deposition in the adjacent Natura 2000 areas drops by thirty percent, because surface water becomes cleaner due to the reduction in synthetic fertiliser, because biodiversity on the fifteen percent Carbon-Alert plots increases (butterfly caterpillars, wild bees, small songbirds), and because the carbon column in the soil permanently sequesters CO₂ via a natural BiCRS mechanism through the deep Juncao roots. The edge of the nature reserve becomes a corridor instead of a buffer zone.

The taxpayer wins because the €215 million no longer needs to be spent on buying out something that works, but on investing in something that earns. Because the €20 billion buffer zone budget that, according to our previous article Let the politicians go to nature school first, is on the table, can be deployed elsewhere — for hydrogen storage, for Dutch protein sovereignty, or simply for repaying national debt. Because the Dutch dairy chain becomes strategically independent of South American soy and thus less sensitive to external shocks.

The Netherlands as a whole wins because the nitrogen, methane, soy, and farmer income files are solved simultaneously by a single architecture that is proven on Dutch soil and requires no foreign suppliers whatsoever. The Netherlands keeps its cow, loses its dependency, and restores its nature — in that order.

The question politics must now ask

The FD poses the question as the coalition has formulated it: how do you compensate the farmer for the loss imposed by the manure norm? We pose the question differently: why do we impose a loss when a growth scenario exists that solves the problem better?

The €215 million to be presented to the Dutch parliament on Friday is not the decisive decision. The decisive decision is whether the cabinet is willing — for one percent of that same €215 million — to finance a real pilot at fifteen Dutch dairy farms in the hardest-hit buffer zones around Eindhoven, the Peel and the Drents-Friese Wold. Fifteen farms, each with six hectares of Carbon-Alert cultivation, a shared mobile Tier 1 unit, a measurement programme at Dairy Campus and Louis Bolk. Total budget: two to three million euros. Payback period for the farmer: three to four years. Political risk for the minister: negligible. Political risk if not implemented: the next Malieveld.

The choice is not difficult. The only question is whether anyone in The Hague dares to ask it.

The edge that is no longer a buffer

For thirty years, the edges of the Dutch Natura 2000 areas have been treated as a problem. As places where farmers had to make sacrifices so that nature would not be further compromised. As buffer zones that became increasingly strict. As postage stamps around which even smaller postage stamps of "increased regulations" were drawn.

What the last seven years of biological field research — at Wageningen, at Louis Bolk, at Grassa, at Dairy Campus, and between 2023 and 2026 on Malta and Mallorca — has yielded is a radically different way of looking at that same buffer zone. It is not a buffer. It is a production zone where the farmer earns more than on the rest of his plot, where nature wins more than in any other scenario, and where three plants — Giant Juncao, white clover, stinging nettle — together achieve what a single 1992 habitats directive can no longer deliver.

The farmer around Eindhoven today, sitting with 3.1 cows per hectare and feeling the 2.6 norm breathing down his neck, has a way out that the FD does not mention, that Jongeneel does not mention, that LTO does not mention and that the cabinet does not mention. That way out does not start with fewer cows. It starts with three plants.

The cow waits. The white clover waits. The stinging nettle waits. The manure norm is coming. The question is which of the four politics will prioritise.
