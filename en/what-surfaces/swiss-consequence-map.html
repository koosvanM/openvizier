Consequence Map · Switzerland · June 2026

The Swiss Consequence Map

Fifteen scenarios, ten Swiss federal mechanisms. What your share of Switzerland in 2030 yields under cumulative Bundesrat policy — when AHV reform, Bilaterale III, the CO₂ Act, individual taxation, Pillar Two and the Kostenbremse compound at third order.

By Jacobus van Merksteijn · Malta · June 2026

The Brussels Consequence Map asked one question of twenty European scenarios: what does cumulative EU policy cost you by 2030? The Swiss version asks the same of fifteen Swiss scenarios, but the mechanisms are different. Switzerland has its own policy stack — and on 2 March 2026 the Bundesrat signed Bilaterale III, the most consequential treaty Bern has ratified since 1999.

This map plots fifteen lives and businesses against ten federal mechanisms in force or being phased in during 2026. The values are third-order effects — meaning each policy is read together with the others, not in isolation. Direct cost, behavioural response, secondary equilibrium. A number you can read.

What the map shows

Read horizontally: each row is one Swiss scenario, from Hans the Zurich retiree to a multinational headquartered in Zug. Read vertically: each column is one federal mechanism, from AHV/BVG reform through Bilaterale III to the SNB's interest-rate policy. The rightmost column shows an alternative reference — what the same scenario would yield under a different federal course.

The ten mechanisms

Social + institutions

  • AHV/BVG reform — the 2024 AHV revision and the 2025 BVG update, raising contribution rates and adjusting conversion rates for occupational pensions.
  • Bilaterale III (EU pact) — the bilateral III package signed in Brussels on 2 March 2026, restoring full market access and dynamic alignment in seven core areas.

Climate + fiscal

  • CO₂ Act 2025-2030 — the revised federal CO₂ act, raising the CHF/ton levy on fossil fuels and introducing border adjustments.
  • Individual taxation — the 2026 referendum decision to abolish joint spousal taxation at federal, cantonal and municipal level.
  • Pillar Two 15% minimum — Switzerland's domestic top-up tax for large multinationals, implemented in 2024 in line with OECD agreement.

Housing + health

  • Kostenbremse (health insurance) — the federal cost-brake initiative limiting premium growth, with structural consequences for hospitals and supplementary insurers.
  • Tenancy revision — the federal tenancy-law revision tightening rent controls in shortage zones.

Climate + monetary

  • Net Zero 2050 — the federal climate-protection act ratified by referendum in 2023, with binding sectoral targets.
  • SNB rate policy — the Swiss National Bank's stance on negative rates, currency strength and capital flows.

Reference

  • Bundesrat reference — what the same scenarios would yield under an alternative federal course (deeper labour-market integration, stronger SME relief, faster Bilaterale III implementation).

How to read the numbers

The numbers are not forecasts. They are derived from federal impact reports, AHV/BVG actuarial files, OECD Pillar Two implementation tracking, and the Bundesrat's Bilaterale III dispatch (Botschaft, February 2026). They are presented as if every mechanism reaches its 2030 stated end-state and compounds with the others. In practice, individual results will diverge. The map is a structure, not a prediction.

What the map does show clearly is which scenarios are net winners and which are net losers under cumulative Swiss federal policy as it stands in mid-2026 — and how much room the Bundesrat reference course (a deeper labour-market integration with the EU, stronger SME relief, faster Bilaterale III implementation) would create for almost every scenario.

The Brussels Consequence Map showed twenty European scenarios losing more than they could absorb. The Swiss version shows a smaller country with a different policy stack — but one where the same compounding logic applies. The Bilaterale III column is the only systematic plus across the board. Read the column.

What's next

This Swiss Consequence Map is the first in a series extending beyond the EU. A Singapore Consequence Map follows in the same publication (published simultaneously). The methodology — fifteen-twenty scenarios × ten-twelve mechanisms × third-order compounding — travels well to any jurisdiction where the policy stack is documented and stable enough to model.

The point is not to map every country. The point is to make the cumulative consequences of policy visible, scenario by scenario, before the elections that decide them.
