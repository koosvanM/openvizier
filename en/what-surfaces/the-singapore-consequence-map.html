The Singapore Consequences Map

By Jacobus van Merksteijn · Malta, July 2026

The ten mechanisms

Social + fiscal

  • CPF Life reform — the 2024-2026 adjustments to the Basic Retirement Sum, Full Retirement Sum, and the payout profile of the CPF Life Escalating Plan.
  • GST 9% (2024+) — the GST increase from 7% to 8% in 2023 and to 9% in 2024, with compensation via the Assurance Package.
  • Pillar Two DTT/IIR — Singapore's Domestic Top-up Tax and Income Inclusion Rule for large multinationals, introduced starting in 2025.

Housing + labor

  • BTO + Prime flat policy — the 2024 BTO classification framework with Prime, Plus, and Standard categories, MOP rules, and subsidy clawbacks.
  • Work permit quotas — MOM's tightening of S-Pass, Work Permit, and EP quotas across sectors.
  • ABSD stamp duty — Additional Buyer's Stamp Duty for second/third properties and foreign buyers.
  • COE car quota — Certificate of Entitlement quota system for vehicle ownership.

Climate + monetary

  • Carbon tax S$25→S$80 — the progression of the carbon tax from S$25/ton (2024) to S$45 (2026-27) and S$50-80 (2030).
  • MAS S$NEER band — the nominal effective exchange rate policy of the Monetary Authority of Singapore, currently on a modest appreciation path.

BiCRS/ethanol route

  • BiCRS/ethanol route — what the same scenarios would yield by replacing the carbon tax and LNG import subsidies with BiCRS (biomass injection in the equatorial belt, directly bordering Singapore) plus ethanol as motor fuel. Singapore is energy-import dependent; BiCRS adoption drastically decompresses import cost and carbon tax pressures. Heavy industry, semiconductor MNCs, and logistics hubs benefit disproportionately; households via lower energy and transport costs.

The fifteen scenarios

Citizens (nine)

Mr Tan 67 — retiree, 4-rm HDB (Bedok)

CPF Life monthly payout, fully paid HDB, modest savings. GST 9% and COE are the largest absolute drags.

Priya 38 — nurse (Tampines)

Public hospital, single mother. GST + COE + work permit quotas reinforce each other; reference rate is the biggest plus.

Daniel 34 — software engineer (Bishan)

Above average, condo tenant, partial SRS saver. MAS band a small plus; GST + COE the primary drags.

Wei & Hui 35 — median couple, BTO

Newly collected BTO in Tampines, dual-income. GST + BTO+Prime + COE stack up to a real loss.

Ah Beng 55 — taxi driver (Hougang)

Self-employed, COE-dependent. The COE column alone is decisive.

Sarah 28 — fresh graduate, S-Pass eq.

Recent local graduate competing with sector S-Pass restrictions. Work permit quotas a small plus.

Rajesh 45 — SME owner (Geylang)

F&B SME, family-run. Work permit quotas + carbon tax + GST reinforce; reference rate provides the most relief.

Mei Lin 50 — HDB rental (Toa Payoh)

HDB tenant. GST + BTO+Prime are the biggest losses; without the Assurance Package, this would be more severe.

Kumar 42 — CPF Life saver

Maximum CPF Special + 3a SRS. CPF Life reform a meaningful drag; reference rate the strongest counterweight.

Businesses (six)

Semiconductor MNC (Tuas)

Pillar Two largest factor; work permit quotas significant; reference rate strongest plus.

Wealth management (Marina Bay)

Private banking. Pillar Two and MAS S$NEER band largest factors; reference rate a compensation.

F&B SME (Tiong Bahru)

Small restaurant. GST 9% (limited pass-through) + work permit quotas + BTO labor pressure reinforce each other.

Logistics hub (Changi)

Freight agent, EU + Asia clients. Carbon tax and work permit quotas biggest drags.

Property developer (CBD)

ABSD on bulk-purchase projects + BTO+Prime competition largest factors.

Family office (Orchard)

Single-family office. Pillar Two + MAS band; reference rate a clear plus.

How to read the numbers

These are third-order modeled effects — direct costs, behavioral adjustments, secondary equilibrium — under cumulative policy. They are not predictions. They are synthesized from MOF budget statements 2024-2026, CPF Annual Reports, MAS quarterly reports, HDB BTO Prime/Plus framework documents (2024), MOM Work Permit framework (2025), and revisions to the carbon tax law (2025).

What the map does show is which scenarios are net winners and which are net losers under cumulative Singaporean federal policy as it stands in mid-2026 — and the way out offered by the BiCRS/ethanol route: a consistently positive effect across all fifteen scenarios, from +7% for the fresh grad to +57% for the Logistics hub in Changi.

Singapore's policy stack is the most readable in this series. The stacking, however, is the same as everywhere else: a citizen or business reads each policy in isolation and finds it manageable. Read together — as the map does — the picture changes. And the BiCRS/ethanol route, driven from the equatorial belt literally bordering Singapore, shows there is a way out that works for everyone.

What follows

The Singapore Consequences Map is published in parallel with the Swiss Consequences Map. Both are first iterations of a methodology that travels well: take a documented policy stack, plot fifteen-to-twenty representative scenarios against ten-to-twelve mechanisms, model third-order stacking, and compare with an alternative course.

The goal is not to predict anyone's 2030 outcome. The goal is to place cumulative policy into a single readable framework, before the budgets and elections that decide them.
