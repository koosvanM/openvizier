The Thought in the Vault

Why European innovation must withdraw — in self-defence, to survive

Jacobus van Merksteijn

  • Author — Jacobus van Merksteijn
  • Date — 11 August 2026 · Calvià, Mallorca
  • Section — What surfaces · Philosophy
  • Core — Building outside Europe is not building an escape route, but a vault
  • Theme — Preserving innovation capacity for whoever asks for it later

What a thought needs to live

An innovative thought is not a text and not a patent. It is a living constellation of four things: a carrier who understands it, an environment that permits it, means to test it, and time to let it ripen. If one of the four is missing, it evaporates within a generation. If two are missing, it disappears within years.

Historically Europe supplied all four. Universities kept carriers alive, guilds and workshops supplied the environment, wealthy cities and later the state supplied the means, and the unhurried tempo of European culture supplied the time. From that combination came the scientific revolution, the industrial revolution, and most of the base innovations on which the modern world runs.

Each of those four has been dismantled step by step over the past half-century. Not at once, not by ill will, but through a slow shift in what the system optimises for. The question is no longer whether the thought can still live here. The question is where it can live, and how it gets there without being stolen or punished.

The immune system of the established order

Every established structure develops an immune system against what endangers it. In a healthy civilisation that immune system is selective: it repels real threats and lets useful changes through. In an exhausted civilisation the immune system loses that distinction and starts attacking all change, including the change that would save the civilisation itself.

Europe is in that second stage. Anyone attempting something new that touches existing arrangements does not meet a single opponent but a network of reactions that starts up on its own: bureaucratic requirements to be met before the innovation may begin, tax treatment that assumes the innovator already makes profit before the first return arrives, media frames that place success in suspicious context immediately, political proposals that want to tax the innovator in advance for consequences that have yet to materialise.

This is not a conspiracy. It is a system that has come to value its own reproduction over its own purpose. The immune system defends not the civilisation but its own existence. And precisely in that, it can no longer tell what is harmful from what is necessary.

Treating symptoms as a form of governance

The deeper cause of this immune reaction is that European government no longer has the instruments to address causes. Every apparatus once set up to intervene deeply — industrial policy, education, spatial planning, fiscal design — has over recent decades been cut into competencies distributed across Brussels, national capitals, regions, municipalities, and dozens of autonomous regulators. What remains is treating symptoms: when something goes wrong somewhere, a rule is added, a subsidy set up, a committee installed.

Treating symptoms has one property that both explains and paralyses it: it requires no understanding of the whole. Each symptom can be addressed by the people working on that symptom, without anyone ever asking where the symptom comes from. Whoever does ask that question meets the same immune reaction as the innovator: it is read as an attack on the existing distribution of competencies.

The consequence is that the system can only respond to things that fit its own language. A biobased replacement for a petrochemical chain does not fit. A different governance model does not fit. An education system that puts ripening before reproduction does not fit. These are cause-changes, and the system is structurally unfit to handle them. It pushes them away, not out of ill will, but because they are unreadable to it.

Why government will not help

This is the point that gets made personal, but is not personal. Anyone in the Netherlands trying to set up an innovation that touches existing distributions meets not an opponent but an absence. No one wishes ill. No one is authorised to say yes. Each desk refers to the next, each policy document requires coordination with another policy document, and by the time the desk that could actually decide is reached, the innovation has moved past the point where the decision still matters.

Government experiences the innovator who does not wait as a runaway. From the desk's perspective that is correct: the request was not completed, the form not filled in, the committee not awaited. From the innovation's perspective it is wrong, because the innovation cannot wait. It has its own tempo, which does not coincide with that of the bureaucracy.

Whoever has been through this knows that government genuinely cannot adapt itself. That is not blame. It is a conclusion drawn from dozens of failed attempts at the same innovation dialogue. Government will not help, because helping demands precisely the authority that no one still holds. And because the system has long since learned to weed out anyone who might hold it.

Where the intuition now comes from

Meanwhile, elsewhere, the thought is being carried. China and India are currently producing the people who used to rise in Europe: individuals who have worked their way up under pressure, who have kept the mammalian foundation of their first years intact, who know what a mistake costs and what responsibility means. America holds itself together by attracting precisely those people and giving them room, supplemented with a domestic residue of hardness maintained by its labour market.

Europe barely produces such people any more, and no longer attracts them either. Those who have worked their way up elsewhere rarely choose a continent where success is suspect and protection expensive. Those born here who have kept the intuition intact discover at some point that they are part of a dying breed, and that the institutional environment no longer recognises their kind.

Not many are needed. Innovation has never been a matter of numbers, always of extremes. A handful of carriers is enough to move a technology, a sector, a civilisation forward by a generation. Europe now lacks them in most strategic domains. Those who remain can choose: dissolve into the systematic dismantling, or arrange their work so that it survives.

What a vault is

The vault metaphor is precise. A vault is not a place of flight. It is a place where something valuable is kept because the environment where it belongs is temporarily unsafe for it. What lies in the vault does not belong to the vault keeper. It belongs to whoever asks for it later. The vault keeper is custodian, not owner.

When a European thinker today sets up structures outside Europe — a production site in Mauritania, a remediation firm on Malta, a governance pilot on Sark, a cultivation system in a non-EU jurisdiction — that is not asset relocation and not tax optimisation. It is a vault. What lies in it is not money, but a thought, together with the infrastructure needed to keep that thought alive across decades.

The vault has four properties that distinguish it from flight. It stands in a jurisdiction where the thought can develop without being immediately swallowed by immune reactions. It is bound to a productive activity that delivers real value to the place it stands. It is transparent about what lies in it and why, without the carrier having to defend it against every passer-by. And it is recoverable: what lies in it can later, when the time is ripe, be deployed, shared, or brought back into an environment that then asks for it.

What lies in the vault

Concretely, what lies in it are things that stay alive only if they are kept in operation. Knowledge of large-scale biomass processing evaporates within ten years if no one still does it. A governance model that works at the scale of a community can only be preserved by running it in a real community. An economic measurement method such as a corrected GDP or an index for domestic productive ownership becomes reliable only when applied and tested against real figures. Paper alone is not preservation.

That means the vault is not an archive but a working installation. A cultivation that keeps growing. A remediation project that keeps running. A pilot community that keeps making its own decisions. A publisher that keeps publishing. Each of these keeps alive a specific piece of knowledge that would be lost the moment the activity stops.

The choice of jurisdictions outside the European Union — or, in the case of Sark, outside the EU but within the British constitutional arrangement — is functional. It allows the activity to actually work the way it is meant to, without every action having to pass through a controlling system that does not understand the action.

Why this is not flight

The difference between flight and preservation lies in what happens with the yield. One who flees takes what he can and leaves behind what he cannot take. One who preserves takes what can survive elsewhere, with the assignment to keep it available for the place he came from.

That difference is not visible from the outside. To the desk where an innovator has to report, it looks the same: activity no longer taking place in the country, capital no longer taxed here, initiative no longer deployed here. The desk cannot see the difference. Only time can see it.

The question is therefore not whether the desk understands. The desk will not understand, and that is fine. The question is whether the thought survives, and whether it is available when in two, three or five decades someone asks: what should have been done, and how was it actually built. At that moment there lies in the vault a done answer, not a book. That is the difference.

What the carrier does, and what he no longer tries

The carrier of a thought in this phase does three things at once. He builds and maintains the vault. He documents what lies in it in a form that remains intelligible after he himself is no longer available. And he keeps a connection to the place the thought originally came from, not to be accepted there again, but to remain findable for the few there who recognise the pattern.

What he no longer does is try to change the established structures. Not because he gives up on them, but because he has learned that change from within does not work with structures whose immune system has set itself precisely against change. The energy that used to go there now goes to the vault and to documentation. This is not cynicism. It is an economic choice of time and attention.

What he also does not do is expect his position to be recognised while he is alive. Recognition comes later or not at all, and that makes no difference to the work. The work is the preservation, not the reputation.

The opening in time

Civilisations that die of their own immune system go through a phase of dismantling, followed by a phase of renewed demand. Rome vanished and was partly reconstructed centuries later by people who did not come from Rome. China went through several cycles of collapse and rebuilding, and each time reached back to what had been preserved elsewhere or reinvented there. What seemed lost turned out later to be available, because someone had stored it outside the collapsing structure.

Europe is likely entering a similar phase. Not concluded in thirty years, perhaps longer, with intervals in which it seems to be going better. But the straight line points one way, and the coming decades will show whether anyone preserved in time what will later have to come back. Whoever then has a working installation instead of an archive, a community that has lived a governance model instead of describing one, a production chain that functions instead of being sketched — has something to contribute to the reopening.

Whether the keeper himself experiences that reopening is uncertain. The likely outcome is that the vault outlasts the keeper. That is not tragic, it is the nature of this kind of work. Whoever built cathedrals in the twelfth century knew that the roof would be placed by his grandson. Whoever builds a vault today for a thought must reckon with the possibility that its user is not yet born.

What government, media and public could understand

Nothing is asked of government. It will not help, and that is fine. What would help is that the reflex to cast the keeper as runaway does not switch on automatically the moment someone sets up structures outside Europe. Every runaway-frame destroys a vault. Every time someone building for the long term is publicly cast as opportunist, the chance of preservation shrinks.

Of media the ask is to distinguish between relocating wealth to be better off oneself and relocating work to let it survive. That distinction is not always easy to see, but there are factual indicators: what kind of activity is undertaken in the foreign jurisdiction, what real production or service does it deliver, which community benefits, and what is made available back to the original environment in the form of knowledge, publications, or recoverable structures.

Of the public nothing is asked, except perhaps this: remember that the people you now favour by crowning them as culprits are the same kind of people who, two generations ago, favoured your ancestors by building things that later became common good. There is a pattern, and it repeats itself. Whoever can now see through it need not do so loudly. It is enough to be able, when the time comes and the vault opens, to recognise what comes out.

Closing — the thought in the vault

“The thought in the vault does not belong to the keeper. It has been given into safekeeping to whoever can carry it now, with the assignment to pass it on to whoever can use it later. Whoever builds a vault does so not because he distrusts Europe, but because he distrusts the times. The vault stands open for whoever later asks for it. Until then it remains closed, and the keeper works on.”
