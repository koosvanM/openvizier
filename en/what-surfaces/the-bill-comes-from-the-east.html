The bill comes from the East

How Europe gave away its money, its industry and its raw materials, and why inflation lies at the end of that road

Jacobus van Merksteijn · 25 November 2026

In this article

If you drive through Dubai, Doha or Abu Dhabi, you see towers paid for with oil. If you drive through China’s new industrial cities, you see factories paid for with exports. In both cases, Europe was at the till. This article traces that money: where it went, what was left of it in Europe, and why the bill is now coming back in the form of higher prices.

The figures in this article come from numbered sources. Where I have done the calculations myself, that is stated in the calculation appendix. Where I am expressing an expectation or opinion, that is explicitly stated.

The money that went away

In 1970, 55.9 per cent of all the crude oil exported by OPEC countries went to Western Europe; by 1980, that figure was still 40 per cent [1]. In 1974, oil-importing countries together paid 82 billion dollars (in 1973 dollars) in taxes and royalties to the oil-producing countries. Of that, 33 billion came from the European Community, 14 billion from the United States and 16 billion from Japan [2]. Europe and America thus paid well over half (calculation appendix A). That money laid the foundations of the Gulf states.

Today, Asia buys 84 per cent of Gulf oil and Europe only about 9 per cent [3]. The towers are there. Europe paid for the foundations; their further construction now comes from Asia.

Our savings are leaving too. European households invest approximately 300 billion euros outside the EU each year, mainly in the United States [4]. In 2024, the total outflow of European savings was 437 billion euros [5]. That money funds factories and technology elsewhere, not here.

And then there is China. In 2025, the EU bought goods worth 559.4 billion euros from China and sold goods worth 199.6 billion euros there. The deficit was 359.8 billion euros [6]. When I add up the deficits from 2015 to 2025 inclusive, I get approximately 2,560 billion euros [7] (calculation appendix B). China’s trade surplus with the whole world was 1.19 trillion dollars in 2025 [21]. About a third of that came from Europe (calculation appendix C).

What was left in Europe

The money went away, and production followed. In 1990, China produced 3.0 per cent of all industrial products worldwide. By 2023, that figure was 31.8 per cent, compared with 15.5 per cent for the EU as a whole and 4.6 per cent for Germany [8]. In 2024, China was the largest producer in 14 of the 18 industrial sectors. In 2000, it was the largest in none [9].

We lack energy too. In 2024, the EU obtained 57.3 per cent of its energy from abroad; for oil the figure was 96.6 per cent and for natural gas 85.0 per cent [10]. And for the raw materials used by modern industry, we are almost entirely dependent on China: all heavy rare earth elements and 85 per cent of light rare earth elements, and 98 per cent of permanent magnets [11]. For magnesium, the figure is 92 per cent; for gallium, 77 per cent [24].

We also work less. In 2025, a German worker worked an average of 1,332 hours, a Dutch worker 1,436 hours and an American worker 1,800 hours [23].

The wall in Brussels

Brussels has chosen the answer it knows: a wall. Since 1 July 2026, only 18.3 million tonnes of steel per year may enter the EU without a tariff, 47 per cent less than in 2024. Anything above that is subject to a 50 per cent tariff instead of 25 per cent [13]. Anti-dumping cases are under way for other products.

A wall has two sides. It protects the European factory, but everything that comes over the wall becomes more expensive. And whoever builds a wall gets a response.

The answer from Beijing

China has already given that answer, in a targeted and measured way. Following European tariffs on Chinese electric cars, tariffs on European brandy followed in October 2024 and on pork and dairy products in December 2025 [14]. In April 2025, China introduced export restrictions on seven rare earth metals and on magnets [11]. In May, magnet deliveries fell by around 75 per cent and car manufacturers had to halt production [12]. In July 2026, China banned supplies to 14 European companies, a day after the EU sanctioned 14 Chinese companies [15].

China can afford to do that. The EU took 14.8 per cent of Chinese exports in 2025 [16]. Exports are 21.1 per cent of Chinese GDP [22], so the European market is about 3 per cent of China’s economy (calculation appendix D). Europe can hurt China. China can bring Europe to a standstill.

Why inflation is coming

Put the pieces together. Europe imports its energy, its raw materials and a large share of its products. Brussels makes those imports more expensive with walls. China responds by restricting the supply of precisely the raw materials Europe does not have itself. That delivers a double blow to prices: cheap imports disappear, and domestic production grinds to a halt for lack of components. Whatever is still available to buy becomes more expensive.

That is not reflected in the official forecasts. The European Commission expects inflation in the eurozone to be 3.2 per cent in 2026, 3.1 per cent in 2027 and 2.1 per cent in 2028 [17]. The ECB’s most severe scenario puts it at 5.4 per cent in 2027 [19]. But the Commission assumes unchanged policy, keeps existing tariffs constant and makes no mention of Chinese retaliation or export restrictions [18]. The ECB’s scenarios differ only in the energy price; none of them includes an assumption about trade or tariffs [19]. The ECB even expects cheap Chinese imports to have a downward effect on prices [19].

The author’s expectation

I expect inflation in Europe to rise to somewhere between 8 and 14 per cent. When that will happen has not yet become clear; it depends on how broadly Brussels builds the walls and how hard China hits back. The official forecasts do not see this, because they do not model the scenario. That does not make my expectation proven, but neither does it disprove it.

Why no one puts the brakes on

The author’s view

The audit offices and the government’s number-crunchers are not calculating incorrectly. They calculate according to the rules set out in our laws, and those rules measure spending and GDP, not what we still make ourselves. That is why policy sees no alarm even as production disappears. No one in the system can change those rules. The press follows policy and offers no resistance. A majority wants to keep following this path.

What this means for you

The author’s expectation, except where a source is cited

  • Everything with a Chinese component will become more expensive or harder to obtain: cars, white goods, electronics, solar panels, wind turbines and machinery. The vulnerability of the European car industry to this was already apparent in 2025 [12].
  • Your savings will lose value. At 10 per cent inflation, 10,000 euros will still have the purchasing power of 9,091 euros after one year (calculation appendix E).
  • Jobs in industry will disappear sooner than jobs in government, because factories feel the blow first.
  • Wages lag behind prices. Those who work will see their purchasing power fall.

The end of the road

The author’s view

Europe is at a dead end. The money has gone, production is elsewhere, the raw materials are in China and the energy comes from outside. To remain a player on the world stage, Europe would have to reverse course sharply: make more at home, work more, have fewer rules. But the rules that prevent this are enshrined in law, and those who could change them cannot see the wall.

So the majority keep driving to the end of the street, where the wall stands. The Europe that existed before that wall will not return. Anyone who sees that can only distance themselves.
