Anyone who demands change must also put forward a solution

From pressure through demonstrations to a binding popular decision

Jacobus van Merksteijn · Malta, 8 November 2026

In this article

A proposal for a democracy in which direction is determined not by the greatest disruption, but by the best alternative.

People may express their dissatisfaction. With the government, with taxes, with regulation, with any decision that affects citizens. But, in my view, the right to be dissatisfied does not give anyone the right to make someone else foot the bill.

That is where I draw the line. A demonstration must not create a negative impact for the government, a business owner or a public institution. Not even when that negative impact is presented as necessary for a higher purpose.

The business owner who cannot reach their business was not necessarily responsible for the policy being opposed. The citizen who is stopped on the way did not necessarily cause the problem. And police officers and government employees deployed to a demonstration cannot spend that time on other duties at the same time.

I therefore want a different route to change. Not more scope to exert pressure, but a greater opportunity to have a solution actually implemented.

An accusation is not yet an alternative

Saying that something is going wrong does not, in itself, show how it could be done better.

It is easy to call a government incompetent. It is just as easy to call a tax unjust. But what would replace it? How would it be paid for? Who would implement it? What consequences would it have for people who are not part of one's own support base?

In my view, responsibility must go hand in hand with this.

Anyone seeking change must submit a proposal that goes further than a demand. Not just: this must stop. Also: this is how we will do things differently.

A solution that merely shifts the bill to another group must be made visible as such. A proposal that reduces public spending must specify which tasks will be discontinued. A proposal that calls for new spending must make clear where the money will come from.

Good intentions are no exemption from that obligation.

Five hundred thousand signatures for a solution

My proposal is a direct citizens' initiative with a binding popular vote.

The initiators develop their solution and collect 500,000 valid signatures in support of it. Those signatures are emphatically not an application for permission to demonstrate. They support the solution.

Once the threshold has been reached, the public will have the opportunity to express its views on that proposal.

That changes the question. No longer: how much disruption can a movement cause before the government gives in? Instead: how many people want to support this specific alternative?

A movement's strength in society will then be measured not in blockades, police deployment or activities brought to a standstill. It will be measured in support for a proposal.

My model is inspired by the principle of direct democratic decision-making: citizens should be able not only to elect representatives, but also to determine a course of action themselves.

Clarity first, then a vote

A popular vote must not be a vote on an attractive slogan.

Before signatures are collected, it must be clear which proposal they support. Before the vote, the public must be able to see:

  • What problem the proposal is intended to solve.

  • What measures will be taken.

  • What costs and funding are involved.

  • Who benefits and who is affected.

  • What uncertainties remain.

  • Who will be responsible for implementation.

  • When and how the outcome will be assessed.

Not every effect can be predicted with certainty in advance. But uncertainty must be acknowledged, not hidden behind persuasive language.

It must also be established before the vote whether the proposal is feasible and what changes to the law it requires. The public must know what it is deciding on. Essential limitations must not emerge only after the proposal has been adopted.

An adopted proposal is not non-binding advice

If the public adopts the proposal, the government must implement it.

Not reopen the same political debate. Not negotiate away the core of the proposal. Not postpone implementation indefinitely because the government would have preferred a different course.

The authority to implement the proposal, its funding and the implementation timetable must therefore be part of the decision-making.

The initiators remain responsible for their case and for the expectations they have raised. The government remains responsible for proper implementation. If the initiators themselves take on an implementation role, that must come with clear powers, obligations and oversight.

A popular mandate is not a licence for careless governance. It is an instruction that must be carried out and scrutinised.

The government must deliver too

This system can be credible only if the obligations are reciprocal.

Asking citizens to develop solutions while allowing the government to disregard them afterwards would merely create a new form of powerlessness.

That is why a valid initiative must actually lead to a vote. An adopted proposal must actually be implemented. And progress must be public and open to scrutiny.

The government must not block the process because a proposal is administratively inconvenient. It must make the necessary data accessible and ensure that the steps being taken remain transparent.

Not endless consultation without consequences, but a fixed process from proposal to decision and implementation.

What this means for demonstrating

I am therefore deliberately choosing a different boundary from the usual weighing up of how much disruption is still acceptable.

In my proposed system, demonstrating without a solution is prohibited. Even a good solution does not give anyone the right to cause negative impacts for the government, businesses or public institutions through a demonstration.

Criticism, publication, persuading people and gathering support remain possible. But there is a democratic route to concrete change that does not depend on disrupting others.

That is a far-reaching choice. I therefore present it as a proposal to change our system, not as a description of the current rules.

From opposition to responsibility

A majority can also be wrong. A popular vote does not prove that a proposal is technically perfect or will succeed economically. That is why the results must be measured after implementation, and any changes must follow a clear democratic process.

What this system must establish is who decides and who must then act.

Anyone who has only an accusation can voice it. Anyone with a solution can win support for it. Anyone who wins sufficient support can put it to the public to decide. And anyone who obtains a popular mandate thereby gains not only influence, but also responsibility.

That is the direction I support: what should carry the day is not the ability to bring society to a halt, but the ability to demonstrably improve it.
