EU: GDP, Real Wages and the Government Multiplier

GDP–wage measure after deducting growth in total government expenditure — how much macroeconomic room remains?

By Jacobus van Merksteijn · Behind the Numbers · 14 min read · 28 May 2026

Method

The Three-Step Method: From GDP to Macroeconomic Room

This analysis uses the same AMECO basis for real GDP and real compensation per employee as the previous analysis, supplemented by AMECO OUTG for real total expenditure of general government (deflator GDP, index 2020=100). OUTG measures real total government expenditure under ESA 2010 (DBnomics AMECO OUTG). AMECO is the European Commission's macroeconomic database with annual datasets (European Commission AMECO).

1

GDP–wage measure

Real GDP growth − growth in real compensation per employee

The baseline measure: does the economy grow faster than labour costs? A positive value means there is macroeconomic headroom above wage developments.

2

After government deduction

GDP–wage measure − real growth in total government expenditure

Once the growth of real total government expenditure is also counted as a separate pressure factor, the measure changes substantially. Government claims a share of the macroeconomic room.

3

With multiplier

GDP–wage measure − real government expenditure growth × government expenditure index (2000=1)

This is a stress measure: rising government expenditure later in the period is weighted more heavily than in 2001, because the real cumulative cost base of government has itself grown larger. Not an official national accounts indicator, but an analytical method to make the multiplier factor visible.

The core question of this analysis: After accounting for real wage developments and growth in total government expenditure, is any macroeconomic room left over?

Interpretation

What Do These Scores Mean? A Conceptually Heavier Measure

This version is conceptually more demanding than the earlier GDP–wage analysis. The earlier chart asked: does output grow faster than real compensation per employee? This analysis poses a fundamentally stricter question:

"After real wage developments and growth in total government expenditure, is any macroeconomic room left over?"

What a negative score does not mean

Government expenditure is not inherently problematic. It can finance productive infrastructure, education, defence, healthcare or economic stabilisation — investments that raise productivity over time.

What a negative score does mean

When total real government expenditure rises faster than the economy as a whole, the free macroeconomic room shrinks. The margin between output and pressure factors (wages + government) disappears.

The multiplier as a stress measure

The third step is not an official national accounts indicator but an analytical way to make the cumulative build-up of government expenditure visible. The heavier weighting later in the period reflects the fact that the absolute cost base has itself grown larger in the meantime.

Implication for policy

Standard GDP growth figures mask how much of that growth has already been "consumed" by government expenditure developments. The actual free macroeconomic room is smaller than policymakers typically measure.

Conclusion

Conclusion: Macroeconomic Room Shrinks Faster Than We Measure

The EU data empirically confirm what the NEPK framework (Net External Productive Core) theoretically asserts about the relationship between GDP growth, labour costs and overhead. The connection is direct and corroborating:

EU data validates NEPK theory: The positive GDP–wage margin of EU27 (+0.9 pp) evaporates entirely once real total government expenditure is included as a pressure factor (−0.9 pp with the multiplier). This is precisely the mechanism the NEPK framework describes for the Netherlands: macroeconomic room is absorbed by costs that standard measures do not fully account for.

01

NEPK theory → EU evidence

The NEPK framework holds that the actual productive core is smaller than GDP figures suggest, because overhead (government, compliance, the circulation sector) absorbs an ever-larger share of growth. The EU27 multiplier data shows this pattern simultaneously across all 27 member states.

02

The convergence trap

Countries such as Latvia, Lithuania and Romania — with the lowest multiplier scores — demonstrate that rapid economic convergence goes hand in hand with even faster growth in government expenditure. This is NEPK dynamics on a European scale: growth combined with shrinking free margin.

03

The Netherlands in European perspective

The Netherlands, with a multiplier score of −0.9 pp, sits at the EU27 average. With a 2024 score of −2.7 pp it is in negative territory, though less extreme than Belgium (−5.9 pp) or Germany (−5.3 pp). NEPK erosion is not a specifically Dutch phenomenon — it is an EU-wide pattern.

"Macroeconomic room shrinks faster than we measure — hence the NEPK framework."

The EU multiplier data are not a standalone indicator of crisis. But they validate the core of the NEPK argument: standard GDP growth and wage indicators underestimate the actual costs pressing on the productive core. The NEPK framework makes that hidden pressure explicitly measurable — first for the Netherlands, now confirmed by EU-wide data.
