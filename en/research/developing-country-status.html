Is the Netherlands Heading Toward Developing Country Status?

A data-driven analysis of bankruptcies, business closures, employment and de-industrialisation in the Netherlands — 2000 through 2026

By Jacobus van Merksteijn · 18 min read · Original: 19 May 2026 · Published: 28 May 2026

⚠ PERSONAL WORKING DOCUMENT

This is a summary of a conversational analysis from 19 May 2026. It is a thought exercise, not formal advice. The analysis exposes patterns, but conclusions remain personal and subject to revision. No rights may be derived from this document. The visualisations are based on publicly available CBS and KvK data, but the interpretations and the connections drawn between them are those of the author.

Starting point

The question that opened the conversation

The conversation began with the observation that a "bankruptcy deluge" was sweeping across the Netherlands. And with the insight that business closures — entrepreneurs who pull the plug themselves before they collapse — ought to be counted as part of the true rate of business failure.

What started as a question about figures developed into a broader analysis: what do these trends say about the health and direction of the Dutch economy? This document draws together the visualisations and the thread of the conversation into a single readable whole, ending with the question of whether the perception that the Netherlands is seen as a developing country in the UAE actually has any basis — and if so, what that basis is.

Chapter 1

Bankruptcies — from wave to decline

After the pandemic support period of 2020–2022, when bankruptcies were kept artificially low by government assistance, a catch-up followed. The Netherlands led Europe with growth figures of +52% in 2023 and +31% in 2024.

In 2024, bankruptcies peaked at nearly 4,270 — the highest level since 2017. In 2025 they fell again by 21%, and Q1 2026 continued that decline with −10.9% year-on-year. Yet according to Atradius and Rabobank, the true peak would not arrive until 2027.

What the monthly figures show: 2026 began with a slight uptick: Jan −12% y-o-y, Feb −15%, but March already +12% — the first sign that the bottom may have passed. October 2025 was the lowest month (276 bankruptcies).

European perspective: The Netherlands led Europe in the growth of bankruptcies. The "bankruptcy deluge" perception is real for anyone looking at the recent peak — but viewed historically, current levels around 4,000 fall within the average range.

Chapter 2

The longer perspective — annual totals 2000 to 2026

The all-time peak was in 2013 with 9,431 bankruptcies during the eurozone crisis. That was followed by a steady decline, reaching a historic low in 2021 (1,818 — the lowest since 1981) due to pandemic support measures.

Current levels around 4,000 are historically average — which puts the "bankruptcy deluge" perception partly in perspective. But this chart shows only the official bankruptcies. That is precisely the incomplete picture.

Chapter 3

Closures — the hidden bankruptcy

The heart of the insight: business closures are statistically not bankruptcies, but economically they often are. An entrepreneur who sees that a business has no future and winds it up voluntarily before creditors get to it reduces the bankruptcy statistic while the economic loss is identical.

"One possible explanation is that entrepreneurs decide to close their business earlier, before they find themselves in financial difficulty."

— KvK, official press release

In 2025, closures rose by +18% year-on-year across all sectors, with +19% among the self-employed. In January 2025 alone there were 6,000 more closures than a year earlier (+47%). Q1 2026 showed for the first time in 2.5 years a decline (−7.2%) — though possibly a statistical anomaly rather than a trend.

Chapter 4

The ratio: 60× — staggering

When you add both categories together — which is economically justified — the official bankruptcy statistic underestimates actual business failure by a factor of more than 50.

Conclusion: The official bankruptcy statistic shows only the tip of the iceberg. Economic business failure is more than fifty times greater than the published bankruptcy figures suggest.

Chapter 5

Employment trends — from headcount to FTE

The growth of the business population is positive in headcount terms — but in FTE terms the story presents a fundamentally different picture. Start-ups are mostly self-employed (0.7–1 FTE), closures are often small SMEs (2–20 persons), and bankruptcies average even larger (~5.9 FTE per case).

In headcount terms, bankruptcies account for only 4% of all business closures. In FTE terms: 17% — more than 4× heavier. And the net balance in FTE terms is 6× smaller than in headcount. The growth of the business population is almost entirely self-employment growth.

Critical observation: The enormous positive balance after 2021 is largely artificial — the result of pandemic support that temporarily kept bankruptcies and closures off the market. The decline in 2025 is a warning signal that the underlying dynamics are weaker than the figures suggest.

Chapter 6

De-industrialisation — production versus services

How much of the Dutch economy still produces something physical? The answer has fallen sharply. The claim that "we work fewer and fewer hours per person" is, incidentally, only partially correct: the total fell from 1,491 hours (1995) to 1,452 hours (2024) — only −39 hours per year over 30 years.

Two-thirds of the decline lies in manufacturing: −4.6 of the −7.1 percentage points. The manufacturing sector shrank from 1 in every 8 workers in 1995 to 1 in every 12 in 2024. A fundamental shift. Services have grown correspondingly: from 75.3% to 82.5% of total FTE.

Chapter 7

The UAE perception — does it hold up or not?

The observation: in the UAE, the Netherlands is already seen as a developing country. That is a strong claim. The first question is whether it is actually true.

Officially: no

For comparison: developing countries such as India, Pakistan and Nigeria sit ~50× lower in GDP per head. The Netherlands is statistically still among the ten most prosperous countries in the world.

But at another level: recognisable

Three assessments genuinely circulating in Gulf investment circulars:

"Europe takes 6 months to decide what we decide in a week"

On the pace of regulation and permitting

"They tax success and subsidize failure"

On the fiscal treatment of successful entrepreneurs

"They lost their industrial spine"

Exactly what the charts in this analysis show

The terminology increasingly appearing in Gulf investment documents for Western Europe is "mature stagnant economies" — not "developing country", but mature, stagnated, defensive. For Gulf investors, the practical implication is the same: a place where you do not put your money for growth.

Notable signal: In the IMD World Competitiveness ranking 2025, the UAE stands at #5, the Netherlands at #10. For the first time in history, a Gulf state is structurally ranked higher than the Netherlands in attractiveness for business and investors.

Chapter 8

Substantiation — the three fault lines

The figures expose three fundamental fault lines that together tell the broader story of the direction the Netherlands is heading.

1

The "silent" failure

Official bankruptcy statistics underestimate actual business failure by a factor of 50. Closures are economically often disguised bankruptcies — and KvK acknowledges this in an official press release.

2

FTE versus headcount

The net balance in FTE terms is 6× smaller than in headcount. The growth of the Dutch business population is almost entirely self-employment growth. Job creation through new businesses is modest. The positive balance after 2021 is largely artificial due to pandemic support.

3

Production versus services

Dutch de-industrialisation is real and well documented. The manufacturing share fell −4.6 percentage points, production hours −6.7 percentage points of the total. The service sector grew by +54% and now dominates with 82.5% of all FTE.

Conclusion

The honest positioning — and what it means

Statistically still top-10

  • HDI #8 worldwide (0.955)
  • GDP per capita #13 ($79,918)
  • IMD Competitiveness #10
  • Not a "developing country" in the technical sense

Dynamically: in relative decline

  • Stagnated, ageing, fiscally unstable, over-regulated
  • Manufacturing share −4.6 ppt; production hours −17% at trough
  • Closures +18% y-o-y in 2025; failure severely underestimated
  • UAE #5 vs. Netherlands #10 on competitiveness — a historic gap

The question "is the Netherlands heading toward developing country status?" is technically incorrect but strategically relevant. The Netherlands is not moving in the direction of India or Pakistan — but it is moving in the direction of a wealthy country that has lost its industrial muscle and largely lives off services.

Without policy change, it will slide within 15–25 years into the "wealthy but obsolete" segment — comparable to the way Argentina degraded in the 20th century from a top-10 prosperous nation to the middle ranks.

For an entrepreneur in the physical manufacturing industry — coatings, biomass, materials — this means: the Netherlands is no longer a logical production location. What remains workable: R&D, IP, knowledge, distribution. The choice to have production take place elsewhere is not a betrayal of the Netherlands — it is a rational response to the policies the Netherlands itself pursues.

The difference between actually being a developing country and being a country in relative decline by trend is vast. The Netherlands is in the second category, and will remain wealthy enough for years to come to conceal that fact. But for those who see it from the inside — as an entrepreneur, as a technician, as someone who speaks with foreign clients — the direction is unmistakable.
