The methane deception

Three FD pieces in one week, three inversions of weight, four demands on the press.

Diagnosis · 14 min read

PDF Word

On Wednesday, 20 May 2026, Het Financieele Dagblad ran the headline: United States warns Europe over methane rules. Three days later, on Saturday, 23 May, a second piece appeared under the names of Bette de Koning and Eva Rooijers: Methane rules promise enormous climate gains, but according to gas companies they threaten Dutch gas production. Three days after that, on Tuesday, 26 May, TNO published a quickscan for the Ministry of Climate and Green Growth showing that 78 per cent of Dutch methane emissions come from livestock farming, and only 2.7 per cent from the energy sector.

Three publications in six days. Three scrupulously correct percentages. Three different authors. And together they form a ranking error of textbook quality. What the reader retains at the end of that week is that the farmer is the problem and the gas company is relatively clean. The inverted reality has entered him, and he does not even know it has.

The molecule

Begin with the molecule. Methane is produced, lives ten to twelve years in the atmosphere, and breaks down into carbon dioxide and water through reaction with the hydroxyl radical. That is not an opinion. It is chemistry. The IPCC has established the lifetime at an average of 11.8 years, with an uncertainty margin of one and a half years.

Over those eleven years, methane is approximately 22 to 23 times more potent as a greenhouse gas than CO₂. This figure was used for decades in Dutch and international engineering practice; it comes from IPCC AR2 and AR4. At the moment of emission the instantaneous forcing is higher — more than a hundredfold — but that peak decays rapidly. What remains, mathematically integrated over a hundred years, is a factor of 28 to 30. That is the figure that has appeared in policy documents since AR5 and AR6.

The IPCC itself draws an explicit distinction between two types of methane. Fossil methane: released during oil and gas extraction, transport, and coal mining. Global warming potential over one hundred years: 29.8. Non-fossil methane: released from livestock farming, rice cultivation, and wetlands. Global warming potential over one hundred years: 27.0. The difference of almost three units is not a rounding error. It arises from the fact that fossil methane adds to the atmosphere carbon that has been absent from it for millions of years; biogenic methane recycles carbon that was drawn from that same atmosphere by plants a short time before. The IPCC acknowledges this distinction in its tables. The Dutch press, in these three publications, does not.

The whole matter is visible in a single figure. Methane peaks at emission, halves every eight years, and has largely disappeared after twenty-five. What remains is the CO₂ from oxidation. For fossil methane that CO₂ is a net addition to the carbon cycle. For biogenic methane from a stable herd it is the closing of a short cycle that the plant opened ten years earlier. The plant breathed in CO₂, the animal ate the grass, the animal emitted methane, the methane oxidised back to CO₂, and the next plant breathed in the same carbon again. From a climate perspective, a stable herd is net neutral.

A gas leak is not net neutral. A drilled well extracts carbon from a stratum that the sun of fifty million years ago locked away. That carbon is new to the atmosphere. It remains there, in the form into which it has been converted, for centuries.

The three inversions

Seen this way, the three FD pieces together contain three separate ranking errors, each reinforcing the others.

The first inversion is the time horizon. Whoever uses the 20-year figure for methane — a factor of 81 — paints the substance more dramatically than physics allows for long-term policy. Whoever uses the 100-year figure — a factor of 28 — grasps the matter more realistically. Which do you choose? In the FD pieces, this choice is never made explicit. The figures appear as though they were natural constants. They are not. They are editorial choices dressed up as physics.

The second inversion is biogenic versus fossil. The TNO quickscan presents 78 per cent livestock and 2.7 per cent energy sector without making that distinction. But the lasting climate contribution of fossil methane per tonne is fundamentally different from that of biogenic methane per tonne. The reading public sees "78 per cent" and thinks "farmer is the main culprit." The reading public sees "2.7 per cent" and thinks "gas is a minor matter." The figures are correct; the conclusion stands at right angles to the physics.

The third inversion is scale. The Netherlands' share of global methane emissions is approximately 0.3 per cent. The major sources of fossil methane are in Russia, the United States, Turkmenistan, and Iran. Halving the Dutch livestock herd affects a fraction of a fraction. Making American shale gas infrastructure leak-free affects orders of magnitude more. Yet the FD editorial team writes as though the Netherlands can solve the problem in its own pastures. The Ministry of Climate and Green Growth reports in the same register. Both actors place the first-order problem somewhere other than where the physics puts it.

What an engineer would do here

If I were given this as a structural assignment, I would do two things.

First, I would make the time horizon explicit. For what purpose are you measuring? For meeting the 2030 target, the 20-year factor weighs heaviest — methane is indeed a lever then. For meeting the 2100 target, the 100-year factor applies — methane then recedes through its own decay and CO₂ becomes the dominant factor. Without making that choice explicit, every percentage is a political statement.

After that, I would separate fossil from biogenic in every calculation — not as a statistical footnote, but as the primary column in every table. Whoever omits this separation produces not science but a cultural assertion: he is claiming that farmer and gas company carry the same weight, while the physics says they do not.

Finally, I would name the global context as a first-order factor. What effect does Dutch nitrogen and climate policy have on the worldwide picture? An honest answer changes the entire debate. The Dutch farmer is not innocent. He is also not the primary agent of climate change. He is a third-order factor who, by virtue of his national visibility in the media debate, has been elevated to first order.

Four demands on the press

This cannot continue. A press that discusses warming without distinguishing the first order from the third delivers not journalism but a fog screen behind which political decisions become indefensible. I therefore address myself today directly to the editorial board of Het Financieele Dagblad and, by extension, to every Dutch newsroom that publishes on climate.

From the very next article on greenhouse gases, I expect your editorial team to meet four requirements.

One. State the time horizon of every GWP value used. Twenty years or one hundred years. Not "methane is x times more potent than CO₂," but "methane is x times more potent over twenty years, y times over one hundred years." Without that, what you are asserting is not measurable.

Two. Distinguish between biogenic and fossil methane in every piece in which you discuss both. The IPCC makes this distinction in its own tables. A journalist who does not is presenting a claim that contradicts his own source.

Three. Place Dutch figures in their global context. A national percentage without its global proportion is a profession of faith, not an observation.

Four. Have every greenhouse gas article read by a physicist or environmental chemist before publication — not by a policy analyst. The subject matter is exact, the consequences are societal, and the margin for error is narrow.

None of these four requirements is contested in science. None of the four is applied by your editorial team today. The distance between those two sentences is the real threat to climate policy in this country.
