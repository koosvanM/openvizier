The plant that moves

Nitrogen, drought, climate shift, fragmentation — and the policy that sees only one of the four.

Diagnosis · 12 min read

PDF Word

A similar error lurks in another dossier, where the stakes are still more personal. Since 2019, the nitrogen dossier has dominated rural politics in the Netherlands. A ruling by the Raad van State (Council of State) in May of that year set aside the Programma Aanpak Stikstof (Nitrogen Approach Programme); since then a cabinet has fallen, tens of thousands of farmers have been bought out or threatened with compulsory purchase, and the political landscape has been reshaped by a single party that built its entire raison d'être on this one issue.

The dossier is presented as though it has one principal cause: excessive nitrogen deposition on vulnerable natural habitats. That is correct as one factor. It is misleading as a first-order factor. Because in the heathlands and poor grasslands where this nature lives, at least three other factors are simultaneously at work, each contributing a comparable or greater share of the actual vegetation loss.

The four factors

The first factor — nitrogen deposition from agriculture and traffic — is the one that policy exclusively identifies. I estimate its contribution to vegetation loss in these areas at approximately thirty per cent. That is a substantial contribution. It is not sole dominion.

The second factor is desiccation. Groundwater levels in the Dutch sandy soils have been falling for decades — through drainage, through the impermeable surfaces of urbanisation that discharge rainwater rather than allowing it to infiltrate, through deep drinking-water extraction. When the root zone dries out, the mineralisation of organic matter in the soil accelerates. In that decomposition, nitrogen and phosphate are released — from the soil itself. The system is suffocating under its own chemistry, even without a single ammonia molecule from a cow. STOWA names this explicitly in its deltafact on terrestrial nature: desiccation thus leads to eutrophication as well. I estimate the contribution of this factor at approximately twenty-eight per cent — nearly equal to the first.

The third factor is climate displacement. Species distribution studies across Europe show that plant and animal species are shifting their ranges northwards at an average of seventeen kilometres per decade. The vegetation we wish to preserve in our Natura 2000 sites may simply not be capable of growing here in 2050 — not because of nitrogen, but because the climate has moved the species optimum to Scandinavia. The European Commission acknowledged this on 25 March 2026 in new guidance for Natura 2000: area boundaries and conservation targets must be able to move with the climate. Brussels says so. The Hague is deaf. I estimate this factor at twenty-two per cent.

The fourth factor is historical habitat fragmentation. Roads, canals, urbanisation, intensive land use between nature reserves — everything that prevents species from finding or reaching their habitat. A species disappearing from the Veluwe can no longer migrate to the Drents-Friese Wold as it could in the nineteenth century. This factor contributes an estimated eighteen per cent to vegetation loss — not the largest, but a permanent one.

Together these four factors account for approximately one hundred per cent. In policy, only the first appears. Three first-order factors are treated as footnotes — when they appear at all — while one is inflated to sole cause.

The static model

The problem is conceptual. Dutch nature conservation operates from a static model: here are designated areas, here are species, here are targets, and policy must preserve the species in their assigned areas as they were recorded in 1990 or 2000. That model is sympathetic. It is also fundamentally untenable in a changing climate.

A dynamic nature policy would look different. It would accept that some species will disappear here and emerge elsewhere. It would not direct resources exclusively towards nitrogen reduction on the Veluwe, but also towards the construction of wildlife corridors towards Germany, towards reserve expansion in cooler areas, towards acceptance of changing vegetation where change is unavoidable. It would spend less energy resisting what cannot be resisted, and more on enabling what is inevitably shifting.

That is precisely what Brussels prescribed in March 2026. It was not to be found on the front page of any Dutch newspaper.

What this does to the farmer

Here the order inversion touches the human scale. Thirty-five thousand Dutch farmers have, in the last five years, faced regulation that threatened or ended their businesses, motivated by one of the four factors in this diagram. To the other three factors — desiccation, climate displacement, fragmentation — no comparable policy effort is attached. Agriculture was held almost solely responsible for a vegetation loss to which it contributes, at best, one third.

This is not a plea to abolish nitrogen policy. That would be the opposite order inversion — treating a third-order factor as zero. It is a plea to govern according to distribution. Whoever bears thirty per cent of the cause bears thirty per cent of the burden. Not one hundred. And the remaining seventy per cent calls for instruments we are not developing today, because our gaze is fixed on a single variable.

A judge in May 2019 saw this, in a certain sense. The Council of State then struck down the Nitrogen Approach Programme because it insufficiently controlled nitrogen as a single factor. That was legally correct. It was incomplete in terms of ranking. Since then the debate has remained locked within that one factor, while three others roll on without regulation. The judge worked with the law before him. Policy should have recognised that the law itself was built on a first-order error.
